# Defenseman Coaching: Decisions, Cues, Mistakes and Drills (for VR training of an adult recreational left-shot D, 5'8", one-handed angler)

Source-quality note for the report writer: the strongest primary sources found are the Hockey Canada "Developing Skilled Defencemen" pathway (2018-19, re-issued as the 2020 "Developing Defence Overview"; read in full via text extraction), the Hockey Canada "Skills of Gold" Defensive Tactics checklist (read in full), the Joseph Bertagna excerpt from *The Hockey Coaching Bible* (Human Kinetics), the Wilson and van Vliet *Hockey Goaltending* excerpt (Human Kinetics), two USA Hockey/ADM pages, and named-coach blogs (HockeyShare, Greg Revak, JJ Santagata, Andrew Trimble, Kelvin Cech at The Coaches Site, Jeremy Weiss). Several items are marked "(search snippet; page not read in full)" where only a search-result summary was available (mostly paywalled The Coaches Site pages or drill-print pages); treat those as weaker. ## 1. Gap control

### Takeaway
Coaches define gap as the vertical and lateral space between the D and the attacker, and teach that the D should match the attacker's speed, take an inside-out position (the D's outside shoulder on the attacker's inside shoulder), keep the stick on the ice in the middle and passing lane, and shrink the gap as the play nears the D's own blue line. The best rule of thumb found is about 3 stick lengths at the far blue line, 2 at the red line, and 1 stick length or body contact at the D's own blue line. Coaches disagree on how aggressively to close: "receive the rush" versus "engage early".

### Cited Findings
- **Definition and importance:** Gap is the space between the D and the forward he covers, both vertical (up the ice) and lateral (side to side). Bertagna: "It is very difficult to be a good defenseman if you don't have good gap control." — [Bertagna, *The Hockey Coaching Bible*, "Gap Control" excerpt, Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/gap-control)
- **Where the gap starts:** "The blue line does not determine where gap control begins." The gap is set by where the opposing forwards are. They sit deeper the longer the play stays in the offensive zone. — [Bertagna / Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/gap-control)
- **Match speed and judge "VMG":** "A good gap allows the defenseman to maintain the same speed as the opposing forward." Serpentine skating can look faster than it is, so D "must judge their opponent's VMG [velocity made good, the speed made up the ice] and adjust their speed accordingly." Use C-cuts, not crossovers. If the forward is flying out of the zone, skate forward first to close, then pivot once the gap is set. — [Bertagna / Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/gap-control)
- **Lateral gap / inside-out:** "The wider the forward goes with the puck, the wider the defenseman should be." "The defenseman should always try to keep his outside shoulder in line with the forward's inside shoulder" to block the cut back to the middle. Then pivot to an angle that eventually forces the forward into the boards. — [Bertagna / Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/gap-control)
- **Numeric rule of thumb (3-2-1 stick lengths):** about 3 stick lengths from the puck carrier at the D's offensive (far) blue line, about 2 at the red line, and 1 stick length or body contact at the D's own (defending) blue line. With a proper gap the D is within one stick length at the attacking blue line, where a poke, angle or contact can force linemates offside. "Even a slight change of direction or delay can stop the rush." Small lateral movement "buys time" when the rush changes direction. — [HockeyShare, "Gap Control Basics", May 3, 2010](https://www.hockeyshare.com/blog/gap-control-basics)
- **Close at or outside the blue line:** Tighten the gap once the forward crosses the blue line; stay inside the face-off dots; deny the middle so the forward has to go outside. — [Andrew Trimble, "Some Gap Control Basics", The Hockey Planner, Jul 24, 2023](https://andrewtrimble.substack.com/p/some-gap-control-basics). In the Michigan Tech 1-on-1 drill, D must close the gap and should get outside the blue line before closing. — [Jeremy Weiss, Weiss Tech Hockey, Oct 21, 2010](https://weisstechhockey.com/michigan-tech-1-on-1-drill/)
- **Gap depends on attacker handedness and wing (strong-side vs off-wing):** On a **strong-side** forward (puck on the boards side), leave a *wider* gap: to cut to the middle he must expose the puck, which gives a poke chance. On an **off-wing** forward (puck on his inside), play a *tighter* gap: he can protect the puck with his body while cutting middle. — [Trimble](https://andrewtrimble.substack.com/p/some-gap-control-basics)
- **Too wide vs too narrow:** Too wide lets the forward cut inside into the "shooting zone" (high-percentage middle). Too narrow lets a fast forward skate past and make a hard cut in front of the net. — [Trimble](https://andrewtrimble.substack.com/p/some-gap-control-basics)
- **Hockey Canada's neutral-zone defend cues:** "Protect the ice between dots; pivot to the middle. Don't attack the rush; receive it." "Good gap... 'stay up, don't step up'. Get up ice with the play." "Read the attack: look through the puck carrier... where is the opponent and where is your support." "Angle and steer the attack wide: be on the inside of the rush." "Stick on ice and deny lanes." Where to challenge (red line or blue line) depends on the system; the aim is to "reduce their speed on entry." — [Hockey Canada, *Developing Skilled Defencemen* 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf) (same text in the [2020 Developing Defence Overview](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2020/developing-defence-overview-e.pdf))
- **Skating detail for gap:** "Transition forward to backward: Accepting the rush, 'heels and hips' and pivot to middle of ice." "Backward starts and stops: Limited crossing of the feet and stay square to opponent." — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **"Engage early" school:** A common mistake is taking about three steps back to get "ready", which leaves the D at the top of the circles giving the forward about three to four stick lengths. "CLOSE SPACE, stop giving forwards so much time and space!" When the forward attacks wide, transition to skating forward, since closing space forward is easier than backward. Keep the stick in front through the transition. — [JJ Santagata, "How to Play a 1 v 1 (Defensemen)", LBX Newsletter, May 17, 2023](https://lbxhockey.substack.com/p/how-to-play-a-1-v-1-defensemen)
- **Where to look:** Keep eyes on the forward's logo/chest, since the chest moves less than the feet or stick and the shoulders reveal intent. — [Santagata / LBX](https://lbxhockey.substack.com/p/how-to-play-a-1-v-1-defensemen). For a one-handed poke: "Watch the player while keeping the puck within peripheral vision. Skate facing the puck carrier." — [MAHG (Quebec minor hockey) "Individual tactics" (search snippet; page not read in full)](https://mahg.hockey.qc.ca/en/individual_tactics/)
- **Stick:** Stick on puck and an active stick are core gap components — [The Coaches Site, "Defencemen Habits: Proper Gap Control" (search snippet; paywalled)](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/defencemen-habits-proper-gap-control). The D's stick should take away the middle of the ice — [IceHockeyMan academy page (search snippet)](https://icehockeyman.com/category/ihm-academy/page/6/). Hockey Canada drill language: "stick on puck, body on body", close the gap without backing into the goalie — [Hockey Canada drill library (search snippet)](https://www.hockeycanada.ca/drills/renderdrills)
- **Common gap mistakes (Bertagna):** backing up as the forward goes wide (gives up the gap); slowing down at the defensive-zone entry, which lets the forward build speed; failing to follow the play up ice. — [Bertagna / Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/gap-control)
- **Young-D fear mistake:** leaving too much gap out of fear of being beaten, which keeps the D deep in his own zone. — [Trimble](https://andrewtrimble.substack.com/p/some-gap-control-basics)
- **Analytics on why the blue line matters:** Tracking from 2013-14 cited by Hockey-Graphs found about 0.66 unblocked shot attempts per controlled entry versus about 0.29 per uncontrolled entry (dump-in). — [Hockey-Graphs, "Measuring the Importance of Individual Player Zone Entry Creation", Aug 10, 2017](https://hockey-graphs.com/2017/08/10/measuring-the-importance-of-individual-player-zone-entry-creation/). Entry denial is "the most repeatable stat of zone entry defense." Controlled entries allowed are more affected by team systems. — [Corey S., "The Defenseman Compass", All Three Zones, Aug 25, 2023](https://allthreezones.substack.com/p/the-defenseman-compass). An Oilers tracking project found D who allowed fewer controlled entries also had fewer shot attempts against. — [The Sports Daily, "Tracking Stats: Zone Defence and Keeping the Other Team Out" (search snippet)](https://thesportsdaily.com/?p=755505)

### Inferences
- The 3-2-1 stick-length rule maps cleanly onto a VR metric: measure the distance from Jon's stick blade (or body) to the puck at each line crossing and score it against bands. Calibrate a "stick length" from Jon's real stick, not a generic number; his one-handed reach adds length beyond the two-handed reach.
- "Matching VMG" is a perception skill VR can isolate. Present attackers who weave (high lateral speed, low up-ice speed) versus those who drive straight, and score whether Jon's backward speed tracks the attacker's up-ice component rather than the apparent speed.
- Trimble's strong-side/off-wing rule is directly relevant to Jon. On the left D side, the attacker coming down Jon's side is usually the opposing right wing. A right-shot attacker there carries the puck on the boards side (strong side, so a wider gap and a poke chance on the cut). A left-shot attacker there carries it on the inside (off-wing, so a tighter gap). VR can randomize attacker handedness and score whether Jon adjusts his gap.
- Analytics say the blue line, not the slot, is where a D's repeatable value shows (entry denial). That argues for making blue-line denial scenarios (forcing a dump-in or an offside) the core of the program.
- The coaching split matters for scoring. Hockey Canada says "receive, don't attack", while Santagata says close space early. A VR scorer should reward speed-matched closing that ends at 1 stick length by the blue line, and penalize both the early step-up (lunge) and the three-steps-back retreat.

### Gaps
- No source gave gap distance as a function of measured attacker speed (for example in km/h or seconds to the blue line). The 3-2-1 rule is the only concrete number found, and it comes from a 2010 blog with no named author.
- No published time-to-close or reaction-time norms for defensemen were found.
- I could not read The Coaches Site's "Proper Gap Control" and "10 Keys to Better One-on-One Play" articles in full (paywalled).

## 2. Angling and one-handed defending (techniques for smaller D)

### Takeaway
Angling means steering the carrier wide and into the boards from an inside-out position, with the stick taking the middle lane. One-handed defending is accepted at the NHL level for mobility, reach and surprise, notably by smaller D (Josh Gorges, Jared Spurgeon). The shared technique is to keep the stick close and hidden, then strike late and fast. Coaches warn against lunging and note that one hand is weaker in battles, so players switch to two hands for loose pucks and board play.

### Cited Findings
- **Angling definition:** "Angling is the ability to force your opponent to go in the direction that you want. This normally would be towards the boards or to the outside of you." — [MAHG "Individual tactics" (search snippet)](https://mahg.hockey.qc.ca/en/individual_tactics/). Hockey Canada U9 drill: "D angles O to boards and rides out." — [Hockey Canada U9 practice plan 28 (search snippet)](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Coaching/u9-program/downloads/2020/u9-practice-plan-28-e.pdf)
- **Pressure control list (Hockey Canada DZ):** "Pressure: you can control speed, angle, stick, body, D side and recovery." Other cues: "D side positioning"; "Keep your stick on the ice"; "Body protect path to net, stick in passing lane"; "Stick on puck, body on body; shoulders square, move feet, no lunging"; "1-1 low 'take your stick with you' when containing"; "Pin and hold briefly; release on stay on D side." — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **Hockey Canada Skills of Gold 1-vs-1 inventory** (useful as a VR skill tree): positioning, redirecting the rush, pinching, gap control, closing the gap, stepping up, pressure or contain, angling, pinning, body checking, finishing checks. Shot blocking: stick in passing lane, active stick, body in shooting lane. — [Hockey Canada Skills of Gold, Defensive Tactics Skills Checklist (P. Carson, 2008)](https://www.hockeyeasternontario.ca/media/ncbaxqjk/hc_defensive_tactics_checklist.pdf)
- **One hand vs two hands, the NHL debate:**
  - Josh Gorges (Canadiens; described as undersized for a top-pair D): "sometimes I find if you have two hands on the stick you're limiting your mobility"; uses a long stick held one-handed in tight to his body: "I can hide it ... then you have that extra element of surprise."
  - P.K. Subban: "I also find it harder to skate with two hands."
  - Hal Gill (about 6'7"): "you still have to have the ability to battle with both hands on the stick."
  - The Sharks taught two hands at all times in 1-on-1s and puck battles because "you weren't as strong" with a free hand.
  - Per Gorges, Duncan Keith enters one-handed ("stick on puck") and switches to two hands once the puck is loose; Shea Weber enters two-handed for contact, then goes to one.
  - Consensus quote: "So for each player it's what works for you."
  — [Sean Gordon, "Should defencemen keep two hands on the stick?", The Globe and Mail, Dec 31, 2011](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/sports/hockey/should-defencemen-keep-two-hands-on-the-stick/article4182331)
- **One-handed poke mechanics:** hold the stick with one hand, forearm slightly bent and elbow close to the body; skate facing the carrier; watch the player with the puck in peripheral vision. "The poke check is primarily used while playing a 1 on 1." — [MAHG "Individual tactics" (search snippet; not read in full)](https://mahg.hockey.qc.ca/en/individual_tactics/)
- **Hide reach, strike late (small-D technique):**
  - Jared Spurgeon (listed 5'9") keeps his stick hidden on the inside so attackers can't judge his reach ("I like to keep it hidden").
  - Lidstrom holds the stick tight by his hip until ready, then pokes "with violence like a cobra."
  - Makar throws a small compact fake poke, waits for the attacker to commit and drive deep, and pokes the moment the attacker looks down at the puck.
  - Checklist: hide your stick and reach; don't overreach or overextend; wait for the correct moment; attack swiftly and purposefully.
  — [Greg Revak, "How to Poke-Check Better with Jared Spurgeon, Nicklas Lidstrom & Cale Makar", Hockey IQ Newsletter, Feb 12, 2023](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/poke-check)
- **Free hand use and anti-lunge:** use the free hand to push and control the forward's back or hips and turn him toward the boards. Don't swing the stick "like a weapon/sword"; overreaching throws the upper body off balance. Keep the stick down, on the puck, and in the shooting and passing lanes. Win the 1-on-1 first so support can get the puck. — [Santagata / LBX](https://lbxhockey.substack.com/p/how-to-play-a-1-v-1-defensemen)
- **Stick blade flat, inside-out, against the pass across:** a coaching source says to angle the stick blade flat to the ice, inside-out, so the pass across becomes impossible. — (search snippet in a 3-on-2 query; exact source page not identified, treat as unverified)
- **Progression advice:** the Horseshoe Defensive Gap Progression adds the stick only after several reps without one, to build feet and body position first. — [IceHockeySystems, "Horseshoe Defensive Gap Progression" (search snippet)](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/5711771)

### Inferences
- **How Jon's habit fits the coaching:** a left shot with only the left (bottom) hand on the stick has the blade on his left. Angling an attacker to his left (toward the left boards when he plays left D) is the textbook inside-out angle: his outside shoulder on the attacker's inside shoulder, with stick reach toward the boards lane. His style is a recognized NHL pattern (Gorges, Keith entering "stick on puck").
- **Risks the sources flag for this style:**
  - (a) Showing reach early. Spurgeon and Lidstrom hide it; a visible extended stick lets the attacker play around it.
  - (b) Lunging, so the body follows the stick (Hockey Canada "no lunging", Santagata).
  - (c) Weakness in battles. The Sharks' and Gill's reason for two hands; Keith switches to two hands when the puck is loose.
  - (d) Inference, not sourced: the one-handed reach is long to his forehand (left) but short across his body to the right. An attacker who cuts inside (to Jon's right/backhand) or attacks down the right side when Jon plays right D tests his weak side.
- **VR scenarios this suggests:**
  - A "hidden reach" score: track how long the stick is extended before the strike, and reward a short exposure followed by a fast poke.
  - A poke-timing trigger keyed to the attacker's cue. Attacker eyes drop to the puck, the puck moves away from the attacker's body, or the attacker takes the stick off the puck (Makar cue). Reward pokes in that window; penalize pokes while the puck is protected.
  - An inside-cut stress test: attackers deliberately cut to Jon's backhand side. Score whether he keeps body position (inside shoulder) rather than reaching across.
  - A "switch to two hands" prompt when the puck becomes loose or the attacker is pinned on the wall (the Keith pattern).
- Gorges (long stick, held tight) and Spurgeon (hidden on the inside) are the most directly relevant role models for a 5'8" one-handed angler.

### Gaps
- No coaching manual (USA Hockey or Hockey Canada) was found that prescribes when to take the top hand off. The guidance is player testimony and blog analysis.
- No data was found on poke-check success rates, or on how stick length or one-handed reach changes takeaways.
- I found nothing specific on how far a one-handed reach extends compared with two-handed reach, or on the risk of a slash or hook penalty when reaching one-handed.

## 3. Reading rushes (1-on-1, 2-on-1, 2-on-2, 3-on-2)

### Takeaway
On a 2-on-1 the traditional rule is "D takes the pass, goalie takes the shooter". The D stays between the attackers with the stick in the passing lane, angles closer to the carrier as he gets deeper, doesn't drift to the weak side, and doesn't commit early. Hockey Canada's checklist and goalie coaches note the alternative of pressuring the carrier, and the system must be agreed with the goalie. Material on 2-on-2 and 3-on-2 reads is thinner: count the numbers, keep tight gaps so it doesn't become a 3-on-1, hold or switch on crossers, step up on delays, and communicate with the backchecker.

### Cited Findings
- **1-on-1 cues:** keep good gap control and don't be flat-footed. The forward can shoot through the D as a screen or go around him. — [Wilson and van Vliet, *Hockey Goaltending* excerpt, Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/defend-against-common-plays-off-the-opposition-rush). Hockey Canada 1-on-1 key elements: Body Alignment, Gap Control, Active Stick, Finish ALL Checks — (search snippet attributed to Hockey Canada drill material; exact page not verified). Read via the chest/logo (see section 1).
- **If beaten wide:** "Turn inside to post if beaten wide." — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf). If beaten, pursue to the inside near the post to cut off a cross-ice pass. — [Trimble](https://andrewtrimble.substack.com/p/some-gap-control-basics)
- **2-on-1, traditional rule:** "the goaltender should take the shot and the defender should eliminate the pass across." Coaching points:
  - Stay *between* the attackers so the goalie doesn't have to move laterally.
  - Keep the stick in the passing lane.
  - As the carrier goes deeper, increase pressure by angling closer to him while keeping the stick in the lane.
  - Don't drift to the weak-side player, which lets the carrier cut to the middle.
  - Force the carrier outside to shoot from there.
  - Don't commit early, especially against criss-crosses; on a criss-cross, hold the middle.
  - On a delay, use the stick to take passing space and don't turn your back on the carrier.
  — [HockeyShare, "2 on 1 Dissected (Part 2)", Apr 26, 2010](https://www.hockeyshare.com/blog/2-on-1-dissected-part-2). Part 1 covers offense and assumes the D sits in the passing lane — [HockeyShare Part 1, Dec 11, 2009](https://www.hockeyshare.com/blog/2-on-1-dissected-part-1)
- **Coaches differ on the 2-on-1:** Hockey Canada's Skills of Gold lists two 2-on-1 rush-defense options: "Play non puck carrier - take away pass" and "Play puck carrier - with pressure." — [Hockey Canada Skills of Gold checklist](https://www.hockeyeasternontario.ca/media/ncbaxqjk/hc_defensive_tactics_checklist.pdf). Goalie coaches note some coaches have the D take the pass, some play strong to the carrier, and some play the middle, so agree on the system in practice. — [Wilson and van Vliet / Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/defend-against-common-plays-off-the-opposition-rush)
- **What the goalie expects:** the goalie plays slightly strong toward the carrier, ready for the pass. If a pass is available he plays a little deeper to prepare a butterfly slide. He reads skater speed early, the shooter's handedness, and whether the non-carrier is heading backdoor. — [Wilson and van Vliet / Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/defend-against-common-plays-off-the-opposition-rush). Search summaries of 2-on-1 coaching material state the trust contract: the D must trust the goalie to make the save, and the goalie must trust the D to stop the backdoor pass. They also say to hold off the slide until the last possible moment, so the ideal outcome is a shot from an angle the goalie can save. — (search snippets from [Hockey Ministries "Thinking the game: dissecting the 2 on 1"](https://www.hockeyministries.org/home-ice/blog/thinking-the-game-dissecting-the-2-on-1-part-1/) / [modsquadhockey forum](https://modsquadhockey.com/forums/topic/59273-how-to-defend-2-on-1); not read in full)
- **Modern critique:** commentary argues "D takes the pass" can hand the carrier a free shot ("essentially give a breakaway"), and that the rule dates from when goalies lacked today's lateral mobility. — (search snippets from [Blueshirt Banter, "How the Rangers Can Improve Their Two-on-One Defending"](https://www.blueshirtbanter.com/how-the-rangers-can-improve-their-two-on-one-defending/) and [Daily Faceoff, McKenna's Breakdown](https://dailyfaceoff.com/mckennas-breakdown-tough-maple-leafs-debut-for-ilya-lyubushkin); exact attribution not verified)
- **2-on-2 and 3-on-2 cues (Hockey Canada):** "Build from individual tactics: 1-1, 2-1, 3-1, 3-2, 2-2, 3-3 off rush"; "Step up on delays into the zone"; "Playing crossing forwards: strategy....hold or cross." — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf). Skills of Gold lists rush situations 2-on-1, 3-on-1, 2-on-2, 3-on-2, 1-on-2, 3-on-3 tracking, 3-on-3 rush, and "Hinge support - 2 on 2" in the D zone. — [Skills of Gold](https://www.hockeyeasternontario.ca/media/ncbaxqjk/hc_defensive_tactics_checklist.pdf)
- **3-on-2 notes (weaker sources):**
  - Keep tight gaps to prevent the 3-on-2 becoming a 3-on-1 on the goalie.
  - Communication with the backchecking forward decides whether the D commits to the carrier.
  - The middle D stays with the player driving the net.
  - Nolan Pratt's rush video theme: "count the numbers, communicate and dictate".
  — (search snippets, including [The Coaches Site, "Keys to Defending the Rush with Nolan Pratt"](https://members.thecoachessite.com/video/keys-to-defending-the-rush-with-nolan-pratt) and [IceHockeySystems "Backchecking vs. 3 on 2 - Weak Side"](https://icehockeysystems.com/print/standard/39386) (403 on fetch); not read in full)
- **USA Hockey drill (3-on-1):** D1 breaks out to two forwards who attack 3-on-1 against D2 (D1 gets one play, then leaves). The D who wins the puck transitions to offense. — [USA Hockey, "14U+: Live Action 3v1 D Activate", Jun 2, 2016](https://www.usahockey.com/news_article/show/655507)
- **Steering:** "Angle and steer the attack wide: be on the inside of the rush. Know the different situations 1-1, 2-1 etc." — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)

### Inferences
- **VR 2-on-1 design:** score (a) staying between the attackers with the stick blade in the pass lane, (b) the gap closing toward the carrier as he crosses the hash marks, (c) no early commit or slide, and (d) whether the shot comes from outside the dots. Add a goalie-system toggle, "D takes pass" versus "D pressures carrier", since coaches and Hockey Canada list both. In rec hockey Jon should learn his own goalie's expectation.
- For a one-handed left-shot D on a 2-on-1, the stick-in-lane posture is natural when the pass lane is on his forehand side (the pass receiver to his left). When the trailing attacker is to his right, his blade has to cross his body. VR should test both sides.
- Rush reads to randomize: attacker handedness, the carrier's head and shoulders (to pass or to shoot), the non-carrier going backdoor or driving middle, crossers (hold or switch with a virtual partner), and delays (step up, per Hockey Canada).

### Gaps
- No authoritative, detailed 2-on-2 or 3-on-2 assignment scheme (who takes whom, switch rules on crossing attackers) was found in readable primary sources. Hockey Canada lists the situations but not the content.
- No study was found on which visual cues (head, shoulders, stick) best predict pass versus shot for defensemen. The cues cited are coaching opinion.

## 4. Retrievals and breakouts

### Takeaway
Coaches teach the D to shoulder check before (and while) going back for the puck, approach it at an angle with deception, "explode" to the puck to buy time, and know the play before touching it. The choice comes from reading pressure and support: quick up, wheel, D-to-D/overs, reverse, rim, or skate it. No source gave a fixed number of shoulder checks. USA Hockey stresses "when and where" to look, and says scripted shoulder-check drills fail unless the player must read a real conflict.

### Cited Findings
- **Retrieval cues (Hockey Canada):**
  - "Retrievals: Picking up the puck: shoulder check, angle, and deception. Explode to the puck to give yourself time to make a play."
  - "Toes up ice pick up if time and space. Learn to 'circle the puck.' Pickup and make plays on forehand when possible."
  - "Support: Look for both support and pressure and make a decision. Good first play. Practice look offs and deception."
  - "Know what you are going to do before you touch the puck."
  - "Strategy: to take a hit and protect the puck. Deception, roll off, contact with opponent before check."
  - "Often the simplest play is the best."
  - "Don't give your problem to someone else, eat it or get it out."
  — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **Breakout vocabulary (Hockey Canada):** "quick up, wheel, overs, reverse, rim, set, move it, flip, glass" — establish this vocabulary with partner and goalie. "Read puck possession quality when supporting your D partner. Good decision from net front to creating an open passing lane." Breakout skills listed: quick up, wheel, overs, reverse, rim, set, support. — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **Neutral-zone regroup decisions (Hockey Canada):**
  - "First option is a quick up to the forwards whenever possible."
  - "Use your partner to spread the forecheck with D-D pass."
  - "Look off F1: draw him? Pass around him?"
  - "Pass when you should not when you have to." "Don't overhandle the puck: 'no dust.'"
  - "Read support and pressure like a quarterback."
  - "All dumps and rims go to an area where goalies cannot play the puck."
  — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **Shoulder check every time (Kelvin Cech):** good defencemen shoulder check before retrieving so they know where the puck should go next. It reduces turnovers and dangerous hits. The author stresses doing it every time but gives no count. Basic drill: a partner plays a puck along the boards; the D checks, retrieves, and makes a return pass, which can then be made more game-like. — [Kelvin Cech, "Defencemen Habits: Shoulder Checks on Retrievals", The Coaches Site (date not shown; likely 2025)](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/defencemen-habits-shoulder-checks-on-retrievals-1)
- **USA Hockey on scanning (ADM):**
  - Ken Martel (USA Hockey Senior Director of Player and Coach Development) calls scanning the new name for "shoulder checks", particularly on puck retrieval in one's own end: "It's more about when and where they look."
  - Traditional shoulder-check drills are ineffective if the player "doesn't really have to observe any conflict on the play." Dan Jablonic (player development manager) says scripted go-here/go-there drills don't build hockey IQ.
  - Design drills where the retriever must scan and solve a problem.
  - Up to nine other skaters can be up to 180 degrees outside a player's direct field of view. Players touch the puck less than about 2% of the game.
  - Scanning also cuts injury risk from unseen hits.
  — [Michael Rand, "12U: Scan Like Messi", USA Hockey / admkids.com, Jun 12, 2024](https://admkids.com/news_article/show/1312331)
- **Both shoulders:** a breakout drill's coaching point: "Defenseman: shoulder check both sides when retrieving the puck." — (search snippet from a HockeyShare/IceHockeySystems breakout drill page; exact page not verified)
- **Pro view (Ian Cole, 1,000+ NHL games, Oct 2026):** "We want to move [the puck] to our forwards as quickly as possible." Breakouts are a "long equation" where variables change between going for the puck and touching it: "These are things we need to train our brain to solve quickly." Reading F3: "If their 'F3' goes and stands in front of our winger, we're going to rim it right to somebody." "Sure, maybe a weak-side rim is the play, but it might not be the play every time." Options named: weak-side rim, strong-side outlet, pass to the center, cut back and skate out. Opponents pick up on repeated patterns, so vary choices. — [Ben Pope, Chicago Sun-Times, Oct 22, 2026](https://chicago.suntimes.com/blackhawks/2026/10/22/blackhawks-breakouts-nhl-science-ian-cole-1000-games)
- **Retrieve at an angle; wall/net bump; head position:**
  - Retrieve at an angle to keep speed and avoid going head-on into the boards.
  - Use a head or shoulder fake to sell the forechecker.
  - Behind the net, don't turn the shoulders unless pressured, since that takes away vision and options.
  - Under heavy pressure, D2 or C can call "wall bump" or "net bump", and D1 bumps the puck to the wall rather than toward the net.
  — (search snippets from breakout articles including [The Coaches Site "Hockey Breakout Options – Part 2"](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/hockey-breakout-options-part-2) and [IceHockeySystems "Tips & Drills To Improve Your Defensive Zone Breakout"](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/140436); attribution to a specific page not verified)
- **Analytics:** carried exits are the most repeatable exit type for a D and depend on skating and moving through a forecheck. The successful D-zone retrieval percentage is weighted over raw retrieval counts. Exact rates appear only in chart images. Jaccob Slavin is cited as having one of the highest entry-denial rates alongside one of the lowest possession-exit rates. — [All Three Zones, "The Defenseman Compass"](https://allthreezones.substack.com/p/the-defenseman-compass)

### Inferences
- **VR retrieval drill (high value, solo-friendly):** a puck is dumped or rimmed in. Forecheckers (F1 and possibly F2) and Jon's own support (partner, winger on the wall, center) spawn in randomized positions that are visible only behind Jon. He must turn his head (tracked by the HMD) before reaching the puck, then choose a call: quick up, wheel, D-to-D, reverse, rim (weak side), bump, or skate. Scoring:
  - number and timing of head turns before the puck
  - whether the choice matches the pressure and support layout
  - decision latency after the touch (Hockey Canada: "know what you're going to do before you touch the puck")
- USA Hockey's point that scripted shoulder checks don't transfer argues for making the information behind Jon change every rep and actually decide the right answer, so a check is required to succeed.
- Cole's "F3 stands in front of our winger, so we rim it" is a concrete if-then cue VR can encode. The anti-pattern warning suggests varying which option is correct, so Jon doesn't learn a single default (such as always rimming).
- A one-handed angler who is smaller benefits most from avoiding hits. Scanning plus "explode to the puck" plus the "eat it or get it out" rule of thumb fits a rec player who can't win many board battles on strength.

### Gaps
- No source gave a specific number of shoulder checks or their timing (for example, two checks: one at the start of the route and one about two strides before the puck). The common coaching phrase "check both shoulders" lacks a verified primary source here.
- No published retrieval-success or exit-success rates by decision type (rim versus pass versus carry) were readable. The All Three Zones numbers are in images.
- Detailed decision trees for reading 1-man versus 2-man forechecks were not found in readable sources.

## 5. Defensive-zone coverage basics for D, and common recreational mistakes

### Takeaway
Hockey Canada's D-zone cues cover most of it:
- Stay D-side with the stick on the ice in the passing lane, and keep the body between the attacker and the net.
- At the net front: box out, face the puck, control sticks, and don't screen the goalie.
- Skate in straight lines with stops and starts, not loops.
- Pin and release on the wall; turn inside to the post if beaten.

The most-cited mistakes are a gap that is too big, lunging or overreaching, puck watching, curling instead of stopping, drifting to the weak side, committing early, and double-screening the goalie. No authoritative beer-league-specific list was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Hockey Canada D-zone cues:**
  - Key aim: "limit time and space"; "Pressure and contain in a responsible manner."
  - "D side positioning"; "Keep your stick on the ice"; "Body protect path to net, stick in passing lane"; "Stick on puck, body on body; shoulders square, move feet, no lunging."
  - "Straight line skating: stops and starts vs. loops"; "Beat them to net front after check."
  - "Net front: box out, face the puck, control sticks, toes up ice, front shots, control F rebounds. Do not get tied up with F. Let goalie see puck, no double screens. Head on swivel."
  - "'Front' the puck or to the side. Let goalie see the puck."
  - "Shade on F3 way"; "Control sticks and jump rebounds"; "Defend cycle and ride them off"; "Turn inside to post if beaten wide"; "Get in shooting lanes for blocks."
  — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **Skills of Gold D-zone situations** (a checklist for scenario coverage): role of D1 and D2; puck carrier behind the net; corner (into and out of); net front; point; side boards; hinge support 2-on-2; 3-on-3 shade; defending the cycle; activated defenseman. Backside support: "Head on a swivel", "Sagging". — [Hockey Canada Skills of Gold checklist](https://www.hockeyeasternontario.ca/media/ncbaxqjk/hc_defensive_tactics_checklist.pdf)
- **Hockey Canada development balance:** by mid-teens and beyond, D emphasis "needs to also include perception skills of anticipation and reaction and the cognitive aspects of team play." Small-area games build "read and react" so D "think the game" in practice. — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **Two most common defensive errors:**
  - (1) Puck watching. Fix: track the puck but verify opponents' locations by sight or touch, and scan continually.
  - (2) Curling instead of stop-starting. "A poor defensive-zone route is a curl away from the check." Fix: sharp stops and starts.
  - Also: be patient, and attach to pass-receiving threats.
  - The article's examples are forwards, but the principles are general.
  — [Greg Revak, "Two Common Defensive Errors (And How To Fix Them)", Hockey IQ Newsletter, Apr 30, 2023](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/two-most-common-defensive-errors)
- **1-on-1 mistakes:** excessive crossovers and sporadic feet; giving up too much gap; chasing with the stick "like a sword"; letting forwards walk untouched in the corners or climb the wall without contact; letting shots reach the net with no stick or body in the way. — [Santagata / LBX](https://lbxhockey.substack.com/p/how-to-play-a-1-v-1-defensemen)
- **2-on-1 mistakes:** drifting to the weak-side player; committing early; letting the stick drop out of the lane; turning your back on a delay. — [HockeyShare Part 2](https://www.hockeyshare.com/blog/2-on-1-dissected-part-2)
- **Gap mistakes:** backing up as the forward goes wide; slowing at the defensive-zone entry; not following the play up ice. — [Bertagna / Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/gap-control)
- **Recreational context (weak sources):** simple left/right half-zone splits are a good starting point because recreational players often struggle just to reach the right spot. — [Defending Big D, "Hockey Positioning Primer: Center and Defensemen in the Defensive Zone" (search snippet)](https://www.defendingbigd.com/hockey-positioning-primer-center-and-defensemen-in-the-defensive-zone/). Adults returning after a break often struggle with balance and backward skating. — (search snippet from [MyPuck, "Common mistakes beer league hockey make"](https://www.mypuck.com/blog/common-mistakes-beer-league-hockey-make); not read in full). A beer-league podcast urges slower players to be where teammates expect them. — [The Beer Leaguer podcast, "Position, Pass, Repeat: A Slow Skater's Guide to Beer League" (search snippet)](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2243200/episodes/18109976-position-pass-repeat-a-slow-skater-s-guide-to-beer-league)
- **Jack Han on what NHL teams break on purpose:** his post covers two D-zone principles that beer league through college should follow but that elite NHL teams (Colorado) break deliberately. The principles themselves are paywalled. — [Jack Han, "DZ mistakes that you *can* make", Hockey Tactics Newsletter, Oct 2, 2025](https://jhanhky.substack.com/p/dz-mistakes-that-you-can-make)

### Inferences
- **VR net-front scenario:** cycle play in the corner and behind the net, with a net-front attacker. Score:
  - D-side body position (between the attacker and the net)
  - stick on the ice in the lane to the slot
  - head-swivel frequency (scanning between the puck and his man, per Revak and "head on a swivel")
  - not standing in the goalie's sightline (Hockey Canada's "no double screens", measured as a puck-to-goalie line-of-sight check)
- **VR "stop, don't curl" check:** track Jon's path in D-zone rotations, and flag loops that open a lane (Revak). This is a positioning-and-decision item that doesn't need skating mechanics.
- The rec mistake set most relevant to Jon's profile is overreaching one-handed (lunging) and a gap that is too big. Both are measurable in VR.

### Gaps
- No authoritative, data-backed list of recreational or beer-league defenseman mistakes was found. The rec-specific items come from blogs and podcast snippets.
- Weak-side D duties (sagging to the slot, taking the backdoor man, when to switch on behind-the-net plays) were listed but not explained in readable primary sources.

## 6. Established drills adaptable to a solo VR player

### Takeaway
Most established D drills are 1-on-1 or rush progressions built on a pattern (pivot, close the gap, 1-on-1, angle to the boards) or on a retrieve-under-pressure pattern. They translate to VR by replacing the live forward with a scripted or AI attacker whose speed, handedness, route and cue timing can be randomized. USA Hockey's guidance favors drills with real conflict and reading over scripted movement.

### Cited Findings
- **Michigan Tech 1-on-1 (Weiss Tech Hockey):** two D skate a figure-8 around cones, make a breakout pass to a forward swinging from the far end, then close the gap on the forward from the same side. Coach whistles switch the forwards' direction, so each D must re-close on a different attacker, possibly several times per rep. Coaching points: the gap "MUST be closed"; get outside the blue line before closing; get speed up quickly after the pivot. — [Jeremy Weiss, Weiss Tech Hockey, Oct 21, 2010](https://weisstechhockey.com/michigan-tech-1-on-1-drill/)
- **HockeyShare gap/pylon drill:** three to four pylons per side. One D and one F go; the D matches the forward's gap through the pylons, pivoting to face the puck at each pylon, ending in a 1-on-1. — [HockeyShare "Gap Control" drill (search snippet)](https://www.hockeyshare.com/drills/print/148); see also [HockeyShare "1 on 1 Defensive Gap Drill"](https://www.hockeyshare.com/drills/print/31491)
- **Horseshoe Defensive Gap Progression:** a progression to teach gap timing, adding the stick after several reps without it. — [IceHockeySystems (search snippet)](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/5711771)
- **1-on-1 Re-Gap Transition:** a drill where the D must re-establish the gap after a transition. — [IceHockeySystems (title/search only)](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/6892841)
- **Wall Rush 1-on-1:** a forward attacks down the boards off a coach's pass, and the D works on gap control and angling. — [The Coaches Site (search snippet; paywalled)](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/wall-rush-1-on-1-2)
- **Hockey Canada forward dump-and-turn gap drill:** a forward dumps the puck, the D retrieves it and breaks the forward out, then the forward turns back for a 1-on-1; "The defenseman must close gap and play the 1-1." — [Hockey Canada drill library (search snippet)](https://www.hockeycanada.ca/drills/renderdrills)
- **USA Hockey 14U+ Live Action 3v1 D Activate:** D1 breaks out to two forwards who attack 3-on-1 versus D2 (D1 gets one play, then leaves), then D2 breaks out to the backcheckers and the next D takes the rush. Emphasis on shutting down the rush or winning the puck, then transitioning to offense. — [USA Hockey, Jun 2, 2016](https://www.usahockey.com/news_article/show/655507)
- **Shoulder-check retrieval drill:** a partner rims the puck, the D shoulder checks, retrieves and returns a pass, then progresses to more game-like pressure. — [Kelvin Cech, The Coaches Site](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/defencemen-habits-shoulder-checks-on-retrievals-1)
- **Design principle (USA Hockey ADM):** scanning drills must force a real read ("simulate a game situation in which a player retrieving the puck must scan and solve a problem"), not prescribed movement. — [USA Hockey / admkids, "12U: Scan Like Messi"](https://admkids.com/news_article/show/1312331)
- **Small-area games (Hockey Canada, citing Barry Trotz):** smaller space, higher intensity and lots of puck touches, putting players in "read and react" situations; skills include angling and steering, transition skating and lateral movement. "Decrease the Space! Increase the Pace!" — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf). Hockey Canada's drill hub has a "Defence" filter with 174 drills (diagram, video, game clip) — [Hockey Canada 2018-19, resources page](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)
- **Other named drills (titles only):** Four Lines 1 on 1 ([IceHockeySystems](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/1184)); Multi-pass 1 on 1 ([Trimble](https://andrewtrimble.substack.com/p/multi-pass-1-on-1-drill)); 2 on 1 to 3 on 2 High Man Backcheck ([IceHockeySystems](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/2971281)); Toronto 3 on 2 ([IceHockeySystems](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/3050051)); Angling Stations ([IceHockeySystems](https://ihstest.icehockeysystems.com/print/practice/rows/3553781)); Defenceman Breakout Drill ([HockeyShare](https://www.hockeyshare.com/free-drills/print/13427)); Strong Side Breakout Drill ([IceHockeySystems](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/3484466))
- **Hockey Canada season emphasis:** retrievals, 1-on-1s, partner support and D-zone coverage recur monthly from November on. The "seasonal structure breakdown" allocates 40% introducing technical skills, 25% developing, 20% refining, 10% introducing individual tactics and 5% developing individual tactics. — [Hockey Canada 2018-19](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)

### Inferences
Each line below adapts a cited drill to solo VR (my synthesis, not from the sources):
- **"Michigan Tech re-gap"** becomes a pass-then-close drill. Jon makes a virtual breakout pass, an AI forward receives it, and random "whistles" switch the attacker's lane, forcing re-closes. Score the gap at each line (3-2-1 rule) and the time to re-establish it.
- **"Pylon gap match"** becomes a pure perception drill. The attacker weaves through virtual pylons at varying VMG; Jon must hold the gap and face the puck at each pylon. Add the stick only after body reps (Horseshoe progression).
- **"Wall Rush 1-on-1"** becomes Jon's signature drill. The attacker comes down his left (forehand) wall. He angles to the boards with a one-handed hidden reach and pokes on the cue (the attacker's eyes drop or the puck leaves the hip). Mirror it to the right wall and inside cuts to train the weak side.
- **"2-on-1 lane hold"** randomizes the carrier side, the backdoor runner, criss-crosses and delays, with a goalie-system toggle.
- **"Scan-to-retrieve"** uses randomized forecheck and support behind Jon that decides the correct outlet, with HMD head-turn tracking. This follows the USA Hockey principle that the check must matter.
- **"Net-front box-out"** scores D-side position, stick lane, scan rate and no goalie screen during a scripted cycle.

### Gaps
- I found no USA Hockey ADM drill specific to defensemen's gap or angling. The USA Hockey items found are the 3v1 D Activate drill and the scanning article.
- No published drill was designed specifically for smaller or one-handed defensemen.
- No validated VR hockey decision-training study was found in this scope; another researcher may cover VR transfer.
