# Does perceptual-cognitive and VR training transfer to on-ice / on-field performance? (evidence for a VR hockey-sense trainer)

Notes compiled 2026-11-07. Scope: 2010 onward (a few older anchors flagged). Each item is tagged where possible with **[IND]** = independent / academic, no stated industry tie; **[IND-LINKED]** = academic authors with a tie to the product maker; **[VENDOR]** = company claim or marketing; **[PRESS]** = media/testimonial. "In-VR / lab gain" means improvement on the trained or a lab test; "transfer" means improvement on a real-sport task (field test, small-sided game, match statistics).

---

## 1. Controlled research on VR training in hockey (Sense Arena, university/team trials) and VR sport training generally: is there transfer to real game performance?

### Takeaway
For ice hockey specifically, I found **no peer-reviewed controlled study showing that VR training (Sense Arena or any other) improves on-ice or game performance**; the hockey VR studies that exist show in-VR or lab-test gains only, with small samples (n = 20-34) and no on-ice outcome. Outside hockey, a small number of well-designed VR studies do show real-world transfer (best example: Gray 2017 adaptive baseball batting, n = 80, with transfer to league stats), and pooled effects across VR sport studies are moderate-to-large (g ~0.6-1.2) but heterogeneous, with transfer rarely tested.

### Cited Findings

**Hockey: Sense Arena-specific evidence**

- [VENDOR, unverified attribution] A Sense Arena CEO quote surfaced in search claiming "a 10-15% improvement in cognitive and physical abilities specific to hockey" after three months of use at Charles University, Prague; no published methodology was found. I could not confirm which page in the results carried the quote (possibly the HTC Vive blog). — [HTC Vive blog](https://blog.vive.com/us/look-sense-arena-vrs-first-pro-level-hockey-training-tool/)
- [IND-LINKED] Tomáš Perič (Charles University) was described in 2018 as part of Sense Arena, helping run its VR lab; the system then targeted beginners aged 10-12. — [Lupa.cz (2018)](https://www.lupa.cz/clanky/ceska-sense-arena-trenuje-hokej-ve-virtualni-realite-zajem-o-system-maji-i-v-nhl/)
- [IND-LINKED] A 2020 Charles University bachelor's thesis (advisor Perič) tested 197 players aged 13-15 on eight VR tests (four cognitive, four hockey-specific) to relate hockey skills to cognitive functions — a correlational/assessment study, not a training trial. Only snippets seen. — [NUSL thesis record](https://invenio.nusl.cz/record/537430?ln=en); [NUSL record 2](https://invenio.nusl.cz/record/456652?ln=en)
- [IND/IND-LINKED] UW-Stevens Point partnered with Charles University (2019) for an 11-week trial with 34 men's and women's players, all baseline-tested, half using Sense Arena, aiming to test performance and injury reduction. I found no published outcome data. — [UWSP news (2019)](https://www.uwsp.edu/ucm/uNews/Pages/WPR-VirtualReality19.aspx); [WPR](https://wpr.org/virtual-reality-brain-training-project-aims-reduce-hockey-injuries)
- [IND] Heilmann & Schubert (2025), *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*: Sense Arena (Meta Quest 2, stick-mounted controller; drills: Multiple-Object-Tracking, Find the Lane, Divided Attention) vs active control (technical training) in 25 elite youth academy players (median age 14; IG n = 12, CG n = 13); 9 weeks x 2 sessions/week (18 sessions; session length reported inconsistently as 40-60 min and 2 x 20-30 min). No funding, no declared conflicts. Outcomes: Flanker and cued Go/No-Go, generic (PC) and sport-specific (projector + stick buttons) versions. Time effects were large (sport-specific Flanker congruent ηp² = 0.722) but **group effects were not significant** (congruent p = .247; incongruent p = .181); within-group IG d ~1.9 vs CG d ~0.45-0.53; accuracy showed no effects. Allocation was described as both "randomized" and assigned by training group (likely non-random); no power analysis; abstract and results p-values disagree. **On-ice performance was not measured.** — [Heilmann & Schubert 2025 (PMC12611849)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12611849/)
- [PRESS] NHL.com and goalie media report goalie use (e.g., Daccord) and on-ice "success" narratives; these are testimonials, not controlled data. — [NHL.com](https://www.nhl.com/news/unmasked-goalies-use-virtual-reality-program); [Shap Shots Hockey](https://www.shapshotshockey.com/p/how-saves-in-virtual-reality-have); [InGoal Magazine (2020)](https://ingoalmag.com/2020/11/30/sense-arena-virtual-reality-vrtraining-for-goaltenders/)

**Hockey: other VR / perceptual studies**
- [IND] Bloechle et al. (2025), *Sports Medicine – Open*, University of Fribourg (+ one HC Fribourg-Gottéron staff member), university-funded, no conflicts. 34 male Swiss National League professionals (30 also national-team players), randomized 17/17. Task: in VR (Pimax 5K XR, Unity, motion-captured goalie), pick the largest exposed area (LEA) of the net from a 5-button box within 2 s. **One ~15-min session (54 trials)**. Feedback group: 0.78 ± 0.07 -> 0.90 ± 0.06 (p < .001, BF = 94,413; ~12 percentage points / ~15% relative); no-feedback control: 0.76 -> 0.78 (p = .21). Mechanism: the "eyes-puck gap" (the open net seen from the eyes differs from the open net seen from the puck); larger gaps lowered pre-test scores, and feedback (showing the puck's-eye view) removed that link. **No retention test, no on-ice/real shooting test.** — [Bloechle et al. 2025 (PMC11993527)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11993527); [Univ. Fribourg press release](https://www.unifr.ch/news/en/32915/everybody-s-a-top-scorer-thanks-to-virtual-reality/)
- [IND] Polikanova et al. (2022), *Sports* (Basel), Moscow State/HSE, Russian Science Foundation: pilot comparing 13 pro hockey players vs 7 freestyle wrestlers in a VR puck-blocking task (SteamVR full-body tracking). No group difference in puck hits in any block; hockey players faster on stick response in 2 of 4 blocks (e.g., 1.13 vs 1.58 s). The authors state VR "does not always contribute to the transfer of the trained skill to reality"; no transfer test. — [Polikanova et al. 2022 (PMC9414154)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9414154)
- [IND] Korean study: MOT-based training in 20 male hockey players (10 elite, 10 non-elite); passing-decision accuracy on video scenarios rose pre-to-post in both groups but the group x time interaction was not significant (response time did show a time effect). Video-test outcome only. — [Korean Journal of Sport Science 37(2)](https://kjss.sports.re.kr/v.37/2/177/1069)
- [IND, pilot; equipment supplied by Nike] Mitroff et al. (2013), *Athletic Training & Sports Health Care*: stroboscopic eyewear with Carolina Hurricanes players in preseason camp; press reports an 18% improvement on an on-ice drill vs no change in controls; sample reported as 11 players (sources disagree); Mitroff called the results preliminary. Primary paper not read. — [Duke Today (2013)](https://today.duke.edu/2013/12/hockeymitroff); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20131216/Hockey-players-who-trained-with-strobe-eyewear-show-improvement-in-practice-drills.aspx)
- [IND] I found no randomized trial of VR training for hockey goaltenders measuring save anticipation with on-ice transfer; the closest goalie-VR work is a 2008 expert-novice comparison (10 novice, 9 expert) of 2D vs 3D virtual environments (pre-2010, assessment only). — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256409802_Ice_Hockey_Goaltenders'_Strategies_Reaction_Times_and_Anticipation_Times_in_Two-_and_Three-Dimensional_Virtual_Environments)

**VR sport training generally (reviews, meta-analyses, key transfer studies)**
- [IND] Gray (2017), *Frontiers in Psychology*: 80 male high-school senior baseball players randomized into 4 groups of 20 (adaptive VR hitting; plain VR batting practice; extra real batting practice; no-extra-training control); 2 x 45 min/week for 6 weeks (30 pitches/session). Adaptive VR group pre-post: real hits d = 1.9; reduced swings at balls outside the zone (O-Swing %) d = 2.8; pitch-type recognition d = 2.7. Versus control: real O-Swing d = 1.7, real hits d = 1.2. **Plain VR batting practice did not transfer to real hits.** 5-year follow-up: senior-season OBP differed by group (F(3,74) = 10.8, ηp² = 0.30; adaptive vs control d = 1.8; vs real practice d = 0.7, marginal); 8/20 adaptive-group players reached a higher level (A/AA/NCAA/NCJAA) vs 1-3 in other groups (χ² = 7.9, p = .047). Caveats: low-fidelity VR (small FOV, no binocular cues), non-game "hit" definition, single author, data collected 2008-2010, some internal inconsistencies (e.g., "68% variance" vs R² = .47). No funding stated; no conflicts. — [Gray 2017 (PMC5733365)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5733365)
- [IND] Michalski et al. (2019), *PLoS ONE*: 57 novices randomized (VR n = 29, control n = 28); 6 x ~30 min sessions of *Eleven: Table Tennis VR* (HTC Vive, adaptive AI opponent; 3.5 h total). Blinded coach-rated real table tennis: post-test 189.9 vs 111.1 (d = 1.08); skill-quality change d = 1.10. No real-practice comparator, single rater, no retention, novices only. — [Michalski et al. 2019 (PMC6736297)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6736297/)
- [IND] Michalski et al. (2019) systematic review, *Frontiers in Psychology*: real-world performance improved after VR training in several sports (incl. baseball batting, table tennis), but not in every study. — [Frontiers 2019 (10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02159)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02159/full)
- [IND] Harris et al. (2020), *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*: Exp. 1 — moving from VR to real putting temporarily disrupted both quiet-eye gaze and accuracy; Exp. 2 — novices trained in VR or the real world improved putting accuracy comparably. Conclusion: skill structure can be learned in VR and transferred, but "perceptual deficiencies will place limits on the use of VR." — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/160944); [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/121321)
- [IND] Racket-sport VR meta-analysis (2026, *PLOS ONE*): 6 controlled trials (tennis, table tennis); Hedges' g = 0.78 (I² = 52%), but 95% prediction interval [-0.01, 1.57] spans zero; the single perceptual-cognitive trial g = 0.81 [-0.05, 1.67]; insufficient testing of transfer to real performance called a recurring weakness. — [PLOS ONE 2026](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0345541)
- [IND] Immersive-training motor competence meta-analysis (2026, *Scientific Reports*): pooled effects 0.62-1.15 across domains; complex skills benefit more; most interventions 4-12 weeks; long-term retention and real-world transfer "underexplored". — [Scientific Reports 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-42962-6)
- [IND] Scoping review of VR decision-making training (2025, *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*): only 6 of 25 papers assessed transfer, flagged as the key gap. (Abstract-level only; full text blocked.) — [Taylor & Francis 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2025.2484727)
- [IND] Jia et al. (2024), *Frontiers in Psychology*, scoping review of animated VR and 360° VR for team-sport decision-making: 20 studies, ~570 participants, sample sizes 3-80; no significant difference between 360° and animated VR for assessing/training decisions; of the studies testing real-game transfer: Pagé et al. (2019) reported gains applying to real-game scenarios, while Panchuk et al. (2018) found immersive-test gains with **no significant change in real-match decisions**; Kittel et al. (2020) found neither 360° VR nor broadcast-video groups improved significantly. 10 of 20 studies used verbal responses, 6 used motion-tracked physical responses, 2 used button presses. — [Jia et al. 2024 (PMC11284098)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)
- [IND] Faure, Limballe, Bideau & Kulpa (2020), *Journal of Sports Sciences* 38(2):192-205: scoping review of 30 articles on VR in team ball sports; VR useful for assessing/training because situations can be controlled and standardized and subskills isolated; existing studies limited mainly by technical issues and study design. (Abstract only.) — [PubMed 31724487](https://www.pubmed.gov/31724487/)
- [IND] Neumann et al. (2018), *Virtual Reality* 22(3):183-198: systematic review of 20 interactive-VR sport articles; VR improved performance, physiological and psychological outcomes; most research on endurance sports (running, cycling, rowing), skill sports under-studied; moderators: presence of others, competitiveness, task autonomy, immersion, attentional focus, feedback. (Abstract only.) — [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/37949/); [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/25a2bc11-d7a6-590f-bab3-ab8622679ec8)
- [IND] Richlan et al. (2023), *Frontiers in Psychology*, narrative review "Virtual training, real effects": aggregated N = 493 across novice, amateur and expert athletes; discusses real-world transfer and generalizability as open issues. — [Richlan et al. 2023 (PMC10622803)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10622803/)
- [IND] Le Noury, Polman, Maloney & Gorman (2022), *Sports Medicine* 52:1473-1489: narrative review of XR; uses ecological dynamics to inform design/assessment; "many unknowns regarding the effectiveness of XR technology" limit adoption. — [PubMed 35286617](https://www.pubmed.gov/35286617/)

### Inferences
- For hockey, the honest state of evidence (as of Nov 2026) is "plausible, unproven": in-VR and lab-test gains exist (Bloechle 2025; Heilmann & Schubert 2025), but no study I found links hockey VR training to on-ice or game outcomes. The vendor's flagship numbers ("over 10%" save %, "10-15%") have no traceable publication.
- The one convincing VR-to-real transfer study (Gray 2017) succeeded with an adaptive, variable, perception-heavy design, and plain "repeat the real task in VR" practice **did not** transfer — the active ingredient was the training method, not the headset.
- The Fribourg LEA study suggests a hockey-specific niche where VR can do something real practice cannot: show the puck's-eye view of the open net as feedback. Feedback, not repetition, drove the gain (no-feedback practice: no change).
- Expect real-world effects to be smaller than in-VR effects; meta-analytic prediction intervals that cross zero mean a given new product could show no benefit.

### Gaps
- No published outcomes found for the UWSP/Charles University Sense Arena trial (2019) or the Charles University "10-15%" claim; the Perič-linked work appears only in theses.
- No full text read for Faure 2020, Neumann 2018, the 2025 IRSEP scoping review, or Le Noury 2022 (paywalled/blocked); their detailed tallies are not reported here.
- No NHL/AHL/NCAA team trial with controlled on-ice outcome data was found; team usage reports are testimonials.
- Mitroff 2013 primary paper not read; the 18% figure and n = 11 come from press coverage.
- No goalie-specific VR training RCT found.

## 2. NeuroTracker / 3D multiple-object tracking (3D-MOT): claimed vs. independently replicated effects

### Takeaway
3D-MOT training reliably improves 3D-MOT scores (near transfer), but far transfer to sport is weak: the flagship positive soccer study (Romeas et al. 2016; ~7 trained players, ~15% passing-accuracy gain) **failed to replicate** in a larger randomized follow-up by the same group (n = 62, 2025), and independent reviews (Vater et al. 2021; Harris et al. 2018) found almost no evidence of transfer to sporting tasks. In ice hockey, NeuroTracker baselines do not predict draft rank or game stats, and no controlled on-ice training study has been published.

### Cited Findings
- [IND-LINKED] Romeas, Guldner & Faubert (2016), *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 22:1-9: 23 university soccer players randomized to 3D-MOT (30 NeuroTracker Core sessions over 5 weeks), active control (30 sessions of 3D soccer videos) or passive control. Passing decision accuracy (but not dribbling or shooting) improved ~15% more in the 3D-MOT group in standardized small-sided games; self-rated confidence tracked it; authors claimed the first evidence that a non-contextual perceptual-cognitive exercise transfers to on-field performance. Faubert is the NeuroTracker developer. — [IAT/LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4045423?lng=de); [NeuroTracker summary](https://neurotrackerx.com/post/3d-multiple-object-tracking-training-task-improves-passing-decision-making-accuracy-in-soccer-players)
- [IND] Vater, Gray & Holcombe's re-reading of Romeas 2016: intervention group n = 7; active and passive controls (n = 7 each) collapsed post hoc into one control group (n = 12) after two injury exclusions; training described as ten sessions, two per week; decision accuracy rated by a single blinded coach; no effect sizes reported; needs replication. (The "30 sessions" vs "10 sessions" difference likely reflects 3 Core runs per training day x 10 days — my inference, not stated.) — [Vater et al. 2021 (PMC8500884)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500884/)
- [IND-LINKED, null] Romeas, Goujat, Faubert & Labbé (2025), *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 76:102770: 62 male professional-academy soccer players randomized to dual-task 3D-MOT (n = 30) or control (n = 32). 3D-MOT scores improved more in the trained group (p < .001), but **no group differences on an attention test or on-field small-sided-game performance (ps > .05)**; no link between training or cognitive scores and early career success. The authors state it did not replicate the earlier findings. — [ÉTS publication page](https://pure.etsmtl.ca/fr/publications/no-transfer-of-3d-multiple-object-tracking-training-on-game-perfo/)
- [IND] Vater, Gray & Holcombe (2021), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 28(5):1458-1483: 29 papers included; of 16 intervention studies only 10 met minimum criteria (non-NeuroTracker control + transfer test); **only 3 tested far transfer to real-world tasks, and 2 were null** (volleyball, Fleddermann et al. 2019; driving, Harris et al. 2020); no preregistered studies; small samples (often 5-8 per group); uncorrected multiple comparisons; unfair or passive controls; expectation effects; Faubert "a co-author on almost every published Neurotracker study"; the manufacturer did not answer a request for unpublished data. They argue the abilities underlying object tracking (a tradeoff between number of targets and speed, partly hemifield-specific) "are not those advertised by the Neurotracker manufacturers." — [Vater et al. 2021 (PMC8500884)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500884/); [Psychonomic Society summary](https://featuredcontent.psychonomic.org/neurotracker-sport-performance); [Bern repository](https://boris.unibe.ch/155847)
- [IND] Harris, Wilson & Vine (2018), *Frontiers in Psychology*: systematic review of commercial cognitive-training devices (incl. NeuroTracker); 43 studies; 17 tested transfer beyond lab cognitive tests, **only 1 tested transfer to a sporting task**; "limited support for far transfer benefits... to sporting tasks"; flagged small samples, no retention tests, little replication by researchers independent of the product, and much research by company-linked researchers. — [Harris et al. 2018 (PMC5958310)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5958310/)
- [IND] Simons, Boot, Charness et al. (2016), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 17(3): evaluated all peer-reviewed intervention studies cited by leading brain-training companies; extensive evidence of improvement on trained tasks, less for closely related tasks, "little evidence" for distantly related tasks and "very weak evidence" for everyday cognitive performance; called for better control groups. — [Univ. of Illinois news](https://news.illinois.edu/review-finds-little-evidence-that-brain-training-games-yield-real-world-benefits/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/10/161003113427.htm)
- [IND] Hockey talent ID (2024): 35 male elite players aged 16-20; NeuroTracker baseline did not relate to draft rank, games played, points per game or on-ice goal differential, though it correlated with working-memory and attention tests; "not specific enough to allow talent identification among same-level elite athletes in ice hockey." — [VU Amsterdam research record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/about-the-predictive-value-of-a-3d-multiple-object-tracking-devic/)
- [IND] University varsity study (2025, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*): 48 athletes (male hockey, female volleyball); preseason NeuroTracker baselines not associated with season statistics, except negative correlations with penalty minutes in male hockey (r = -.40; per game r = -.39); "Neurotracker performance is not associated with sports performance." — [Frontiers 2025 (fspor.2025.1608463)](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1608463/pdf)
- [IND] In a 136-athlete varsity sample, male athletes and hockey players had higher NeuroTracker scores than counterparts (a group difference, not a training effect). — [Frontiers 2024 (fspor.2024.1372350)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1372350/full)
- [IND] A Mitacs-funded project is testing whether perceptual-cognitive (NeuroTracker) training improves performance in major-junior hockey players; no published results found. — [Mitacs project page](https://www.mitacs.ca/our-projects/efficacy-of-a-perceptive-cognitive-training-to-improve-ice-hockey-performance/)

### Inferences
- A generic MOT drill (as included in Sense Arena's drill set per Heilmann & Schubert 2025) is the weakest-evidenced component a hockey trainer could build; it will raise MOT scores, which risks "test learning" being sold as hockey improvement.
- The most informative signal is the developer's own group failing to replicate with ~3x the sample — the classic pattern of a small early positive study shrinking under a larger trial.
- If MOT is kept, it should be justified as a warm-up or engagement feature, not as the core transfer mechanism, and embedded in hockey-specific scenes (where the tracked "objects" are players and the decision is a pass/shot).

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for Romeas 2016 were not reported in the sources I read; the exact group sizes are inconsistent between the abstract (23 randomized to 3 groups) and Vater's reading (7 vs 12 analyzed).
- Full NeuroTracker-in-hockey training data (Mitacs project) unpublished; NeuroTracker company research pages were not reviewed directly.
- I did not verify whether Simons et al. 2016 discussed NeuroTracker specifically.

## 3. Video-based / occlusion / 360° decision-making training: transfer findings and effect sizes

### Takeaway
Video-based perceptual-cognitive training has the best-quantified transfer evidence: a 2024 meta-analysis (Zhu et al., 22 studies) found large lab gains (g = 1.51) that roughly halve on-court (g = 0.65), and a 2024 meta-analysis of temporal-occlusion anticipation training (Müller et al., 12 studies, N = 248) found d = 1.21 overall with d = 0.85 on field-based transfer tests. Both literatures are small, heterogeneous and at risk of publication bias, and 360° video studies with umpires/referees often show no transfer.

### Cited Findings
- [IND] Zhu et al. (2024), *Behavioral Sciences* 14(10):919, PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis of perceptual-cognitive training in team sports: 22 studies (14 RCTs, 8 non-randomized), 45 effect sizes. **Task-specific response accuracy g = 1.51 [0.98, 2.05] (17 studies, n = 381, I² = 78.7%)** vs **on-court transfer accuracy g = 0.65 [0.15, 1.16] (13 studies, n = 247, I² = 71.4%)**; task-specific response time g = -0.91 [-1.45, -0.38] vs on-court RT g = -0.44 [-0.81, -0.06]. Authors: transfer "effects were limited." Only 2 of 14 RCTs adequately described randomization/allocation concealment; 7 lacked double-blinding; authors state there was publication bias (funnel asymmetry; trim-and-fill did not change CIs); only 7 studies had retention tests. Some reporting errors (frequency subgroup values duplicate session-length values; RoB counts inconsistent). — [Zhu et al. 2024 (PMC11505547)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/)
- [IND] Müller, Morris-Binelli, Hambrick & Macnamara (2024), *Sports Medicine* 54(10):2597-2606: meta-analysis of video temporal-occlusion anticipation training; 12 studies, 25 effect sizes, N = 248 (107 highly skilled); pooled transfer d = 1.21 [0.83, 1.59], I² = 65%; video-test transfer d = 1.26 [0.80, 1.72] vs **field-test transfer d = 0.85 [0.40, 1.30]** (difference n.s., p = .21); training used a non-occluded replay after each prediction as feedback; skilled athletes also improved. Publication bias inconclusive (Egger p = .018 one-tailed; trim-and-fill imputed none). — [Müller et al. 2024 (PMC11467115)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11467115)
- [IND] Morris-Binelli, Müller et al. (2021), *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*: Australian international/national male field-hockey goalkeepers facing penalty-corner drag-flicks; trained goalkeepers (temporal occlusion) improved across video, field and competition assessments, controls did not; video-test gains retained at 6 months; improvement was individualized and non-linear. — [Murdoch research portal](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Individual-differences-in-performance-and-learning/991005544098607891); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1469029220308141)
- [IND] Female field-hockey goalkeepers (2021/22, *Optometry and Vision Science*): transfer of anticipation across opponent sex was partial — none transferred for tight runs; five could for loose runs. — [Notre Dame Australia record](https://researchonline.nd.edu.au/physiotherapy_article/202)
- [IND] Kittel, Larkin, Elsworthy & Spittle (2019), *J Sci Med Sport*: in Australian football umpires, 360° VR and broadcast video tests had similar reliability/construct validity; 360° VR judged more specific to in-game decisions; participants rated 360° VR more relevant and enjoyable. — [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/38375/); [Kittel thesis](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/42042/)
- [IND] Kittel et al. (2020) training RCT (amateur Australian football umpires, N = 32, 360° VR vs broadcast video): neither group improved significantly; Kittel et al. (2020/21) transfer study (elite umpires, n = 21, two 25-clip tests) — the Jia et al. review summarizes that neither video test distinguished elite referees. Full texts not accessed. — [Jia et al. 2024 (PMC11284098)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/); [VU record of transfer study](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/41586/)
- [IND] Van Maarseveen et al. (2018) (via Jia review): perceptual-cognitive test scores do not reliably predict on-field performance. — [Jia et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)
- [IND] Larkin et al. (2015), *International Journal of Sport Psychology* 46(6):555-586: systematic review; video-based training can enhance perceptual-cognitive performance; calls for better validity/reliability of measures; studies vary in skill level and amount of video presented. — [IJSP abstract](https://ijsp-online.com/abstract/view/46/555)
- [IND] Zentgraf, Heppe & Fleddermann (2017), *German Journal of Exercise and Sport Research*: review of 16 off-court training studies in interactive sports; transfer (near, further or far) "mostly not studied empirically"; only limited, weak evidence for intermediate and far transfer. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12662-017-0441-8)
- [IND] Broadbent, Causer, Williams & Ford (2015), *European Journal of Sport Science*: transfer of retained perceptual-cognitive training to field performance is the key area for future research. — [SPONET record](https://sponet.de/Record/4034617)

### Inferences
- Rule of thumb for a designer: **expect on-ice effects to be roughly half (or less) of what your in-app test shows** (Zhu: 1.51 -> 0.65; Müller: 1.26 -> 0.85).
- Occlusion-style anticipation training with immediate "what actually happened" feedback is the most consistently supported paradigm and maps directly onto hockey problems (goalie reading release, defender reading pass intent, shooter reading the goalie).
- 360° video alone (passive, verbal response) has a weak transfer record; its advantage is realism and ease of capture, not proven learning.

### Gaps
- No ice-hockey occlusion or video-training transfer study (2010+) was found in this pass; hockey-specific evidence relies on field-hockey goalkeeper analogues.
- Pagé et al. (2019) and Panchuk et al. (2018) full details (n, effect sizes) not read; only the Jia review's summary.
- Classic transfer studies (e.g., Williams et al. 2002 tennis; Gabbett et al. softball; Lorains et al. above-real-time video) and the Hadlow et al. (2018) near/far transfer classification framework were not verified in this pass.

## 4. Design features that predict transfer

### Takeaway
The features with the most support are: **sport-specific, representative stimuli; action-coupled responses (move the stick/body rather than press a button or speak); explicit feedback showing the correct outcome; adaptive difficulty and practice variability; and more than ~4 weeks of training at 2-4 sessions/week of ~10-30 min**. Most moderator comparisons are underpowered and non-significant, so these are direction-of-evidence calls, not established laws.

### Cited Findings
- [IND] Zhu et al. (2024) on-court transfer moderators (none significantly different between subgroups): stimulus — computer g = 0.19 [-0.56, 0.94], life-size video g = 0.46 [-0.08, 1.00], **3D/VR g = 0.96 [0.02, 1.89]** (3D/VR category includes 3D-MOT); response — verbal/keyboard g = 0.41 [0.07, 0.75], **sport-specific action g = 0.87 [-0.22, 1.96]**; duration — ≤4 weeks g = 0.38 [0.01, 0.74], **>4 weeks g = 1.25 [-0.16, 2.65]**; session length <20 min g = 0.56, >20 min g = 0.72; age — adults g = 0.63 vs adolescents g = 0.58. Authors' recommended prescription: first-person VR video, sport-specific responses, >4 weeks, 2-4 sessions/week, 10-25 min/session. Explicit vs implicit instruction not meta-analyzed. — [Zhu et al. 2024 (PMC11505547)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/)
- [IND] Gray (2017): adaptive training (one-up-one-down staircases per pitch type, ±2 mph steps; location variability grown from ±5 cm up to ±25 cm; interleaved pitch types; switched pitcher handedness late) tied to the challenge-point hypothesis and practice variability produced transfer; **non-adaptive VR batting practice did not** transfer to real hits; Gray notes higher fidelity is not shown to improve training. — [Gray 2017 (PMC5733365)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5733365)
- [IND] Gray (as cited by the Exeter group): for training, psychological fidelity matters more than physical fidelity. — [Harris et al. 2021, Virtual Reality (PDF)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/166505/13/Harris2021_Article_ExploringSensorimotorPerforman.pdf)
- [IND] Bloechle et al. (2025): with identical VR exposure (54 trials), the feedback group improved ~12 points and the no-feedback group did not change; feedback showed the puck's viewpoint of the open net — i.e., information unavailable in real practice. — [Bloechle et al. 2025 (PMC11993527)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11993527)
- [IND] Müller et al. (2024): occlusion training with replay feedback after each prediction transfers to field tests (d = 0.85). — [Müller et al. 2024 (PMC11467115)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11467115)
- [IND] Jia et al. (2024): recommends athletes make decisions "while simultaneously executing corresponding actions" (motion-captured responses) to improve transfer; notes Wirth et al. (2018) found action responses outperformed verbal ones; recommends computer-generated (animated) content to build more extensive scenario libraries (360° filming is slow), eye-tracking integration, and richer feedback on alternative solutions for high-level athletes; 360° vs animated VR showed no significant difference in effectiveness. — [Jia et al. 2024 (PMC11284098)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)
- [IND] Neumann et al. (2018): presence of others, competitiveness, task autonomy, immersion, attentional focus and feedback are important variables in interactive VR sport outcomes. — [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/37949/)
- [IND] Le Noury et al. (2022): ecological dynamics (representative design) proposed as the framework for designing and assessing XR training. — [PubMed 35286617](https://www.pubmed.gov/35286617/)
- [IND] Immersive motor competence meta-analysis (2026): complex skills benefited more; typical effective programs ran 4-12 weeks. — [Scientific Reports 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-42962-6)
- [IND] Near vs far transfer pattern across MOT and brain training: trained-task gains are reliable; distantly related (sport) tasks show little benefit (Simons 2016; Harris 2018; Vater 2021; Romeas 2025). — [Vater et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500884/); [Harris et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5958310/); [ÉTS: Romeas et al. 2025](https://pure.etsmtl.ca/fr/publications/no-transfer-of-3d-multiple-object-tracking-training-on-game-perfo/)
- [IND] Individualized, non-linear learning curves in skilled goalkeepers suggest per-athlete adaptation and tracking matter. — [Murdoch portal (Morris-Binelli et al. 2021)](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Individual-differences-in-performance-and-learning/991005544098607891)
- [VENDOR] Sense Arena's recommended dose (2-3 sessions/week, 20-30 min) is in line with the Zhu et al. prescription, but Sense Arena's FAQ also promotes use as a pre-game "warm up to stimulate the brain," a different (unstudied) use case. — [Sense Arena FAQ](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/faqs)

### Inferences
- A defensible design brief for a hockey-sense trainer: (1) hockey game situations rendered from the player's real viewpoint, with realistic opponents/teammates; (2) responses made with the stick and body (pass/shoot direction, body orientation), under game-like time pressure; (3) outcome feedback after each rep, ideally showing what the player could not see live (e.g., the puck's-eye view of the net, the open lane from above, the alternative pass); (4) adaptive difficulty per skill and high scenario variability (handedness, speeds, systems); (5) a 6-12 week program, 2-4 x/week, 10-30 min; (6) built-in transfer measures that are not the training task (video-of-real-game decisions, on-ice drills, coach-rated game decisions).
- Building scenarios procedurally (animated VR) looks better than filming 360° video for a scalable library, since effectiveness appears similar and variability is easier to generate.
- Psychological fidelity (the right information and the right decision under time pressure) seems to matter more than photorealism, so engineering effort should go to scenario realism and timing before graphics.

### Gaps
- No study isolates feedback type (outcome vs. corrective vs. gaze-guided) in hockey VR.
- Spacing/dose-response and retention beyond a few weeks are rarely measured (Zhu: 7 studies with retention).
- No direct comparison of stick-controller vs hand-controller vs button responses for hockey was found.

## 5. Skeptic and independent-reviewer critiques; known problems of VR for sport

### Takeaway
Independent reviewers consistently flag: tiny samples, few RCTs with proper allocation concealment, inadequate or passive controls, no preregistration, single raters, rare retention tests, publication bias, heavy involvement of product developers (NeuroTracker especially), and outcomes that are the trained task ("test learning") rather than performance. VR-specific risks include degraded depth cues and missing haptics that may change how vision guides action (bad for interceptive skills), latency, cybersickness (worse standing than seated), and — for hockey — no skating, no puck feel and no contact.

### Cited Findings
- [IND] Zhu et al. (2024): only 2 of 14 RCTs adequately described randomization and allocation concealment; 7 without double-blinding; 4 with unexplained high dropout; 3 with selective outcome reporting; authors state publication bias was present; about half the studies had placebo groups; English-only inclusion. — [Zhu et al. 2024 (PMC11505547)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/)
- [IND] Vater et al. (2021): no preregistration; groups of 5-8; uncorrected multiple comparisons; post hoc collapsing of control groups; single rater; expectation effects (trained group likely expected more); developer co-authorship on almost every study; unanswered request for unpublished studies (file-drawer concern). — [Vater et al. 2021 (PMC8500884)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500884/)
- [IND] Harris et al. (2018): limited replication by researchers independent of commercial products; much research by company-linked researchers; little testing in athletes; only 1 of 43 studies tested a sporting task. — [Harris et al. 2018 (PMC5958310)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5958310/)
- [IND] Simons et al. (2016): brain-training evidence base (studies cited by companies) shows trained-task gains but weak transfer; calls for better control groups. — [Univ. of Illinois news](https://news.illinois.edu/review-finds-little-evidence-that-brain-training-games-yield-real-world-benefits/)
- [IND] "Test learning" in hockey: Heilmann & Schubert (2025) found large within-group time effects in both groups but no significant between-group effects, with an outcome test (stick-button Flanker) close to the trained format and no on-ice measure; the paper also contains internal statistical inconsistencies. — [Heilmann & Schubert 2025 (PMC12611849)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12611849/)
- [IND] Racket VR meta-analysis (2026): prediction interval [-0.01, 1.57] crosses zero; transfer testing insufficient. — [PLOS ONE 2026](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0345541)
- [IND] Harris, Buckingham, Wilson & Vine (2019), *Experimental Brain Research*: VR's weaker binocular depth cues, conflicting depth information and limited haptic feedback may push users away from the dorsal-stream ("vision for action") online control mode; if so, VR skills may be "unrepresentative of the real-world, and transfer of training will be compromised." — [Harris et al. 2019 (PMC6794235)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6794235)
- [IND] Harris et al. (2020): moving from VR to real putting temporarily disrupted gaze control and accuracy; perceptual deficiencies limit VR use. — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/160944)
- [IND] Gray (2017) listed his VR's low fidelity (smaller field of view, no binocular cues, no simulated bat) yet still got transfer — fidelity limits are not automatically fatal when the training design is right. — [Gray 2017 (PMC5733365)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5733365)
- [IND] Stauffert et al. (2020), *Frontiers in Virtual Reality*: high motion-to-photon latency can reduce performance in interactive graphics and provoke cybersickness. — [Frontiers in VR 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/frvir.2020.582204)
- [IND] Ang & Quarles (2023), *Frontiers in Virtual Reality*: review of 89 papers on cybersickness reduction in HMDs; individual susceptibility emphasized. — [Frontiers in VR 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/virtual-reality/articles/10.3389/frvir.2023.1027552/pdf)
- [IND, low-quality source] An athlete treadmill-VR study (Atlantis Press) reports cybersickness was more common when standing than seated, and that athletes using HMDs for tactical/observational training seated had fewer issues; used SSQ > 33.3 as "high." Publication details unconfirmed. — [Atlantis Press PDF](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/126013021.pdf)
- [IND] Jia et al. (2024): 360° video allows no interaction; animated VR struggles to simulate objects realistically; CAVEs are costly; low task complexity and limited feedback may explain null results. — [Jia et al. 2024 (PMC11284098)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)
- [PRESS, critical] Hockey-specific limitations of Sense Arena: no skating (stepping instead), no physical puck/rebound feedback, athletes know it isn't real, adoption not visible despite NHL/PWHL sponsorship. — [University of Rochester Libraries](https://www.library.rochester.edu/about/news/sense-arenas-vr-hockey-training-works-why-isnt-it-widespread)
- [IND] Polikanova et al. (2022) note long equipment/sensor setup time as a practical barrier, and that VR skill transfer is not yet consistently demonstrated. — [Polikanova et al. 2022 (PMC9414154)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9414154)
- [IND] NeuroTracker scores fail as hockey talent predictors (35 elite juniors; 48 varsity athletes), a warning against using a generic cognitive score as the trainer's success metric. — [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/about-the-predictive-value-of-a-3d-multiple-object-tracking-devic/); [Frontiers 2025](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1608463/pdf)

### Inferences
- VR is better suited to training **what to look at and what to decide** (perception and decision) than to the fine timing of interceptive actions (stickhandling feel, one-timer contact, goalie rebound control), where degraded depth cues and absent haptics matter most. A hockey-sense trainer should lean on reads, scanning, lane/net selection and anticipation, and treat motor execution as secondary.
- Seated or low-locomotion modes are probably safer for cybersickness, but they reduce perception-action coupling; a standing, stick-in-hand, small-footprint mode with no artificial locomotion is a reasonable compromise (inference, not tested in hockey).
- To avoid the field's credibility problems, the product's own validation should use preregistered, randomized, active-control designs with an outcome that is not the trained task (e.g., coach-blinded decision coding of game video or on-ice small-area games) and a retention test.

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative cybersickness prevalence data for athletes using hockey or sport-training VR, and no measurements of Quest-class latency effects on sport decisions.
- No independent audit of Sense Arena's internal "diagnostic" metrics or their relationship to on-ice performance was found.
- Funding/affiliation of the 2026 racket and motor-competence meta-analyses and the 2025 IRSEP scoping review was not checked (full texts not read).
