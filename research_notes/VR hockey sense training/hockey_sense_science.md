# The Sports Science of "Hockey Sense" (Hockey IQ): Perceptual-Cognitive Skills and Expert-Novice Differences

Notes for designing a VR hockey-sense trainer for an adult recreational defenceman.

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[ICE HOCKEY]** means the study used ice hockey players or ice hockey tasks.
- **[SOCCER]**, **[FIELD HOCKEY]**, **[TENNIS]** or **[MULTI-SPORT]** mean the evidence is borrowed from another sport and has to be carried over to ice hockey with care.

Sample sizes, designs and effect sizes are given wherever the source reported them. Some sources could only be read as an abstract, a press release or a search snippet, and those are marked. A 2026 date on a paper means it is very recent; several of the newest ice-hockey studies only appeared in 2025-2026.

---

## Q1. How do researchers define and break down "game sense" / hockey IQ?

### Takeaway
No single agreed definition exists. Researchers treat "hockey sense" as a bundle of perceptual-cognitive skills: visual exploration (scanning, which in hockey also means shoulder checks), anticipation from advance cues, recognition of familiar game patterns, and fast, accurate decisions. Some add domain-general executive functions such as inhibition, working memory and switching. The newest ice-hockey work measures "game sense" as scouts' or coaches' ratings and then checks which objective tests (anticipation, eye movements, VR hockey, cognitive batteries) predict those ratings.

### Cited Findings

**How hockey researchers operationalise it [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Fortin-Guichard et al. (2025, *Journal of Sports Sciences*, "Beyond the trained eye: An objective method to predict game sense in team sports", DOI 10.1080/02640414.2025.2491976):
  - Sample: 40 elite male hockey players, about 15-16 years old at recruitment. Five hockey scouts rated each player about once a week for a year.
  - Approach: scout judgments of game sense were combined with objective tests of anticipation and eye movements, a VR hockey test, and the Creyos cognitive battery (memory, attention, deductive reasoning).
  - The authors say long-term follow-up is still needed to confirm predictive accuracy.
  - Source: [McGill Newsroom, 7 May 2025](https://www.mcgill.ca/newsroom/node/317174). The press release was summarised through search because the page returned HTTP 403, and I did not read the paper's full text.
- Tétreault, Fortin-Guichard & Grondin (2025, *International Journal of Sports Science & Coaching*, 20(2), 724-741):
  - Sample: 88 youth elite forwards and defencemen who were eligible for a Major Junior draft.
  - Tests at age 15: perceptual-cognitive skills (decision-making and anticipation, eye-tracking, a temporal occlusion task) plus other psychological characteristics. Future performance was tracked for 4 years.
  - The abstract gives no effect sizes.
  - Source: [doi:10.1177/17479541241304360](https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541241304360), abstract via Europe PMC.
- A 2023 *Journal of Sports Sciences* paper by the same group used psychological characteristics to identify "sleeping" (late-emerging) talent in junior elite ice hockey — [Fortin-Guichard author page, McGill](https://www.mcgill.ca/education/daniel-fortin-guichard). Details were not read.
- Magnaguagno & Beck (2025, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*) interviewed top Swiss players from five sports, including ice hockey, about decision-making research. Themes were gaze anchors, anticipation, and intuitive "if-then" decisions. This is qualitative work with no numbers. Source: [doi:10.3389/fspor.2025.1653834](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1653834).

**Gaze strategy and "quiet eye" [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Martell & Vickers (2004, *Human Movement Science* 22(6):689-712) described how elite players see the defensive game:
  - Expertise is a cascade of fixation/tracking: brief early fixations or tracking of specific locations, ending in one long fixation on a relatively stable target.
  - Source: [SPONET abstract record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4008469?lng=en).
- Vickers' quiet eye (QE) is "the final fixation or tracking gaze that is located on a specific location or object in the task space prior to the onset of a critical movement." An early onset is read as better anticipation, and a longer duration as better focus. Source: [Vickers et al., look-up-line paper, LJMU accepted manuscript](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4081/3/Effect%20of%20the%20Look-Up%20Line%20on%20the%20Gaze%2C%20Head%20Orientation%20and.pdf).

**Recognition-primed decisions [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Mulligan, McCracken & Hodges (2012, *International Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 10(3):198-210):
  - Expert hockey players labelled in-game decision situations as "familiar" about twice as often as non-experts.
  - Familiar decisions were rated as better quality only when the decision was based on recognising the event.
  - Source: [UBC Motor Skills Lab](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/node/160).

**Scanning as a component [ICE HOCKEY / SOCCER]**
- Definition from soccer: a scan is "an active head movement where the face (and hence, the eyes) is temporarily directed away from the ball" to gather information about teammates and opponents before engaging with the ball. Source: [Jordet et al. 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7573254). [SOCCER]
- A 2025 TUM colloquium abstract stated that only one study had examined scanning in ice hockey (Berg et al.). Source: [TUM colloquium announcement, 6 Nov 2025](https://www.hs.mh.tum.de/en/bewegungswissenschaft/news-single-view-en/article/einladung-zum-kolloquium-sensomotorische-kontrolle-06112025-1715-uhr-am-olympiacampus-11-seminarraum-s6/). [ICE HOCKEY]

**Executive functions [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Wang, Yang & Shi (2026, *Frontiers in Psychology*) studied 132 Chinese youth players:
  - 65 experts (mean age 15.8, 6.7 years of training) and 67 novices (mean age 16.2, 3.2 years).
  - Experts responded faster on Flanker congruent (Z = -2.681, p = .007) and incongruent trials (Z = -1.998, p = .046), on the 2-back working-memory task (Z = -2.378, p = .017), and on size-parity shifting (Z = -2.548, p = .011).
  - Accuracy did not differ between groups.
  - Years of training correlated with faster response times: inhibition r = -0.450 (congruent) and -0.267 (incongruent), updating r = -0.257, shifting r = -0.185.
  - Source: [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1670150](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1670150).
- Wang et al. (2026, *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation*) compared 199 adolescents (92 ice hockey, 39 figure skating, 68 speed skating):
  - Hockey players were faster than speed skaters on Flanker congruent (β = -90.03, p = .012; units not stated in the abstract), incongruent (β = -83.38, p = .010) and shifting (β = -170.49, p = .004).
  - Hockey players were also more accurate (β = 5.75, p = .018).
  - Source: [doi:10.1186/s13102-026-02004-0](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-02004-0).

**What separates skill groups at all [SOCCER, youth]**
- Ward & Williams (2003, *Journal of Sport & Exercise Psychology* 25:93-111) tested elite and sub-elite youth soccer players aged 9-17 on visual, perceptual and cognitive measures.
  - Anticipation and the use of situational probabilities best separated the skill groups.
  - Basic visual-function tests did not consistently separate them at any age.
  - Recall of structured patterns of play was the strongest predictor of age.
  - Source: [UCF STARS record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/2149).

### Inferences
- A working decomposition for a trainer has five parts:
  1. Exploration: scanning and head turns away from the puck.
  2. Cue pick-up and anticipation: reading the puck carrier's body, stick and skating.
  3. Pattern recognition and familiarity: recognising rush structures.
  4. Decision selection and timing: when to step up, angle or retreat.
  5. Gaze control at the moment of action: a "quick then quiet" sequence ending in a stable quiet-eye fixation.
- Executive functions are probably a supporting layer rather than the core of hockey sense. Expert-novice differences there show up as speed rather than accuracy, and generic cognitive training transfers poorly (see Q4).
- "Hockey sense" is still validated mostly against coach and scout ratings, so there is no gold-standard objective measure yet. Any trainer metric is a proxy.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed construct-validation paper that decomposes "hockey sense" into factors, for example with factor analysis. The Fortin-Guichard 2025 full text, including which tests carried the prediction and how much variance they explained, was not accessible (HTTP 403 and no open abstract).
- I found no Hockey Canada sport-science document that defines hockey sense; searches turned up only USA Hockey coaching articles.
- I found no ice-hockey study of pattern recall (the classic recall paradigm of structured versus unstructured game situations). The pattern-recall evidence here is from soccer (Ward & Williams 2003).
- I found no ice-hockey multiple-object-tracking study that measures on-ice outcomes, apart from Acquin et al. 2025 in Q2.

## Q2. Measured expert vs. novice differences in ice hockey (with numbers)

### Takeaway
Ice-hockey evidence is thin but growing. Compared with less skilled players, elite players:
- fixate tactical locations faster and then hold a long final fixation ("quick then quiet");
- produce QE fixations of about 1.1 s before 1-v-1 contact;
- benefit measurably from scanning before receiving a pass (Swedish pros, β = 0.12);
- show short scan fixations (about 135 ms) in junior elite players;
- anticipate more accurately and faster in occlusion tasks (one fNIRS preprint: 77% vs 51%, 812 ms vs 1074 ms).

Meta-analyses across sports confirm that experts are more accurate and faster, use fewer but longer fixations, and have longer quiet-eye periods. Expertise effects are larger when the response is a real sport action.

### Cited Findings

**Defensive gaze, elite vs. near-elite [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Martell & Vickers (2004), live defensive-zone task with Team Canada players. Source: [SPONET abstract](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4008469?lng=en).
  - Early fixation/tracking durations were significantly shorter than the final fixation/tracking.
  - On successful plays, the elite group fixated tactical locations more rapidly than the non-elite group.
  - The final fixation/tracking before movement was significantly longer in both groups. It averaged about 30% of the final part of the phase and happened as the athlete isolated one object or location to end the play.
- The companion conference abstract (Vickers & Martell, AAHPERD 2004) compared elite players with teammates and other players known for tactical errors in games. Source: [AAHPERD 2004 abstract](https://aahperd.confex.com/aahperd/2004/finalprogram/paper_5579.htm).
  - The groups did not differ in how fast they could move their gaze around the ice.
  - The elite players showed a "quick then quiet" cascade: ultra-short tracking fixations on opponents' movements, then a long QE fixation isolating one moving target. The authors propose this lets elite players "pick up time" early in the play.
- Sample sizes and millisecond values for this study were not available in the abstracts I accessed.

**1-v-1 offence vs. defence gaze, elite university players [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Vickers et al. (2017, *European Journal of Sport Science*; accepted manuscript read in full). Source: [LJMU manuscript](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4081/3/Effect%20of%20the%20Look-Up%20Line%20on%20the%20Gaze%2C%20Head%20Orientation%20and.pdf); publication record at [British Library](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100068448016_0x000001).
  - Sample: 18 elite male university players, mean age 23.2 (SD 1.7), 12.2 years of organised hockey.
  - Task: 1-v-1 checking drill toward the boards with mobile eye-trackers, 482 trials analysed.
  - Quiet eye: one per trial, defined as the final fixation before contact. Mean QE was 1117 ms (14.9% of the trial) on one rink and 1125 ms (15.4%) on the other.
  - Trials lasted about 7.35-7.65 s and contained about 7.5-7.9 fixation/tracking gazes, with no difference by rink.
  - Gaze locations coded: puck/stick, opponent, near ice, far ice, the warning line by the boards, net, boards.
  - Near-ice fixations "usually occurred when the athletes were having a problem controlling the puck on the stick."

**Scanning before receiving the puck [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Berg et al. (2025, *Journal of Sports Sciences* 43(2):145-150), "Scanning is associated with better performance in professional ice hockey". Source: [SPONET record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4092070?lng=en), DOI 10.1080/02640414.2024.2433899.
  - Sample: 88 players (43 men in the SHL, 45 women in the SDHL), 22 filmed matches, 2,545 actions.
  - Scanning before a pass reception was associated with a higher probability of a successful next action: β = 0.12, 95% CI [0.07, 0.17].
  - Neither gender nor position moderated the effect.
  - The authors recommend teaching scanning with concrete methods so players learn to scan before receiving the puck.
  - The abstract does not report scan frequency or raw probabilities.
- Bespomoshchnov, Lemoyne, Jordet & Vater (2026, *Journal of Sports Sciences*, online ahead of print), "Scanning in junior elite ice hockey players: in-situ analysis of gaze behaviour". Source: [doi:10.1080/02640414.2026.2744018](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2026.2744018), PMID 42860766, abstract via Europe PMC.
  - Sample: 16 USA Hockey National Team Development Program players (mean age 16.5) wearing mobile eye-trackers in scrimmages.
  - 58.1% of scans contained only one fixation between "puck anchors" (looks back at the puck).
  - Mean fixation duration was 134.52 ms.
  - Forwards directed 47-53% of their first fixations to open space.
  - Scans before puck reception were shorter and less complex than scans made when not in possession.
- A Western University thesis found that hockey players' heads dropped toward the ice as the number of players increased, and that looking down at the ice impairs what players can see. Source: [Western University thesis, ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8528](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8528). Read via search summary only.

**Soccer scanning benchmarks, for calibration only [SOCCER]**
- Jordet et al. (2020, *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:553813). Source: [PMC7573254](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7573254).
  - Sample: 27 players from one English Premier League club, 21 matches, 9,574 possessions.
  - Method: scans counted in the 10 s before receiving the ball.
  - Overall rate: 3.0 scans per 10-s window, or 0.44 scans/s (SD 0.30). Central midfielders scanned most and forwards least (overall d = 0.55).
  - Effect of opponent pressure: scanning was lowest when the nearest opponent was 0-1 m away, rose with distance and plateaued at about 4 m (0-1 m vs 10+ m, d = 0.57).
  - Effect of pitch zone: above 0.6 scans/s around the player's own penalty area, and below 0.3 near the boundaries.
  - Successful actions came after 0.46 scans/s and unsuccessful ones after 0.37 (d = 0.20).
  - Completed passes came after 0.46 scans/s and incomplete passes after 0.40 (d = 0.15).
  - Bayesian model, controlling for pass difficulty: scanning coefficient μβ = 0.13, 95% HDI [0.02, 0.24]. The effect was positive for about 86% of players on average.
  - The authors call the effect "small, but positive."
  - Caveats: one possession-based club, home games only, and partial funding from Arsenal FC.
- A coaching-app blog quotes scan-rate "tiers" such as "83%+ completion for high scanners". These come from a different sample (1,118 situations) and should not be treated as peer-reviewed numbers. I did not confirm which page carries them; the candidates from search were [3cb Performance](https://3cbperformance.substack.com/p/does-scanning-the-pitch-before-receiving) and [Be Your Best, "Scanning & Metrics"](https://beyourbest.com/scanning-metrics).

**Anticipation, occlusion and decision speed [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Ma, Zhao, Qin & Qu (2026, Research Square preprint, not peer-reviewed). Source: [doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-9304532/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9304532/v1).
  - Sample: 20 elite experts vs 20 novices, ice hockey temporal-occlusion decision task with fNIRS.
  - Accuracy: 77.0% ± 6.5% vs 51.0% ± 15.4%, t(38) = 6.97, p < .001.
  - Response time: 812.0 ± 130.1 ms vs 1073.6 ± 160.9 ms, t(38) = -5.63, p < .001.
  - Experts showed deactivation of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and that deactivation correlated with accuracy (r = -0.398).
- A Swedish fMRI pilot study using ice hockey clips. Source: [PMC3841982](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3841982). Authors and year were not confirmed in my search.
  - Sample: 6 elite players from the Swedish second division, 5 intermediate players, 6 non-hockey controls.
  - When experts anticipated correctly they recruited motor and temporal brain regions. Novices relied on visual regions while watching and prefrontal regions while deciding.
- Mulligan, McCracken & Hodges (2012) analysed real game decisions. Source: [UBC MSL](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/node/160); [IJSEP abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1612197X.2012.672009).
  - Sample: 23 experts vs 14 non-experts, 118 decision points from game film, decision quality scored by two expert coaches.
  - Experts labelled situations "familiar" twice as often as non-experts (p = .021).
  - Familiar decisions were judged better than unfamiliar ones (p = .01), but only for recognition-based decisions.

**Multiple-object tracking [ICE HOCKEY / MULTI-SPORT]**
- Faubert (2013, *Scientific Reports*), 3D-MOT task with no sport content, 15 learning sessions:
  - 102 professionals (NHL, English Premier League, French Top 14 rugby), 173 elite amateurs (NCAA and a European Olympic training centre), and 33 non-athlete students.
  - Professionals started highest and had the steepest learning curve. There was no sex difference.
  - Sources: [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130131095149.htm); [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/professional-athletes-appear-to-learn-faster-than-university-students-1.1361724).
  - Conflicting reports on how many NHL players took part: one French-language report gives 51 soccer, 30 rugby and 21 NHL players.
  - Conflict of interest: Faubert is chief science officer of CogniSens, which sells NeuroTracker.
- Acquin et al. (2025, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*) tested 48 university varsity athletes (male ice hockey and female volleyball) on baseline NeuroTracker 3D-MOT across 2019-2022.
  - Baseline scores did not correlate with season performance statistics.
  - The one exception was hockey penalty minutes (total r = -.40; per game r = -.39).
  - Source: [doi:10.3389/fspor.2025.1608463](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1608463).

**Meta-analyses across sports [MULTI-SPORT]**
- Mann, Williams, Ward & Janelle (2007, *Journal of Sport & Exercise Psychology* 29:457-478): 42 studies and 388 effect sizes.
  - Sport type, research paradigm and stimulus presentation mode significantly moderated expert-novice differences.
  - Later papers cite it for three findings: experts use fewer fixations of longer duration, look at specific fixation locations, and have a longer quiet-eye period.
  - Sources: [PubMed record 17968048](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17968048/); restated in [Vickers et al. look-up-line manuscript](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4081/3/Effect%20of%20the%20Look-Up%20Line%20on%20the%20Gaze%2C%20Head%20Orientation%20and.pdf).
  - I could not get the exact pooled effect sizes from the paper itself. A d = 1.53 for quiet eye that circulates online comes from a 2016 quiet-eye meta-analysis, not Mann 2007.
- Travassos et al. (2013, *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 14:211-219): 111 effect sizes and 882 expert and non-expert participants.
  - Six measures were pooled: verbal knowledge, eye movements, decision time, response accuracy, movement accuracy, movement time.
  - Expertise differences were clearer when participants had to perform actual sport actions and when stimuli were presented in situ.
  - Source: [SHU repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/7398/). Moderator effect-size values were not retrieved.

### Inferences
Possible targets for a VR trainer:
- **Scanning rate.** Ice hockey has no published scans-per-second norm. Soccer's 0.44 scans/s is a rough reference only: hockey is faster and played in a smaller space, and in-situ hockey scans are short, about one fixation of roughly 135 ms. Scans that pick up opponents and open space during off-puck defensive phases are probably the more relevant hockey target.
- **Quick-then-quiet sequence.** A defensive "read" should show several brief tracking fixations on attackers and space, then a stable final fixation on the puck/stick or the puck carrier's body about 1 s before engagement (elite 1-v-1 data: about 1.1 s QE).
- **Head-down drift.** Head-down behaviour increases with traffic. A trainer can measure head pitch and penalise long near-ice fixations.
- **Decision timing.** Elite anticipation advantages of about 250 ms in response time and about 25 percentage points in accuracy appear in one non-peer-reviewed hockey study. These give a rough order of magnitude for occlusion-based scoring, not a validated norm.

### Gaps
- Martell & Vickers (2004): sample size, millisecond values and the elite vs near-elite QE durations could not be extracted from the available abstracts.
- Berg et al. (2025): scan frequency per position and how a scan was defined were not in the abstract.
- I did not find peer-reviewed occlusion-paradigm accuracy data for ice-hockey skaters reading a puck carrier (as opposed to goalies reading shots), apart from the Ma et al. preprint.
- I recall Mulligan, Lohse & Hodges (2016, *Psychological Research*), an ice-hockey shot-prediction study with an action-incongruent secondary task as evidence for motor simulation. I did not verify it in this session, so it is not cited above.
- A 2024 VR study comparing eye and head tracking of hockey players, wrestlers and controls on a puck-hitting task ([PMC11574569](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11574569/)) was found but not read.

## Q3. What is known about defencemen and goalies specifically?

### Takeaway
Defenceman-specific perceptual-cognitive research is very sparse. The core evidence is the Vickers lab's defensive-tactics and 1-v-1 checking studies:
- elite defenders fixate tactical locations faster;
- successful checks follow a longer QE on the puck/stick or the attacker's body.

I found no peer-reviewed study of gap-control decisions, reading odd-man rushes, or anticipating passes by defencemen. Goalie research is richer: an earlier and longer QE on the puck/stick predicts saves.

### Cited Findings

**Defenders [ICE HOCKEY]**
- In the 1-v-1 defensive play studied by Martell & Vickers (2004), gaze before contact combined fixation and tracking on the opponent, puck, stick, boards and net. "Successful checks occurred when the D used a longer QE on the puck/stick and/or the opponent's body prior to contact." Source: [Vickers et al. look-up-line manuscript, summarising Martell & Vickers 2004](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4081/3/Effect%20of%20the%20Look-Up%20Line%20on%20the%20Gaze%2C%20Head%20Orientation%20and.pdf).
- Martell & Vickers: elite defensive players fixated tactical locations more rapidly on successful plays, and ended with a long final fixation isolating one target ("quick then quiet"). Their maximal gaze-shift speed did not differ from near-elite players, so the difference lies in gaze control, not raw oculomotor speed. Sources: [SPONET](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4008469?lng=en); [AAHPERD 2004](https://aahperd.confex.com/aahperd/2004/finalprogram/paper_5579.htm).
- Vickers et al. (2017) used a 1-v-1 drill toward the boards with offence (O) and defence (D) roles:
  - Phases: preparation, decision-making, cut to the boards, contact.
  - The defender did not know which way the attacker would go; catch trials prevented him from anticipating it.
  - QE before contact was about 1.1 s for both roles combined, and rink-by-position interactions were not significant.
  - Source: [LJMU manuscript](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4081/3/Effect%20of%20the%20Look-Up%20Line%20on%20the%20Gaze%2C%20Head%20Orientation%20and.pdf).
- In Berg et al. (2025), the link between scanning before receiving and the success of the next action did not differ by position, so it applies to defencemen receiving outlets as much as to forwards. Source: [SPONET](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4092070?lng=en).
- Tétreault et al. (2025) included both forwards and defencemen in an 88-player talent-identification sample, but the abstract reports no position-specific perceptual-cognitive results. Source: [doi:10.1177/17479541241304360](https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541241304360).
- In soccer, central defenders scanned at rates close to wingers (pairwise d = 0.16) and below central midfielders (d up to 0.56 against side defenders). Source: [Jordet et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7573254). [SOCCER]
- Jordet et al. (2020) list defensive scanning as unanalysed, so even soccer has little defender-specific off-ball scanning data. Source: [Jordet et al. 2020 limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7573254). [SOCCER]

**Goalies [ICE HOCKEY]**
- Panchuk & Vickers (2006, *Human Movement Science* 25:733-752):
  - Relative QE onset was earlier on saves (8.6% of the trial) than on goals (18.86%).
  - QE was longer on saves (952.3 ms, 80.5% of the trial) than on goals (826.1 ms, 70.1%).
  - Source: [VU repository record](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/3062/).
  - The University of Calgary press release adds that the best goalies settle their gaze on the puck and the shooter's stick almost a full second before release — [ScienceDaily, Oct 2006](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/10/061026095347.htm).
- A later study found that QE predicts goaltender success on deflected shots, with long QE durations and early onset. Source: [VU repository, "Quiet eye predicts goaltender success in deflected ice hockey shots", 2017, European Journal of Sport Science](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/32496/). Numbers were not retrieved.
- A Jyväskylä thesis compared the gaze of elite and amateur ice hockey goaltenders — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/111555). It was not read.
- Counter-evidence [SOCCER]: QE findings for goalkeepers are inconsistent outside hockey. One tactical goalkeeping study found shorter QE on saves (200.4 ± 150.2 ms) than on goals (248.5 ± 95.7 ms) — [IJESAB abstract](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol16/iss3/319). A 2022 soccer goalkeeper study (Franks et al.) found no QE-outcome correlation, per the same search summary.
- [FIELD HOCKEY] Goalkeepers facing drag-flicks: occlusion results suggest that the kinematic information available, not processing time, drives expert anticipation — [Baker, Farrow, Elliott & Anderson 2009 record](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4018389?lng=en).
- [FIELD HOCKEY] In a 2021 *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* study, temporal-occlusion training improved expert goalkeepers' anticipation in video, field and competition assessments on an individual basis, while controls did not improve — [Murdoch research portal](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Individual-differences-in-performance-and-learning/991005544098607891).

### Inferences
- A defenceman's key perceptual-cognitive moments map onto the available evidence as follows:
  - **Reading the rush:** early anticipation from attacker skating lines and puck carrier posture, plus scanning for the second and third attackers.
  - **Gap decision:** when to step up versus retreat, which needs familiarity with rush patterns (recognition-primed).
  - **Engagement:** a stable QE on the puck/stick or the carrier's body, about 1 s before contact.
  - **Breakout:** scanning before receiving the puck, as in Berg et al.
- Only engagement and the breakout have direct ice-hockey data. Rush reading and gap timing have to be inferred from general anticipation research and from soccer and field-hockey analogues.
- The finding that elite and near-elite defenders do not differ in raw gaze speed suggests a trainer should coach where and when to look, not drill eye-movement speed.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study on gap control, odd-man rush defence (2-on-1, 3-on-2), defensive-zone coverage scanning, or pass-lane anticipation by defencemen.
- I found no study of adult recreational defencemen's gaze or decisions.
- The sample sizes and methods of Panchuk & Vickers (2006) were not confirmed; the figures come from the repository record via search.

## Q4. Which theoretical frameworks matter for training design, and what do they imply?

### Takeaway
Two traditions shape design:
- **The expert performance approach** (Ericsson, Williams, Ward): capture the representative task in a lab, find what experts do differently with eye-tracking, occlusion and verbal reports, then train it with video simulation, instruction and feedback.
- **Ecological dynamics and representative learning design** (Pinder, Davids, Renshaw, Araújo): perception and action must stay coupled. Training needs the same information sources (functional fidelity) and similar actions (action fidelity).

Meta-analytic data favour the second for transfer:
- Expertise effects grow when responses are real sport actions in situ (Travassos 2013).
- Perceptual-cognitive training transfers on-court at about g = 0.65, versus 1.51 on the trained task. VR or 3D stimuli and sport-specific responses show the larger transfer, though the moderator differences were not statistically significant (Zhu 2024).
- Generic, decoupled tasks such as 3D-MOT improve only themselves (Romeas 2025).

### Cited Findings

**Representative learning design and perception-action coupling [MULTI-SPORT]**
- Pinder, Davids, Renshaw & Araújo (2011, *Journal of Sport & Exercise Psychology* 33(1):146-155) proposed representative learning design, building on Brunswik's representative design. Practice is judged by:
  - functionality, meaning the same informational cues are available as in competition;
  - action fidelity, meaning the movements match those used in competition.
  - Source: [secondary summary, "What needs to be real"](https://1thoughtgarden.substack.com/p/what-needs-to-be-real). The original was not read.
- A 2019 *Frontiers in Psychology* editorial noted two problems. Perceptual training research has mostly used simplified protocols "that fail to replicate the tight coupling between perception and action." A systematic review of commercial generalised cognitive-training devices found good evidence only for near transfer. Source: [Editorial, PMC6901661](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6901661/).
- Travassos et al. (2013): the requisite response (sport action versus verbal or button press) and the stimulus presentation (in situ versus video) moderated expertise effects. Representative constraints, with sport actions in situ, were the most functional protocols. Source: [SHU](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/7398/).

**Transfer of perceptual-cognitive training [TEAM SPORTS, elite athletes]**
- Zhu et al. (2024, *Behavioral Sciences* 14(10):919) meta-analysed 22 studies (45 effect sizes) of elite team-sport athletes with more than 3 years' experience. Source: [PMC11505547](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/).

  | Outcome | Hedges' g | 95% CI | I² |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Task-specific response accuracy | 1.51 | [0.98, 2.05] | 78.7% |
  | On-court transfer, response accuracy | 0.65 | [0.15, 1.16] | 71.4% |
  | Task-specific response time | -0.91 | [-1.45, -0.38] | 48.0% |
  | On-court transfer, response time | -0.44 | [-0.81, -0.06] | 13.8% |

  Moderators for transfer of response accuracy (none of the between-group differences were significant):

  | Moderator | Level | g | 95% CI |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Stimulus (p = .449) | Computer video | 0.19 | [-0.56, 0.94] |
  | | Life-size video | 0.46 | [-0.08, 1.00] |
  | | 3D/VR | 0.96 | [0.02, 1.89] |
  | Response (p = .429) | Verbal or keyboard | 0.41 | [0.07, 0.75] |
  | | Sport-specific action | 0.87 | [-0.22, 1.96] |
  | Duration (p = .241) | ≤ 4 weeks | 0.38 | [0.01, 0.74] |
  | | > 4 weeks | 1.25 | [-0.16, 2.65] |

  - Recommendations: first-person VR stimuli with sport-specific responses, more than 4 weeks, 2-4 sessions per week, 10-25 minutes per session.
  - Conclusion: "transfer effects were limited." Heterogeneity was high and samples small.
- Zentgraf and colleagues reviewed 16 off-court training studies in interactive sports. About 60% of training-specific measures improved, but transfer results were mixed and inconsistent. Source: [Münster CRIS record](https://cris-portal.uni-muenster.de/portal/de/publication/23499732).
- Cè & Repetto's systematic review of immersive training (VR, 360° video, CAVE) in sport found the evidence "limited and methodologies heterogeneous." Where effects were reported they were moderate to large, and only 4 studies included cognitive measures. Source: [UCSC repository PDF](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/retrieve/ea4872a5-55a6-4c7d-9068-09347b928671/Effects%20of%20immersive%20training%20on%20sports%20perceptual-cognitive%20skills%20%20A%20systematic%20literature%20review.pdf).

**Decoupled, generic training: no far transfer**
- [SOCCER] Romeas, Goujat, Faubert & Labbé (2025, *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 76:102770) ran a randomised trial with 62 male academy players (30 on dual-task 3D-MOT, 32 controls).
  - 3D-MOT scores improved (p < .001).
  - There was no transfer to a near-transfer attention test, to small-sided game performance, or to early-career success (all p > .05).
  - The study "failed to replicate previous findings."
  - Source: [ÉTS Pure record](https://pure.etsmtl.ca/en/publications/no-transfer-of-3d-multiple-object-tracking-training-on-game-perfo/).
- [ICE HOCKEY] Heilmann & Schubert (2025, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*) trained 25 young elite players (median age 14, range 11-17) with sport-specific cognitive VR training twice a week for 9 weeks (n = 12) against technique-training controls (n = 13).
  - The sport-specific Flanker test improved, with a group-by-time interaction (p < .001 for congruent and incongruent trials).
  - There was no effect on the cued Go/NoGo task or on the general Flanker effect.
  - This is near transfer only.
  - Source: [doi:10.3389/fspor.2025.1682165](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1682165).
- [ICE HOCKEY] Brinkbäumer et al. (2026) ran a randomised crossover with 74 elite youth players. A 20-minute VR warm-up and a 20-minute dual-task warm-up both improved simple tapping. On the dual-task tapping test, dual-task training helped while VR produced "a small decline." Dual-task Stroop gains correlated with on-ice measures (ρ = .27-.39). Source: [doi:10.3389/fspor.2026.1766605](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2026.1766605).
- [ICE HOCKEY] Baseline 3D-MOT did not predict varsity hockey season statistics, apart from penalty minutes — [Acquin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1608463).

**Expert performance approach and perception-only training [TENNIS]**
- Williams, Ward, Knowles & Smeeton (2002, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 8(4):259-270) measured anticipation with realistic film simulations, movement-based responses and portable eye-tracking. They then trained recreational tennis players with video simulation, instruction and feedback. Source: [Brighton research portal](https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/anticipation-skill-in-a-real-world-task-measurement-training-and-/).
- Williams, Smeeton & Allen (2004) trained novices on court and compared perception-action training, perception-only training and a technical-instruction control. They found no significant difference between the perception-action and perception-only groups. Coupling is therefore not always shown to add benefit in novices. Source: [JASP abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413200490518002), via search summary.

**Practitioner framework [ICE HOCKEY, governing body, non-peer-reviewed]**
- USA Hockey's American Development Model promotes small-area games to multiply decisions per repetition. It uses rule constraints to shape reading and adjusting, and argues that skills learned in isolated, blocked drills transfer poorly to games. The evidence offered is anecdotal or practitioner-based. Sources: [ADM "Developing decision makers at 10U"](https://www.admkids.com/news_article/show/543515-developing-decision-makers-at-10u); [USA Hockey news article](https://www.usahockey.com/news_article/show/816141).

### Inferences
Implications for a VR defenceman trainer:
1. **Keep perception and action coupled.** Have the player respond with head turns, body orientation, stick position and skating proxies such as stepping up or pivoting, not button presses. Representative responses produce larger expertise effects (Travassos) and trend toward larger transfer (Zhu).
2. **Preserve the specifying information.** Show the attacker's skating kinematics, stick and puck position, and support players' positions in first person and at true scale. VR's 3D, life-size, first-person view is its main advantage over video.
3. **Avoid generic cognitive drills** such as MOT spheres, Stroop or Flanker as the main content. They improve themselves and transfer near at best (Romeas 2025; Heilmann & Schubert 2025; Acquin 2025).
4. **Plan the dose:** more than 4 weeks, 2-4 sessions per week, 10-25 minutes each.
5. **Vary the scenarios and build a library of rush and zone patterns.** This builds the familiarity that drives recognition-primed decisions (Mulligan 2012). Vary them randomly rather than blocking them, in line with ADM and contextual-interference reasoning.
6. **Treat VR as a supplement to on-ice play.** Transfer from VR is moderate at best (g of about 0.65-0.96, with wide intervals). VR should prepare and repeat situations that are rare on the ice, not replace games.

### Gaps
- I found no study that directly compared coupled and decoupled perceptual training in ice hockey.
- I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of commercial hockey VR trainers (for example Sense Arena) with on-ice transfer outcomes.
- Exact effect sizes for the Travassos (2013) moderators were not retrieved.
- Pinder et al. (2011) was read only through secondary summaries.

## Q5. How do these skills develop with age and experience, and can adult recreational players still improve?

### Takeaway
Perceptual-cognitive advantages appear early. Elite youth soccer players outperform sub-elite peers from about age 9, and anticipation and the use of situational probabilities are the best discriminators. These skills grow with accumulated sport-specific experience: in youth hockey, years of training correlate with faster executive-function responses. For adults, training studies show improvements in recreational and novice players in other sports, and a meta-analysis of elite athletes found adults transfer about as well as adolescents (g = 0.63). No study tests adult recreational ice-hockey players directly.

### Cited Findings
- **Age 9-17 [SOCCER]:** Elite youth players showed better perceptual-cognitive skills than sub-elite peers from age 9. Anticipation and situational probabilities best separated skill groups, and pattern recall best predicted age. Source: [Ward & Williams 2003, UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/2149).
- **Years of training [ICE HOCKEY]:** Among 132 Chinese youth players, years of training correlated with faster responses on inhibition, updating and shifting tasks (r = -0.185 to -0.450, all p < .05). Expert-novice differences appeared in speed, not accuracy. Source: [Wang, Yang & Shi 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1670150).
- **Learning rate [MULTI-SPORT, including NHL]:** On a 3D-MOT task over 15 sessions, professionals improved fastest, followed by elite amateurs and then students. This suggests experts differ partly in how quickly they learn dynamic scenes. Source: [ScienceDaily on Faubert 2013](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130131095149.htm). Caveat: learning a 3D-MOT task does not transfer to games (Romeas 2025).
- **Adults versus adolescents [TEAM SPORTS, elite]:**
  - Adults: on-court transfer of response accuracy g = 0.63, 95% CI [0.29, 0.98], I² = 0%.
  - Adolescents: g = 0.58, 95% CI [-0.65, 1.82], I² = 88.7%.
  - The groups did not differ (p = .939). All participants were elite, so no results exist for lower-skill participants.
  - Source: [Zhu et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/).
- **Recreational and novice players [TENNIS]:** Recreational players were trained with video simulation, instruction and feedback ([Williams et al. 2002](https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/anticipation-skill-in-a-real-world-task-measurement-training-and-/)). Novices were trained on court with perception-action or perception-only training ([Williams, Smeeton & Allen 2004](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413200490518002)). The exact sizes of improvement were not retrieved this session.
- **Experts keep improving [FIELD HOCKEY]:** Expert goalkeepers improved anticipation after temporal-occlusion training, with transfer to field and competition tests on an individual basis. Source: [Murdoch 2021](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Individual-differences-in-performance-and-learning/991005544098607891).
- **Late developers [ICE HOCKEY]:** Talent-ID work on "sleeping talent" (2023) and on 4-year outcomes of tests taken at age 15 (Tétreault et al. 2025) suggests psychological and perceptual-cognitive profiles carry information beyond current performance. Sources: [McGill author page](https://www.mcgill.ca/education/daniel-fortin-guichard); [Tétreault et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541241304360).
- **Situational familiarity [ICE HOCKEY]:** Familiarity comes from experience and is linked to better recognition-based decisions. Source: [Mulligan et al. 2012](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/node/160).

### Inferences
- An adult recreational defenceman probably has a smaller library of familiar situations and less automatic scanning than elite players. These are the most trainable parts, because recognition and anticipation are knowledge built from exposure, not fixed capacities.
- Raw visual function, such as acuity and gaze speed, does not separate skill levels (Ward & Williams 2003; Martell & Vickers 2004). Training should target the use of information, not basic vision.
- Expected size of improvement: elite studies suggest moderate transfer, about g = 0.4-0.65, after more than 4 weeks of training. A novice starting further back may gain more on the trained task (lab-type effects reached g = 1.5 in elites), but on-ice transfer is uncertain.
- An adult also has to undo habits such as puck-watching and a head-down posture in traffic. The trainer should measure these explicitly, for example with head pitch and the share of fixations on the puck versus on attackers and space, and reward exploration away from the puck.

### Gaps
- I found no study of perceptual-cognitive training in adult recreational ice-hockey players, and no data on age-related decline in these skills among adult hockey players.
- I found no Hockey Canada or USA Hockey long-term athlete development document with research on adult (40+) learning of hockey sense. The ADM material focuses on youth.
- I found no ice-hockey longitudinal study tracking how scanning or anticipation changes with age.
