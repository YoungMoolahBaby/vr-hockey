# Tracked contact in VR: controller tracking at speed, blade velocity, and contact rules

Research round "Shot release", 2026-11-10. Topic: how Quest 3 / Touch Plus tracking behaves at high speed; how to estimate an implement's velocity robustly with low latency; how VR sports games and physics engines handle double hits, long carries and tracking jumps. What it means for the sim's 1 kHz swept blade contact.

Tags: **[M]** measured in the cited study. **[D]** derived by me (arithmetic or my own analysis of Jon's recordings, method given). **[E]** estimate or physics with stated assumptions. **[V]** vendor claim or developer anecdote. **[S]** secondary (read in a citing source).

Earlier notes this builds on, not repeated here: `reports/VR hockey stick and puck feel.md` (tracking guards: TRACKED flags, a 3 cm/tick sweep cap), `VR hockey stick and puck feel/vr_sports_implement_feel.md` (Quest 3 fast-swing forum reports, Eleven, Golf+, gyro-based racket speed), `stationary_play_audio_latency.md` (Warburton 2-13 ms, predictedDisplayTime), `Hockey physics feel/stick and puck contact.md` (Woo, Swarén, Villaseñor shot data).

## Bottom line

1. **At Jon's speeds the tracking holds, and the too-fast shot tail is real tracked motion, not jitter.** During the fastest blade motion (blade above 10 m/s), both controllers report full tracking 98.3-99.9% of the time. His hands peak at 8-9 m/s and the controllers turn at up to 2,800 °/s. Six of the eight shots over 80 mph (up to 105 mph) happened during swings where the blade, smoothed over 20 ms, was already moving at 30-38 m/s. The runtime's own velocity output put the blade at 35-47 m/s at those moments [D]. Smoothing the velocity over 4-20 ms would trim the top of the tail by only about 2-12% [D].
2. **The missing physics is the stick's inertia.** In real slap shots the stick's rotation gives the blade only 16-17 m/s; the rest (9-13 m/s) comes from the body and arms moving the stick ([Woo et al. 2004](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/1368)) [M]. Conditions: 5 varsity and 5 recreational men, stationary slap shots on a lab platform covered in polyethylene, a wooden stick, 60 Hz tracking; the translation part is the remainder after subtracting the rotation part. Jon's ordinary virtual shots get 16-17 m/s from rotation too [D]. That looks realistic, but the comparison crosses shot types: Woo measured slap shots, and nobody has measured stick rotation in wrist or snap shots (added in fact-check). His shots over 30 m/s get 27-31 m/s from rotation alone, from wrist flicks with the hands 19-23 cm apart [D]. A real stick (roughly 0.35-0.45 kg·m² about the top hand) would need a 330-720 N hand couple to swing like that [E]. Measured grip forces are far smaller. In high-calibre men's slap shots, the mean peak force on any one face of the shaft is 15-62 N. The four faces of one hand sum to 91-118 N ([Zane 2012](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d), Table 6, lab synthetic ice) [M]. So the flick needs about 3-8 times the measured force per hand [D]. (Corrected in fact-check: the earlier "at most about 160 N measured" is the top of a colour scale in Zane's Figure 36, not a measured peak.) The controllers weigh about 126 g each and resist nothing.
3. **The sim's per-tick blade velocity (1 ms pose differences) is about 10 times noisier than it needs to be.** At the blade it jumps by up to 1.5 m/s per tick (p99) during stickhandling and 13 m/s per tick during shots. That implies 3,000-15,000 m/s² of acceleration, which a hand-driven stick cannot produce [D]. A 4 ms window cuts the noise 4-5 times for 2 ms of delay. An 8 ms window cuts it about 9 times for 4 ms of delay. The runtime's own `XrSpaceVelocity` cuts it 7-9 times and lags 2-9 ms [D]. Keep the raw poses for the sweep and use the smoothed velocity for the impulse.
4. **Do not predict the physics poses forward. Sample at "now", or a few ms in the past.** Quest Link sends the headset pose and "input state" unpredicted, and the PC runtime extrapolates them ([Meta 2019](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/how-does-oculus-link-work-the-architecture-pipeline-and-aadt-explained/)) [V]. The blog does not name controller poses; that they travel the same way is my inference [E] (narrowed in fact-check). Prediction over-compensates after a motion onset [M] and overshoots on sudden stops ([Warburton et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-01983-5)). The stop overshoot is shown only in their supplement, in an experiment "not designed to assess this in detail", and reversals were not tested [E] (retagged in fact-check). In Jon's predicted session the 1 ms rotation noise was about 3 times worse [D]. OpenXR requires runtimes to keep at least 50 ms of interpolated history "preceding the most recently received pose" ([spec §2.16](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenXR/specs/1.1/html/xrspec.html)) [M, spec text], so a 5-10 ms look-back is a cheap A/B. Over Link, a time 5 ms back is interpolated only if a pose newer than that has already arrived; the Link pose rate is not published [E] (caveat added in fact-check).
5. **Use the 1€ filter on the drawn stick, not on the contact velocity.** Its cutoff rises with speed ([Casiez et al. 2012](https://gery.casiez.net/publications/CHI2012-casiez.pdf)) [M], so at shot speeds it lets through exactly the speed-proportional spikes the contact needs removed [D]. For contact, use a short window plus plausibility gates. Meta's own throw code takes a RANSAC consensus over the last 8-10 samples and skips the newest 2 ([Meta ISDK](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/reference/interaction/v201/class_oculus_interaction_throw_r_a_n_s_a_c_velocity)) [V]. Valve's SteamVR rejects controller motion beyond what it assumes is humanly possible ([Road to VR](https://roadtovr.com/?p=168587)) [V].
6. **Physics engines do not cap contact time.** They gate bounces below 1-2 m/s, report hit events only above about 1 m/s, cap how fast overlap is pushed out (3 m/s in Box2D, 10 m/s in Unity), and give teleports no velocity [M, engine docs]. Hobby VR paddle code adds hit cooldowns of about 50 ms and notes that this costs shot variety [V]. Real puck contacts last 15-38 ms in slap shots and one-timers [M] (two pros on ice; 4 elite and 5 recreational players in a lab on polyethylene). Wrist shots are estimated at "about 100ms or more" [E] (Plesch et al.'s own estimate, not measured; retagged from [S] in fact-check). A contact-time cap below ~100 ms would cut real wrist shots.
7. **Tracking jumps are rare in Jon's data and easy to tell apart from motion.** Positions became "inferred" (valid but not tracked) only 4-11 times per hand per session. None of these began while the hand moved faster than 3 m/s, and re-acquisition moved the controller at most 30 mm in one tick [D]. Real pose jumps (recentring, snap turns) show up as large position changes while the runtime reports almost no velocity. That gives a sharper jump test than the current 0.2 m per tick, which allows up to 200 m/s.

## 1. How Touch Plus tracking works

- **Sensors.** Touch Plus controllers have IR LEDs on the face (plus one at the base of the handle) and an IMU. The headset cameras see the LEDs. Optical data is fused with the accelerometer and gyroscope. Quest 3 also runs hand tracking all the time and fuses it with the LED tracking when the LEDs are hidden ([UploadVR, Bosworth AMA, June 2023](https://www.uploadvr.com/meta-explains-quest-3-controller-tracking/); [Road to VR, Sept 2023](https://roadtovr.com/quest-3-touch-plus-tracking-coverage/)) [V].
- **Coverage.** Quest 3 sees less above the head than Quest 2, and more around the torso and shoulders. For short spells outside the cameras' view, the headset "can effectively estimate the position" of the controllers ([Road to VR](https://roadtovr.com/quest-3-touch-plus-tracking-coverage/)) [V]. In 2020 that IMU-only estimate worked "only for a few seconds at most" ([UploadVR](https://www.uploadvr.com/oculus-jedi-controller-driver-found/)) [V].
- **The fusion.** Insight computes controller poses "every millisecond". Optical fixes bound the drift from integrating the IMU. An "extrapolation function with dynamic damping" and a "kinematic model" predict motion ahead ([Meta AI, 2019](https://ai.meta.com/blog/powered-by-ai-oculus-insight/)) [V]. LED detection uses camera images with very short exposures ([Meta dev blog](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/increasing-fidelity-with-constellation-tracked-controllers/)) and needs at least 3 LEDs in view to solve a pose from one image ([Meta, LED matching](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/tracking-technology-explained-led-matching/)) [V].
- **The IMU.** The Quest 3 controller board carries a TDK ICM-42686-P 6-axis IMU ([iFixit chip ID](https://www.ifixit.com/Guide/Meta+Quest+3+Chip+ID/165932)) [M]. TDK rates it to ±32 g and ±4,000 °/s ([TDK](https://invensense.tdk.com/products/motion-tracking/6-axis/icm-42686-p)) [V]. Which range Meta's firmware selects is not published. The 2016 Rift Touch used a TDK ICM-20601 ([UploadVR 2020](https://www.uploadvr.com/oculus-jedi-controller-driver-found/)) [V].
- **What the gyro must cover in Jon's play.** His controllers turned at up to 2,680-2,790 °/s (top hand) and 1,950-2,340 °/s (bottom hand) in three sessions [D, runtime angular velocity]. These are above ±2,000 °/s, so the firmware range is wider than that, or the value is not raw gyro [D]. Hand acceleration, from 5 ms differences of the runtime velocity, reached 14-18 g at p99.9, 20-24 g at p99.99, and 31-46 g at the maximum [D]. If the accelerometer were set to ±16 g, the hardest moments would clip it.
- **Link transport.** The Quest client sends the PC runtime the headset pose, input state and vsync timing "without prediction"; the PC runtime "extrapolates these values to match the target display period" ([Meta, Link architecture, 2019](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/how-does-oculus-link-work-the-architecture-pipeline-and-aadt-explained/)) [V]. The rate at which controller poses cross the cable is not published. So a pose located at "now" on the PC is an extrapolation from the last packet [E].
- **Mass.** A Touch Plus weighs about 126 g with its AA battery ([VR wiki](https://vrarwiki.com/wiki/Meta_Quest_Touch_Plus_Controllers)) [V]. A senior stick weighs about 0.4-0.45 kg (see section 5).

## 2. Latency and prediction

- **Controller motion-to-photon latency** (HTC Vive, Rift, Rift S, Index; high-speed camera): 21-42 ms at a sudden movement onset. Once prediction locks on, it falls to 2-13 ms, within 25-58 ms of the onset. "The virtual controller over-compensates to catch up to the real one before stabilizing." The systems also overshoot on sudden stops, shown only in a supplementary figure; the experiment was "not designed to assess this in detail" ([Warburton et al. 2023, Behav Res Methods 55:3658](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/193470/3/s13428-022-01983-5.pdf)) [M]. Under constant-acceleration prediction the position is "underestimated" while accelerating and "overestimated" while decelerating (same source, reasoning from LaValle's predictor) [E] (retagged from [M] in fact-check: this is their argument, not a measurement). No Quest controller was tested.
- **Prediction error grows with the horizon.** Head-orientation error under an unmodelled 1,000 °/s² acceleration: about 0.2° at 20 ms, 0.8° at 40 ms and 3.2° at 80 ms. Prediction "makes catastrophic errors when the head motion abruptly changes" ([LaValle, Oculus blog 2013](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/the-latent-power-of-prediction/)) [M for the DK1 head data; the error figures are his arithmetic].
- **Streaming makes it worse.** Over CloudXR to a Quest 2, users reported controller "rubberbanding": "The more acceleration, the more overshoot we see on the controller tracking" ([NVIDIA forum, 2021](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/controller-jitter-on-oculus-quest-2/169566)) [V]. A PC VR streaming developer wrote that noisy velocity sent to the PC is amplified by "the PC runtime's own extrapolation to photon time", so the controllers "shake at rest". They used deadbands of about 0.05 m/s and 10 °/s ([Apple developer forum](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/855999)) [V]. HTC's runtime notes that prediction stability "will greatly depend on the quality of the velocities" ([Vive Wave docs](https://hub.vive.com/storage/docs/en-us/PosePrediction.html)) [V].
- **The history window.** "The runtime must retain and return at least 50 milliseconds of historical data, interpolating as required, preceding the most recently received pose." During tracking loss the runtime keeps POSITION_VALID set but may clear POSITION_TRACKED for "inferred or last-known" poses ([OpenXR 1.1 spec §2.16 and xrLocateSpace](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenXR/specs/1.1/html/xrspec.html)) [M, spec text].
- **Hand tracking, for comparison.** Robot-arm tests of Quest 3 hand tracking: 38 ± 31 ms motion-to-photon. Separately, the hand-tracking (detection) latency ranged 14-221 ms ([Godden et al. 2025, IEEE TVCG](https://ericg5.github.io/ResearchPortfolio/papers/characterization)) [M]. (Corrected in fact-check: the 14-221 ms range belongs to the detection latency, not to the 38 ± 31 ms motion-to-photon figure.) Touch Plus falls back on hand tracking when its LEDs are hidden, so a hidden controller is likely to be both later and noisier [E].
- **The sim's own test** (sandbox.py comment, 2026-11-09): sampling poses at display time made per-sample hand speed spike to 3.4 times its 20 ms average (p99), against 1.4 times at "now", and shots jumped to 75-100 mph [V, project log]. My re-analysis of those two sessions agrees. The ratio of 1 ms to 20 ms rotation speed was p99 5.4-6.9 (p99.9 9-12) with prediction, against p99 1.6-2.4 (p99.9 2-8) without [D].

## 4. What Jon's recordings show

**Method [D].** I read `rec.csv` from five sessions without changing anything: sandbox 2026-11-07 11:59 (20 min, standing, no locomotion), free skates 2026-11-09 14:20 (prediction on) and 14:32 (off), and 2026-11-10 06:00 and 12:08 (21 and 23 min; 12:08 is the one with the 105 mph shot). The sampler locates both controllers at 1 kHz, at "now", and logs the runtime's linear and angular velocity. I modelled the blade as a point 1.40 m from the top (right) hand along the line through the bottom hand, which is close to the sim's "top hand holds, bottom hand aims" grab but not identical. The free-skate poses include the rig's motion; I separated it out using `rig.csv`. Shot speeds come from the logged `shot` events, and puck speeds from `puck.csv` as recorded.

**Sampling.** The sampler runs at 1,000 samples/s (937/s in the sandbox session), with a p99 interval of 2.5-3.8 ms. Stalls longer than 10 ms occur 5-7 times per session, at most 38 ms (165 ms once in the sandbox session) [D].

**Repeated poses.** In the standing session, an exact repeat of the last position happened in 1.7% (bottom) and 8.1% (top) of all samples. Almost all were with the hand at rest (one run of 1,872 samples looks like a controller set down). With the hand moving faster than 2 m/s, repeats were 0.01% (bottom) and 1.1% (top) [D]. In free skates the rig motion masks exact repeats, so they can't be counted there.

**Hands.**

| Session | Bottom hand peak, runtime | Top hand peak | Rotation max (top / bottom) | Hand accel p99.99 (bottom) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sandbox 11-07 | 9.1 m/s | 5.4 m/s | 2,713 / 2,113 °/s | 24 g |
| free skate 11-10 06:00 | 9.0 m/s | 4.2 m/s | 2,675 / 2,343 °/s | 21 g |
| free skate 11-10 12:08 | 8.7 m/s | 4.5 m/s | 2,786 / 1,948 °/s | 20 g |

**Tracking state.** With the blade above 10 m/s (20 ms speed), both hands were fully tracked 98.3% (sandbox), 99.6% (12:08) and 99.9% (06:00) of the time. "Inferred" runs: 4-11 per hand per session, median 0.2-15 s long, none starting with the hand above 3 m/s. The first tracked tick after a run moved the controller by a median of 1.3-7.2 mm and at most 30 mm [D].

**Blade velocity estimators.** "Tick-to-tick" is the p99 change in the blade's velocity vector from one 1 ms tick to the next. "Delay" is the time shift that best matches a centred 20 ms reference. Both hands tracked, no jumps, sandbox and 12:08 pooled [D]:

- The blade's speed changed by p99 3,250-4,160 m/s² and p99.9 9,700-15,300 m/s² per tick under 1 ms differences, against p99 330-380 and p99.9 1,040-1,090 m/s² under the runtime velocity [D]. The second set is physically plausible; the first is noise.
- The runtime velocity follows the pose-derived velocity within 0-4 ms (mean error 0.17-1.73 m/s per hand) in the unpredicted sessions [D, a second alignment method]. At hand speeds of 6-20 m/s its ratio to the 20 ms speed was p1 0.99 and p99 1.32 in the sandbox session: no dropouts [D].
- Where position jumps without motion (recentring, snap turns), the runtime velocity stays near 0 while the 20 ms position speed reads 6-65 m/s. Two cases: top hand in the 12:08 session (81 samples, median ratio 0.02) and the sandbox session (56 samples, p99 ratio 0.15) [D]. That is a clean jump detector.

- 19 of 117 shots (16%) at 12:08 and 7 of 69 (10%) at 06:00 left at 30 m/s (67 mph) or more [D].
- On hard shots the puck left at 0.95-1.09 times the blade's instantaneous peak (1 ms) and 1.18-1.29 times its 20 ms speed [D].
- The 105 mph shot (puck 47.0 m/s): blade 37.8 m/s at 20 ms, 47.1 m/s runtime, 41.6 m/s at 1 ms; hands 0.19 m apart [D].
- Of the eight shots over 80 mph at 12:08, six had 20 ms blade speeds of 30-38 m/s with hands 0.18-0.23 m apart. Two (81 and 82 mph) had blades of only 22-27 m/s with hands 0.30-0.31 m apart [D]. Something other than blade speed added the extra there, such as a strike from a distance or the contact itself; that belongs to the contact physics, not tracking.
- The fastest swing in that session (t = 872.5 s): over 60 ms the 20 ms blade speed rose from 24.6 to 44.1 m/s. The bottom hand moved at 7.4-7.9 m/s while the top hand slowed from 3.5 to 1.8 m/s, and the hands closed from 0.25 to 0.20 m apart. That is a swing of about 33 rad/s with the blade rising from the ice to ~1.8 m. Both hands were fully tracked, and all three estimators agree within 10% [D]. This is a real flick, not a tracking error.

**The launches are a separate problem.** Puck launches with vertical speed above 6 m/s (101 at 12:08, 48 at 06:00, as recorded) had a median blade speed of 16.6-17.7 m/s in the 60 ms before them, the blade rising at 6.0-6.4 m/s, and hands 0.26-0.30 m apart. The puck's median peak vertical speed was 8.7-8.8 m/s [D]. They are mid-speed follow-through scoops, not the fast flicks behind the speed tail.

## 5. Real sticks, for comparison

- **Blade speed at impact, stationary slap shots** (5 elite, 5 recreational; Polhemus electromagnetic tracking at 60 Hz): 29.14 ± 1.39 m/s elite, 26.46 ± 0.66 m/s recreational. The part from the stick's angular motion was 16.00 m/s (elite) and 17.38 m/s (recreational); the part from translation was 13.14 and 9.08 m/s ([Woo, Loh, Turcotte & Pearsall, ISBS 2004](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/1368)) [M]. Conditions added in fact-check: the elite players were from a university varsity team, the recreational players played under twice a week, all shot from a lab platform covered in polyethylene, with a wooden Bauer Supreme 3030 stick, tracked at 24 and 145 cm from the heel. The translation parts are the totals minus the rotation parts ("Thus 13.14 m/s and 9.08 m/s were attributed to..."). At 60 Hz a frame is 17 ms, about the length of a puck contact, so the peak blade speed may be under-sampled [E]. If that rotation acts over the same ~1.4 m lever, it is a swing of about 11.4-12.4 rad/s [D].
- **Blade-tip speed through a pro shot (added in fact-check).** Two pros on ice, a marker at the blade tip, 300 Hz. Read by eye from Fig. 2 of [Swarén et al. 2019](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2019/83516/83516.pdf), so ±2-3 m/s. The tip peaks at about 22-28 m/s just before the blade meets the ice and drops to about 10-17 m/s at puck contact in slap shots (lower in one-timers). It peaks again at about 27-35 m/s (slap shots) and up to about 37 m/s (one-timers), some 40 ms after puck contact, once the puck has left [M, read off figure]. The pucks left at 36-38 m/s. So even pros' blade tips are well under 30 m/s at the moment of puck contact. Above 30 m/s comes only in the recoil after release, which in the sim belongs to the flex model, not to the rigid stick. Their blade went from about 25 to 12 m/s in about 30 ms, then back up to 34 m/s in about 40 ms: roughly 400-600 m/s² [D, from the figure].
- **Puck speeds:** recreational slap shots 22.3-23.3 m/s, elite slap shots 33.3 m/s; elite wrist shots 27-29 m/s; recreational wrist shots about 17-20 m/s (an unsupported estimate) (our `stick and puck contact.md`) [M/E]. Cross-study ratios of puck to blade speed: 22.3 / 26.5 ≈ 0.84 recreational and 33.3 / 29.1 ≈ 1.14 elite [D].
- **Contact time on the blade:** slap shots 27 ± 5 ms recreational and 38 ± 9 ms elite ([Villaseñor et al. 2006](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jab/22/3/article-p202.xml)) [M]. Those were 4 elite and 5 recreational men in a lab, shooting on polyethylene, with an accelerometer inside the puck. Two professionals on ice: 20 ± 2 ms slap shot and 15 ± 2 ms one-timer, with 20 Qualisys cameras at 300 Hz ([Swarén et al. 2019](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2019/83516/83516.pdf)) [M]. Wrist shots: "about 100ms or more", an estimate the authors use for a rough force calculation, not a measurement ([Plesch et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.02635)) [E] (retagged from [S] in fact-check).
- **Hand forces** (corrected in fact-check from the thesis itself): 41 players, 32 pressure sensors on the shaft, lab synthetic ice. In slap shots by high-calibre men, the group-mean peak force on one face of the shaft was 15-62 N. The largest was the lower hand's toe-up face, at 61.9 N. The four faces sum to 118 N for the lower hand and 91 N for the upper hand [D, sum of Table 6 means; the peaks need not coincide]. The "160 N" is only the top of the colour scale in Figure 36 for total grip force, not a measured value. In wrist shots, the lower hand's toe-up face peaked at 27-43 N across the four groups, 42.7 N for high-calibre men ([Zane 2012, McGill](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d), Tables 6-7) [M]. These are squeezing forces on the faces, not the net sideways force of each hand. The net force in the swing direction is at most the larger face force [E].
- **Why a real stick can't be flicked like a controller [E].** WSU measured stick moments of inertia of 0.160-0.213 kg·m² about a pivot 0.89 m up from the blade ([Anderson & Smith](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)) [M]. Moved to the top hand (about 1.4 m from the blade) by the parallel-axis rule, assuming 0.45 kg with its centre of mass 0.6-0.7 m up from the blade, that is about 0.35-0.45 kg·m². Assume the hands drive the stick as a force couple. In the fastest recorded swing the swing rate rose from about 17 to 33 rad/s in 50 ms (α ≈ 320 rad/s²). On a real stick that needs about 110-145 N·m. With the hands 0.20 m apart, that is a couple of 560-720 N. Even the median shot over 30 m/s (22 rad/s, if reached within 0.1 s) needs about 330-430 N at 0.23 m. That is about 3-8 times the measured per-hand sum of face forces (91-118 N), and 5-12 times the largest single-face mean (62 N) (corrected in fact-check from "2-4.5 times the highest total hand force", which used a colour-scale maximum). Even about the stick's centre of mass, the smallest possible moment of inertia (about 0.12-0.20 kg·m² on the same assumptions), the fastest swing needs a 190-320 N couple [E, added in fact-check]. Real shooters get their extra blade speed from translation (9-13 m/s), with long wind-ups and body weight, not from wrist flicks.

## 6. Estimating implement velocity: methods and what they cost

Formulas [D], for samples h apart with white position noise σ:

| Method | Delay | Noise or bias | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-sample difference | h/2 | SD √2·σ/h (1.4 m/s per mm of noise at 1 ms) | What the sim uses (`vb_r = (rigid_bot - last_bot)/dt`) |
| Secant over window W | W/2 | SD √2·σ/W; lags by a·W/2 under acceleration a | At the blade's p99 acceleration (~350 m/s²): 0.7 m/s low at W = 4 ms, 1.4 m/s at 8 ms; at 1,000 m/s²: 2 and 4 m/s |
| Least-squares line over N samples | (N−1)h/2 | SD σ / (h·√(N(N²−1)/12)); N = 10 is about 13 times quieter than a 1-sample difference | Same delay as a secant, a bit quieter. Needs true timestamps: the sampler's spacing varies from 0.4 to 2.5 ms |
| Savitzky-Golay | as the window centre | smooths velocity and acceleration | LaValle used it on gyro rates for prediction ([blog](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/the-latent-power-of-prediction/)) [V] |
| Adaptive window (FOAW) | variable | widest window whose line fit stays inside the noise band | Built for haptic devices ([Janabi-Sharifi, Hayward & Chen 2000, IEEE TCST 8:1003](https://doi.org/10.1109/87.880606)) [M, abstract] |
| 1€ filter | small at speed, larger at rest | cutoff = fcmin + β·\|speed\| | See below |
| Double exponential (DESP), Kalman | ~1-2 frames | overshoot "when the pointer abruptly decelerates" (DESP at α = 0.06, in [Casiez et al. 2012](https://gery.casiez.net/publications/CHI2012-casiez.pdf); attribution corrected in fact-check) | DESP runs about 135 times faster than a Kalman predictor with "equivalent prediction performance" ([LaViola 2003](https://www.eecs.ucf.edu/~jjl/pubs/vr2003_laviola.pdf)) [M] |
| Runtime `XrSpaceVelocity` | 2-9 ms in Jon's data [D] | smooth: as quiet as an 8 ms secant [D] | Already logged by `record.Sampler`, unused by the physics |

- **The 1€ filter** (Casiez, Roussel & Vogel, CHI 2012) [M]: a first-order low-pass whose cutoff rises with speed. The derivative behind that speed is itself low-passed at a fixed 1 Hz. Tuning: set β = 0 and fcmin ≈ 1 Hz while holding still, then raise β during fast moves. Their pointer test matched a 14-sample moving average's jitter, using fcmin 1 Hz and β 0.007. Their budget: "Jitter should be less than 1mm mean-to-peak, but lag should be below 60 ms", and with 40-50 ms of system lag "that leaves less than 10-20 ms for the filter" ([paper](https://gery.casiez.net/publications/CHI2012-casiez.pdf); [page](https://gery.casiez.net/1euro/)). **For a contact model this is the wrong way round [D].** The 1 ms spikes grow with speed (13 m/s per tick at shot speeds against 1.5 at handling speeds), but the 1€ cutoff opens with speed, so the spikes pass through.
- **Outlier rejection.** A Hampel filter replaces a sample with the window median when it lies far from it ([Pearson et al. 2016, EURASIP JASP](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13634-016-0383-6)) [M]. Meta's Interaction SDK selects throw velocity from a rolling buffer by RANSAC consensus. Defaults: 10 samples (8 in v68), the 2 newest excluded "from the consensus", and at least 2 high-confidence samples. Synthetic poses that fill tracking gaps are capped by `MaxSyntheticSpeed` ([Meta ISDK v201](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/reference/interaction/v201/class_oculus_interaction_throw_r_a_n_s_a_c_velocity); [v68](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/reference/interaction/v68/class_oculus_interaction_throw_r_a_n_s_a_c_velocity/)) [V].
- **Known failure: spikes after jumps.** Developers saw "gigantic velocities" from Meta's StandardVelocityCalculator on release. One cause was that "the poses are currently stored in world space, so any teleport will immediately make velocity really large". The workaround was to clamp the change per update with `MoveTowards` ([Meta forum, 2023](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/unity-development-123/randomly-large-velocities-from-standard-velocity-calculator-but-only-in-apk-126495)) [V]. The sim also differences world-space poses that include the rig's motion; its `JUMP_M` guard covers snap turns and teleports.
- **Release velocity in VR throwing:**
  - Unity XR Interaction Toolkit averages samples over a "throw smoothing duration" (0.25 s in the docs' example), up to 20 frames, weighted by a curve that is flat by default ([Unity XRI](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.xr.interaction.toolkit@3.0/api/UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Interactables.XRGrabInteractable.html)) [V].
  - One shipped game's developer rejected a moving average ("averages the slow wind-up with the fastest part of the release") and peak velocity (throws "too powerful"). He fitted a linear regression over the last few frames (4 in his debug view), measured at the hand's centre of mass ([Deck, Game Developer 2016](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/why-throwing-in-vr-sucks-and-how-to-make-it-better)) [V].
  - Another found the runtime's velocities more consistent than any pose-derived estimate, and took the release velocity at any point on the controller from them ([NormalVR 2016](https://www.normalvr.com/blog/throwing-throw-down/)) [V].
  - A GI '24 study used backward differences over 5 frames for controllers and 10 for trackers ([Ghasemaghaei et al. 2024](https://www.cs.ucf.edu/~jjl/pubs/gi24-16final.pdf)) [M, method].
- **When release timing is noticed.** Observers (n = 34) could not reliably tell release-point shifts of ±10 ms from the original throw. Shifts of ±20 to ±50 ms differed significantly ([Yamac & O'Sullivan, FauxThrow 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02166)) [M]. That was third-person video of a throw, not first-person play.
- **Light implements over-reward flicks.** "When I flick my paddle wrist the ball goes flying very far". Scaling the paddle's velocity down "only scales the problem down" ([Unity forum 2022](https://discussions.unity.com/t/unrealistic-ball-and-paddle-physics/893735)) [V]. "Sometimes even a flick of the wrist would cause insanely high forces" ([Unity forum 2024](https://discussions.unity.com/t/vr-table-tennis-physics/946361)) [V]. A golf developer said "the controller for the Meta Quest is very light" ([UploadVR](https://uploadvr.com/ultimate-swing-golf-interview)) [V]. These match section 4: virtual implements have no inertia to stop a wrist flick.

- No engine or shipped title I found documents a maximum contact time, a "release" rule for an implement carrying a ball, or its velocity estimator for hits. Throwing has documented release rules (trigger release, a trigger falling 20% below its peak, a distance threshold); hitting does not [D].
- Ball sports barely need such a rule: ball contacts last a few ms ("four or five milliseconds" in tennis, per Racket Club's lead) [V]. Hockey shots keep the puck on the blade for 15-38 ms (slap shots, one-timers) [M] and an estimated ~100 ms (wrist shots) [E] (retagged from [S] in fact-check). So the sim cannot borrow a ball-game rule that ends contact after one impulse [D].

## 8. Handling tracking jumps

- **Spec:** while position is inferred, POSITION_VALID stays set and POSITION_TRACKED clears ([OpenXR](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenXR/specs/1.1/html/xrspec.html)) [M]. The sim requires the VALID bits only (`NEED_POSE` in record.py), so inferred poses drive the blade [D, code].
- **Meta ISDK:** speed-capped synthetic poses "fill tracking gaps", for example when a tracked hand is not high-confidence, and the consensus needs at least 2 high-confidence samples ([Meta ISDK](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/reference/interaction/v201/class_oculus_interaction_throw_r_a_n_s_a_c_velocity)) [V]. The page does not say that low-confidence samples are replaced (softened in fact-check).
- **SteamVR:** controller motion beyond an assumed human limit is treated as implausible. Valve raised that limit after Beat Saber experts exceeded it ([Road to VR](https://roadtovr.com/?p=168587)) [V].
- **Teleports:** keep the history in tracking space, not world space, or clear it on a teleport ([Meta forum](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/unity-development-123/randomly-large-velocities-from-standard-velocity-calculator-but-only-in-apk-126495)) [V]. The sim clears its sweep when the blade jumps more than 0.2 m in one tick (`JUMP_M`).
- **Jon's data [D]:** re-acquisition jumps are at most 30 mm. Inferred runs never started during fast motion. Non-physical jumps show near-zero runtime velocity. A 0.2 m guard per 1 ms tick lets through anything below 200 m/s, but no real blade moves faster than about 47 m/s (4.7 cm per tick) in his data.

**Order of work, cheapest first [E]:**

1. **Compute the contact velocity from a 4-8 ms window** of the blade points (least squares over true timestamps), or from the runtime's `XrSpaceVelocity` (v_top + ω-derived swing). Keep the raw poses for the swept contact. Cross-check the two against each other: where they disagree by more than about 3 times, treat it as a jump. Expected effect: per-tick noise down 4-9 times; the shot tail down only 2-12%.
2. **Add a physical ceiling for the missing stick inertia.** Two honest options. (a) Cap the rigid blade's speed at contact at about 30 m/s. (b) Better: limit the shaft's angular acceleration about the top hand to τ_max/I, with I ≈ 0.35-0.45 kg·m² and τ_max set from measured hand forces. Either way, the blade lags the hands only during flicks no real stick could do. In ordinary handling and his realistic shots (swing ≤ 12-16 rad/s) nothing changes. Option (b) is a "physics stick", which the earlier report warned can feel floaty, so A/B it blind.
3. **Tighten the jump guard and use the true dt.**
4. **A/B a 5-10 ms look-back on the sampler** (`lag_ms` already exists in `record.Sampler`) and re-measure the table in section 4.
5. **Re-run these numbers with the sim's own grab model** (`grab.replay`) before changing anything. My blade point is a 1.40 m approximation.

## Gaps

- **Touch Plus at speed is unmeasured.** No study gives accuracy, latency or dropout rate of Quest 3 controllers in fast motion. Meta publishes neither the IMU ranges its firmware selects, nor the rate at which Link sends controller poses, nor whether `XrSpaceVelocity` is raw gyro, filtered, or predicted. My 2-9 ms lag estimate for the runtime velocity is indirect.
- **Shipped VR sports titles document nothing about contact.** Eleven, Golf+, Racket Club, WIN Reality and the hockey titles publish nothing on hit velocity estimation, cooldowns, carry or release rules, or tracking-jump handling. The only code-level sources are Meta's and Unity's throw APIs and hobby scripts.
- **Real stick kinematics outside slap shots.** No measured stick angular velocity or blade speed for wrist or snap shots. The Woo slap-shot data are n = 5 + 5 at 60 Hz. Wrist-shot puck contact time is unmeasured (the ~100 ms is Plesch et al.'s rough estimate). So are the shortest real interval between stickhandling touches and the hand couple players can produce with the hands close together.
- **Hand spacing.** In his fastest shots the controllers were only 19-23 cm apart. Nobody has measured real hand spacing during shots. Shots with this spacing also need checking against his fitted grip.
- **My analysis is approximate.** The blade is a point 1.40 m along the two-hand line. Puck speeds are from the recorded physics, which changed during 2026-11-10. Repeats could only be counted in the standing session.

### What changes for the sim

- **The ~30 m/s ceiling on the rigid blade at contact stands, and is stronger.** On ice, even pros' blade tips are at 10-17 m/s when the puck is struck. Speeds over 30 m/s come only from recoil, which the sim's flex model already supplies. Pros' blade-tip accelerations read off Swarén's figure are about 400-600 m/s², which supports a plausibility gate of about 1,000-2,000 m/s² per tick.
- **Don't set τ_max from "160 N".** If the shaft's angular acceleration is limited, start from the measured face forces: tens of N per hand, with the hands' spacing as the lever. These are squeeze forces, and the wrists can add torque, so treat any τ_max as an A/B starting point, not a measured limit.
- **No contact-time cap below ~100 ms still holds**, but the wrist-shot ~100 ms is one author's estimate. The measured floor is 15-38 ms (slap shots and one-timers).
- **The 5-10 ms look-back A/B still holds.** But the 50 ms window is counted back from the last pose that reached the PC, so log how old that pose is (if the runtime exposes it) before reading anything into the result.
