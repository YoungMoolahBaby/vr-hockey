# How real hockey players feel and control the puck through the stick (hands -> stick -> puck)

Scope: the real-world human side (what the hands sense, what they do, which cues matter), to guide which cues a controller-only VR trainer should reproduce or substitute. Ice hockey evidence first; other sports only where hockey evidence is thin (flagged). Source quality is marked: [peer-reviewed], [thesis] (McGill/Pearsall-lab MSc theses, supervised but not journal-reviewed), [conf] (conference/proceedings), [coaching] (practitioner opinion), [vendor/retail] (marketing claims), [secondary] (a finding I only saw quoted in another source).

## 1. What a player feels through the stick: impact transients, vibration, puck weight and "cupping", blade-ice contact, shaft flex and load

### Takeaway
The measured signals are: a puck-blade contact of roughly 25-40 ms in shots (forces peak in the first ~10 ms), stick bending modes around 47-60 Hz and 116-161 Hz plus higher bending/torsional modes around 210-260 Hz that sit right in the hand's most vibration-sensitive band (200-400 Hz), and composite sticks ringing longer than wood (wood damps 2-4x more). Yet no study has found a strong link between measured stick vibration and players' sense of "feel", and none measured vibration at the hands during real play. Blade-on-ice scraping and friction have essentially no measurement literature.

### Cited Findings
**Puck properties (what is on the other end of the stick)**
- Puck: vulcanized rubber disc, 3 in (76 mm) diameter, 1 in (25 mm) thick, 5.5-6 oz (about 156-170 g). Drop-test coefficient of restitution (COR) is 0.45-0.55 at room temperature and 0.12-0.27 frozen. Pucks are frozen before play "to reduce bouncing and increase controllability" [conf] — [Anderson & Smith, WSU, "Experimental Characterization of Ice Hockey Sticks and Pucks" (c. 2007)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)
- At game-like speed (55 mph cannon), freezing cut puck COR by 32% and raised dynamic stiffness by over 600%. COR fell linearly with impact speed while stiffness rose with speed, so the puck "behaves as a non-linear spring". Two brands of recreational puck differed by 10% in COR and 33% in stiffness [conf] — [Anderson & Smith (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)
- Puck hardness (pucks at 22 °C vs -4 °C) had no measurable effect on stick performance. The authors attribute this to the "relatively long contact duration" of puck-stick impacts, which minimizes the trampoline effect that dominates in golf and baseball [conf] — [Smith & Bigford, "Laboratory measurements of ice hockey stick performance" (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/laboratory-measurements-of-ice-hockey-stick-performance.pdf/)

**Contact transients in shots**
- In a slap shot by an "upper level recreational player" (1000 fps video), the puck and stick were in contact for 32 ms. In the first 10 ms the stick speed drops sharply and then rises sharply; puck-stick forces are largest then, as the puck is accelerated to stick speed. During the remaining 22 ms the stick "unloads", adding speed to the puck [conf] — [Smith & Bigford (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/laboratory-measurements-of-ice-hockey-stick-performance.pdf/)
- In slap shots measured with a triaxial accelerometer inside the puck, blade-puck contact time was longer for elite than recreational players (38 ± 9 ms vs 27 ± 5 ms) with similar puck acceleration, and elite players reached higher puck speeds (120 ± 18 vs 80.3 ± 11.6 km/h) [peer-reviewed; numbers from the paper's results table as indexed] — [Villaseñor, Turcotte & Pearsall 2006, "Recoil effect of the ice hockey stick during a slap shot", J Appl Biomech 22(3)](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jab/22/3/article-p202.xml); [results table](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Main-Results-in-Puck-Acceleration-Puck-Velocity-Blade-Puck-Contact-Time-and-Stick_tbl2_6585109)
- In a slap shot "the stick contacts the ice slightly before the puck", loading the shaft. The stick acts as a beam in three-point bending: the upper hand and the ice are the outer supports and the lower hand applies the central load [conf] — [Smith & Bigford (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/laboratory-measurements-of-ice-hockey-stick-performance.pdf/)

**Stick vibration modes and damping**
- Modal analysis (roving impact hammer plus accelerometer on the shaft) of wood and composite player sticks: first bending mode 47 Hz (a wood stick, the lowest) to 60 Hz (a composite, the highest); second bending mode 116-161 Hz. There was little difference between composite and wood in the first two bending modes [conf] — [Anderson & Smith (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)
- Same study: wood sticks had the lowest frequencies and "are reported to provide the best feel for the puck", followed by high-end tapered composites, which are said to feel better than mid/low-end composites. But "a strong correlation between the measured vibrational response and player perception of feel was not observed" [conf] — [Anderson & Smith (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)
- Goalie sticks (experimental modal analysis, accelerometer on the blade toe): modes of interest at 261 Hz (bending) and 213 Hz (torsional) in a composite stick. Composite frequencies tend to be higher than wood, and wood damping rates are "between two and four times larger than composite." The modes lie "close to [the] range of 200-400 Hz where the hands are more sensitive to vibrational stimuli." Mode shapes with large amplitude near the grip contribute to "sting." A full roll of tape on the butt end lowered frequencies, raised damping and moved the bending node 7-8 in up the shaft (vs about 2 in without tape) [conf, acoustics meeting abstract] — [Russell et al., "Vibrational assessment of ice hockey goalie sticks", ASA 162nd meeting](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/162nd/Russell_3aED.html)
- Goalies' preference for wood because it "stings less" is reported as anecdotal. The authors cite baseball-bat research in which higher damping is perceived as less sting, and suggest players prefer lower frequencies and more damping [conf; the bat claim is secondary] — [Russell et al. (ASA)](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/162nd/Russell_3aED.html)
- A vibration absorber added to a composite goalie stick made the vibration signal decay faster than on the stick without one [peer-reviewed, abstract only seen] — [Implementation of a vibration absorber for composite hockey goalie sticks (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257724791_Implementation_of_a_vibration_absorber_for_composite_hockey_goalie_sticks)

**Blade surface (tape) and contact quality**
- Blade covering changes shots measurably. Thirteen players, from amateurs to NHL players, shot with a hard, rough adhesive blade cover (Specter) and with traditional cloth tape (Renfrew). The harder, rougher cover raised wrist-shot speed by about 2.8% and puck rotation by about 5.6%, and slap-shot speed by about 2.4% and rotation by about 6.4%. The authors attribute this to "lower loss in damping" and a rougher surface [preprint, physics.pop-ph; small n; my reading of a garbled table] — [Plesch, Plesník & Ružičková 2019, arXiv:1903.02635](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.02635)
- Coaching definition of "cupping": closing the blade (tilting its top over the puck) so the puck does not bounce off the face [coaching] — [How To Hockey / coaching summaries](https://howtohockey.com/never-miss-pass/)
- Forehand vs backhand geometry: the curved blade's concave forehand face can "cradle" the puck, while the convex backhand face cannot, so the puck "is presumably more difficult to control" and is more likely to roll off [thesis, interpretation] — [Lawrie 2014, McGill MSc thesis](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)

**Shaft load felt at the hands (shots)**
- Grip forces measured at 1000 Hz with 32 piezoresistive sensors under both hands (41 players; shaft stiffness 77, 87 and 102 flex): high-calibre slap shooters loaded the lower hand first, then the upper hand. Colour-map scales for total hand force run to 160 N in high-calibre males. In the wrist shot, high-calibre males averaged peaks of 42.7 N on the lower hand's toe-up face and 32.3 N on the upper hand's toe-down face [thesis] — [Zane 2012, "Force measures at the hand-stick interface during ice hockey slap and wrist shots", McGill MSc](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d)

### Inferences
- The "heavy rubber puck vs light composite stick" character has a measurable basis. The puck is a lossy, nonlinear, speed-stiffening spring (COR about 0.5 warm and far lower frozen), so impacts should read as a dull, short thud rather than a bouncy click. The light composite stick is lightly damped (wood damps 2-4x more), so a short ring around 50-60 Hz (first bending), 120-160 Hz (second) and 200-260 Hz (higher modes) is plausible after a hit.
- Ordinary passes and stickhandling taps carry far less energy than slap shots and have no published contact durations. The 25-40 ms figures come only from slap shots, which also involve shaft pre-loading.
- Because a measured vibration-to-feel link was not found even in lab work, the "feel" players describe is probably a composite of force and load at the hands, timing, sound and visual outcome, not vibration spectrum alone. This supports a VR design that layers cues (sound plus a short vibration transient plus visual flex) instead of trying to match a stick's vibration spectrum exactly.
- Real players wear thick padded gloves, which likely attenuate the higher frequencies (an inference: I found no measurement of hockey-glove vibration transmission). The "feel" real players get is therefore already a filtered, low-bandwidth signal, which makes controller-grade vibration a less unrealistic substitute than it first appears.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures vibration at the hands, or under the glove, during real stickhandling, passing or receiving. All vibration data are modal tests or shots.
- No measurement found of blade-on-ice friction, scraping vibration or sound, or of how players use them. This is a clear evidence gap.
- No data found on the contact duration or force of soft receptions or stickhandling taps.
- Smith & Bigford found wood sticks performed 10% better than composite in their idealized cannon test, while Anderson & Smith (same lab) reported composite performance "significantly greater than wood." Both are WSU conference papers. The conflict is unresolved, and it concerns performance, not feel.

## 2. Roles of the top and bottom hands: stickhandling, passing, receiving, shooting, grip pressure and sliding, and one-handed play

### Takeaway
Coaching consensus, partly backed by grip-force measurement, is that the top hand is the control hand (it steers the blade, rotating it for cupping and forehand-backhand changes) and the bottom hand is a loose, sliding support and guidance hand that becomes the "power" hand in shots. In skilled shooters the two hands act as an opposed force couple that bends the shaft and then clamps it during recoil. Low-calibre players lack this coupling.

### Cited Findings
- Top hand = control hand: it "initiates the blade's movements, whether cupping the puck or spinning it on and off the blade." Bottom hand = support hand: "provide support, guidance, and stability when puckhandling" and "should be easily slid up and down the hockey stick's shaft." Poor reception control is "almost always driven by a bottom hand that grips the stick too tight." The bottom hand becomes the "power hand" in passing and shooting [coaching] — [Revak, "Puckhandling technique: top & bottom hands", Hockey IQ newsletter](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/puckhandling-technique-top-and-bottom-hands)
- In shooting, coaching sources describe the top hand pulling back while the bottom hand pushes down and through, flexing the stick [coaching] — [Hockey IQ, "Shooting: top & bottom hands"](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/shooting-top-and-bottom-hands); [Ice Hockey Systems, "Improve your wrist shot"](https://www.icehockeysystems.com/skill-development-videos/improve-your-wrist-shot)
- Measured bimanual force couple in slap shots (high-calibre players): at ice contact the lower hand's lagging/toe-up faces and the upper hand's leading/toe-down faces load together ("cupping" force groups), giving three-point bending with the lower hand acting against the upper hand and the ice. During recoil the pairing flips (lower leading/toe-down, upper lagging/toe-up) to "clamp" the stick. The two hands' forces act "in equal but opposite directions". Low-calibre players "did not show this distinct coupling," suggesting they "did not effectively take advantage of the stick elastic flexion properties" [thesis] — [Zane 2012, McGill](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d)
- Zane also measured: large toe-up and toe-down forces at ice contact, interpreted as pressing the blade into the ice for friction and as clamping to stop the stick being dislodged from the hands. Each player showed a unique, highly repeatable force "signature." Stick stiffness (77/87/102) did not change hand forces or shot velocity. Wrist-shot lower-hand peaks were 29-42 N (toe-up) and 8-19 N (lagging), comparable to Wu et al.'s 37-51 N vertical blade forces [thesis] — [Zane 2012](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d)
- Moving the bottom hand lower on the shaft, even below its midpoint, produced more stick bend, more strain energy and higher puck speed [peer-reviewed, seen secondhand] — [Wu et al. 2003, Sports Engineering, as reviewed in "Biomechanics of ice hockey slap shots: which stick is best?", The Sport Journal](https://thesportjournal.org/article/biomechanics-of-ice-hockey-slap-shots-which-stick-is-best/)
- In stickhandling (figure-8 around two obstacles), the stick behaves as a lever pivoting about the hands. Varsity players amplified velocity from the butt (top hand) to the blade much more than recreational players did: blade-minus-butt peak velocity was 2.08 vs 1.26 m/s on the forehand cross and 2.27 vs 1.61 m/s on the backhand. The butt moved least of all markers in both groups (peak velocity order butt < puck < blade). Varsity players also lifted the butt (top hand) higher [thesis] — [Lawrie 2014, McGill](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)
- The top hand drifting toward the body changes blade angle and is cited as the biggest receiving mistake: "keep the top hand away from the body" [coaching] — [How To Hockey, "Never miss a pass"](https://howtohockey.com/never-miss-pass/)
- Bimanual-control background used in the hockey literature: the non-dominant limb's motion breaks down first as rhythmic bimanual speed rises (in clapping, the ceiling was 6.92 ± 0.56 Hz, at which the non-preferred limb effectively stopped), and extensive practice extends stable bimanual patterns to higher speeds [secondary, cited in thesis] — [Lawrie 2014, citing Morrison et al. 2009](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)

### Inferences
- Mapping two controllers to one stick: the top (right, for a left shot) controller should own blade orientation (roll of the shaft = blade open/closed, which is cupping) and most of the steering. The bottom (left) controller acts as a guide point whose position along the shaft can change, ideally a soft constraint the stick can slide through (as the real bottom hand slides) rather than a rigid weld.
- Shot loading needs a force couple. In the real stick the hands push in opposite directions while the blade is held to the ice. In VR, shaft bend could be driven by the relative motion of the two controllers against blade-ice contact (top hand pulling back while bottom hand drives forward and down). Recreational players often fail to produce this couple, so a trainer could show it (for example, as a visual bend indicator) as a teachable skill.
- The top hand moves least in stickhandling (it is near the pivot) while the blade moves 1-2 m/s faster. Small top-hand rotations become large blade motions, so controller rotation noise and latency at the top hand are amplified at the blade. Rotation filtering is a precision priority.
- One-handed play: the top hand alone holds the stick for poke checks and reach (see Gaps; coaching only). Letting the right controller hold the stick alone and pivot it from the butt matches real play.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed measurement of grip pressure or hand forces during stickhandling, passing or receiving. Zane 2012 covers only shots.
- I found no study of how the bottom hand slides along the shaft (how often, how far, in which skills). Only coaching statements exist.
- I found no research on one-handed play (poke check, stick lift, one-hand puck protection). It appears only in coaching material, which I did not retrieve in detail.
- I found nothing specific to left-shot players or to short (5'8") adults beyond standard stick-length coaching.

## 3. Motor control: touch and proprioception vs vision, expert vs novice differences, and degraded haptic feedback

### Takeaway
Hockey has almost no controlled evidence on how much stickhandling relies on touch versus vision. One lab study found that changing a stick's inertia (heavier, up to +25% moment of inertia) left stickhandling kinematics nearly unchanged even though every subject felt the change, which the author read as vision dominating the haptic cue. Experts differ from recreational players in speed, forward puck position and a "volley-and-catch" style (the blade moves faster than the puck). Heads-up puck handling, with the puck in the lower periphery, is a coaching ideal rather than a measured fact. General motor-control science says touch confirms contact and triggers the next motor command.

### Cited Findings
**Expert vs recreational stickhandling kinematics (the only 3D study found)**
- Twenty-seven males (13 varsity/junior-plus, 14 recreational) did a figure-8 puck-cycling task on synthetic ice (Bauer One100 stick, 445 g, P88 blade), with Vicon capture at 240 Hz. Varsity players completed a loop 18% faster (2.24 ± 0.30 s vs 2.76 ± 0.52 s) without more errors. They were 25% faster in the forehand-to-backhand ("right") phase, carried the puck pattern about 5 cm further forward, gave obstacles about 30% less clearance, and reached higher peak stick and puck velocities and accelerations [thesis] — [Lawrie 2014, McGill](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)
- For varsity players the blade moved much faster than the puck at peak velocity (blade-minus-puck 0.61 m/s vs 0.08 m/s for recreational players on the forehand cross; 0.57 vs 0.26 m/s on the backhand). Recreational players' puck closely followed the blade ("plough-push"). Varsity players' did not, consistent with a hypothesized "volley-and-catch" technique of striking and then receiving the puck. The puck paths actually fell into four patterns (rectilinear, curvilinear and two hybrids) [thesis] — [Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)
- Recreational players slowed most at the forehand-to-backhand transition, interpreted as speed traded for accuracy because the convex backhand face cannot cradle the puck [thesis] — [Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)

**Haptic vs visual cues about the stick**
- Adding mass (moment of inertia up to about +25%, roughly 110 g) at the blade end, the middle or the butt had a negligible effect on stickhandling kinematics. "All subjects reported being able to detect the inertial change in the stick, but it appears that visual cues overruled the haptic cues." Subjects could see where the weight was. The author speculates that weight distribution might matter more "when vision is compromised", for example when looking at teammates [thesis] — [Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)
- Dynamic touch with hockey sticks: people with no hockey knowledge could perceive mass-distribution differences just by wielding the sticks, judged distal-weighted sticks best for power and butt-weighted ones best for precision, and kept these ratings after using them. Experts preferred the butt-weighted (lightest-feeling) stick for both power and receiving, citing stickhandling, and named shaft "flex" as the second most important factor [peer-reviewed, secondary summary] — [Hove, Riley & Shockley 2006, as summarized in Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)
- Grip-force control science: in lift tasks, grip force and its rate are set by skin-surface friction, and "motor commands to lift the object were released once tactile signals verified appropriate contact." The grip "safety margin" stays consistent across loads and surfaces [peer-reviewed, secondary] — [Johansson & Westling 1984/1987, as summarized in Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)
- Tool-length and mass perception: in reaching with rods, people were "highly sensitive to the length of the tool and moderately affected by the mass," and a more distal centre of mass changed posture and stopping distance [peer-reviewed, secondary] — [Bongers, Smitsman & Michaels 2003, as summarized in Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)

**Vision and gaze**
- In a VR puck-hitting task (HTC Vive Pro Eye, real stick on plastic ice, pucks up to 170 km/h), 13 hockey players, 9 wrestlers and 9 controls all tracked the puck mostly in the central (0-5°) and near-peripheral (5-15°) retina. Hockey players tracked longer in the near periphery, especially on misses, and unlike the other groups did not raise their central-vision share when they only watched rather than hit. The authors conclude the optimal strategy is "parafoveal tracking in the near periphery (5-15°) and partial foveal tracking (0-5°)" [peer-reviewed, small n, VR with 110° FOV, about interception not dribbling] — [Polikanova et al. 2024, Psychology in Russia 17(3)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11574569/)
- In defensive play, elite and near-elite players' quiet-eye fixation was on the puck or stick during shot preparation and execution in 70.53% of trials [peer-reviewed, abstract only] — [Martell & Vickers 2004, Human Movement Science, "Gaze characteristics of elite and near-elite athletes in ice hockey defensive tactics"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167945704000065)
- The first in-situ mobile eye-tracking study of scanning in junior elite players during play (Tobii Pro Glasses 3, 50 Hz) exists, but I saw only metadata [peer-reviewed, not read] — [Scanning in junior elite ice hockey players, J Sports Sci 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2026.2744018)
- Heads-up stickhandling is taught as a progression: start stationary with eyes on a point ahead and keep the puck in the bottom edge of vision [coaching] — [RinkHive, "Eyes-up stickhandling"](https://training.rinkhive.com/2026/06/24/eyes-up-stickhandling-drills-hockey-puck-control/); [HockeyShot off-ice stickhandling](https://hockeyshot.com/blogs/training-academy/stick-handling-off-ice-skills-series)

**Degraded or altered haptic feedback (thin evidence; mostly not sport-specific)**
- Gloves generally reduce fingertip tactile sensitivity and grip strength, with fit and material mattering, though some studies find no significant effect. In one latex-glove study, manipulating objects with a tool was unaffected even though gloves hampered direct handling [peer-reviewed, not hockey] — [Case study on sports gloves and hand performance, Applied Ergonomics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687018303739); [Effects of latex gloves on the kinetics of grasping](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223887322_The_effects_of_latex_gloves_on_the_kinetics_of_grasping)
- In haptic VR, impact sounds changed perceived stiffness: with identical physical stiffness, subjects ranked surfaces by their sound, and naive users were more swayed by sound than experienced ones [conf] — [DiFranco, Beauregard & Srinivasan 1997, ASME DSC-61 (abstract)](https://typeset.io/papers/effect-of-auditory-cues-on-the-haptic-perception-of-4jztux1fiy); the attack transient's spectral centroid is known to drive auditory stiffness perception — [Avanzini & Crosato 2006](https://avanzini.di.unimi.it/downloads/publications/avanzini_lncs06.pdf)

### Inferences
- Lawrie's null result (subjects felt +25% inertia, but their kinematics did not change) suggests that missing true stick mass in VR may hurt gross stickhandling less than feared, provided the visual stick is right. Stick length was the property people were most sensitive to (Bongers), so getting virtual stick length, lie and blade position exactly right likely matters more than mass.
- Johansson's principle (the next motor command is released once touch confirms contact) argues that the most important haptic job in VR is a crisp, low-latency contact-onset event when the blade meets the puck, and again when it leaves. A long, rich vibration texture matters less. Missing or late contact confirmation would push the player to watch the puck.
- Recreational players handle the puck closer to the body and let it follow the blade (plough-push). Skilled players strike and re-catch it (volley-and-catch), which means many brief, separate contact events. A VR trainer that renders each tap as a distinct, well-timed click-thud, rather than continuous rumble, supports the expert pattern.
- Heads-up handling depends on keeping the puck in the lower periphery. If a headset's field of view cuts off the area near the feet (to be checked against Quest 3 specifications by the hardware researcher), players will be forced head-down, which makes non-visual cues (sound, vibration) even more important for the puck's state.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled hockey study that removed or blocked vision during stickhandling (occlusion, strobe glasses) or measured gaze during dribbling. The heads-up claim rests on coaching.
- I found no study in which hockey players' hand sensation was degraded (numbing, thicker gloves, damped sticks) and stickhandling or receiving performance measured. The cross-sport glove evidence is mixed and not implement-specific.
- I found nothing on adult recreational learners' rate of skill gain or reliance on vision, beyond the varsity-vs-rec comparison above.

## 4. Puck reception and passing feel: soft vs hard passes, cushioning, and rolling, bouncing or on-edge pucks

### Takeaway
Real evidence here is coaching material only. The consensus: meet the puck with a square, slightly closed ("cupped") blade, let the stick give a few inches on contact with loose hands (especially a loose bottom hand), and keep the top hand away from the body. Too little give and the puck bounces off; too much and it dribbles away. Rolling or on-edge pucks are hard to receive, and frozen, low-bounce pucks are preferred because they bounce less.

### Cited Findings
- Cushioning means the stick meets the puck and then "embraces it upon impact." Young players either let the puck deflect away by not cushioning or let it dribble off the end of the stick by cushioning too much [coaching] — [HockeyShot, "How to effectively receive a pass"](https://hockeyshot.com/blogs/training-academy/hockey-skills-how-to-effectively-receive-a-pass)
- Absorb the impact "by having a slight give (only a few inches)" in the stick. A rigid stick makes the puck "bounce right off your blade." The blade should be square to the puck's path, since a tilted blade causes deflections and bobbles [coaching; quotes came via search summaries, so the exact page for each quote is not confirmed] — [How To Hockey, "Never miss a pass"](https://howtohockey.com/never-miss-pass/); [How To Hockey, cushioning the puck lesson](https://thepond.howtohockey.com/courses/free-lessons/pond-lessons/cushioning-the-puck/)
- Advice on blade angle conflicts between coaching sources: one says keep it flat and not angled, a provincial U11 resource says turn it slightly toward the ice to form a pocket, and the IIHF says keep the blade at 90° to the puck's direction [coaching] — [BC Hockey U11 passing resource](https://cdn-ca.aglty.io/bc-hockey/image-gallery/coach-page/U11%20Passing%20Resource%20v002.pdf); [IIHF, backhand passing and receiving](https://iihf.com/en/coaching/18606/backhand-passing-receiving)
- A soft first touch comes more from preparation (body position, lower and looser hands for more give) than from "soft hands" drills [coaching, field hockey] — [The Hockey Site, soft first touch](https://thehockeysite.com/how-do-i-coach-a-soft-first-touch-in-field-hockey/)
- Poor reception is "almost always driven by a bottom hand that grips the stick too tight" [coaching] — [Revak, Hockey IQ](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/puckhandling-technique-top-and-bottom-hands)
- A puck rolling on its edge "makes things very difficult" for the receiver [coaching, fragmentary source] — [Villanova digital library item](https://digital.library.villanova.edu/Item/vudl:1122022). Pucks are frozen to "reduce bouncing and increase controllability" (frozen COR 0.12-0.27 vs 0.45-0.55 warm) [conf] — [Anderson & Smith (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)
- Drills that train absorption use livelier objects (tennis balls) or the "eggs" metaphor, because a bouncier object forces real absorption [coaching] — [Sportplan, teaching soft hands](https://www.sportplan.net/drills/answers/Hockey/General/teaching-and-improving-soft-hands-skill.jsp)
- Hockey players rely on the force-and-damping quality of contact: in Hove et al.'s study, experts preferred lighter-feeling (butt-weighted) sticks for "pass catching" [secondary] — [Lawrie 2014 summary of Hove et al. 2006](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)

### Inferences
- In physics terms, a "soft" reception is the receiver lowering the blade's effective impedance at contact: moving the blade backward with the puck and lowering the effective restitution through compliance from loose hands and shaft flex. A "hard" pass is one whose momentum exceeds what the receiver's give can absorb before the puck leaves the blade or deflects off a tilted face. In VR the puck-blade contact model should let the player's real retreat speed (controller velocity at contact) decide the outcome, with low puck COR (about 0.3-0.5 for game-temperature pucks) and a closed-blade pocket that resists rebound.
- The receiver's cues are the impact thud (sound), the brief force at the hands and the visual stop or bounce. In VR, a reception vibration whose strength scales with relative speed and shortens when the player gives (a soft catch = low, damped pulse; a hard stab = sharp, louder pulse with a click) would give the "soft vs hard" feedback directly.
- Rolling and on-edge pucks: the cue is mostly visual (the puck wobbling and rising) plus irregular, bumpy contact. A VR trainer could make difficulty adjustable by sometimes sending pucks with wobble or bounce.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed measurement of reception mechanics (blade retreat speed, contact time, hand-force change during a catch) in ice hockey.
- I found no research on what players feel when a puck is on edge, rolling or bouncing, beyond coaching statements.
- I could not retrieve USA Hockey's or Hockey Canada's own coaching text on soft hands and receiving.

## 5. Shooting: stick loading and release, flex, "kick point", what shooters feel, and flex vs shot speed

### Takeaway
Shot speed comes mostly from the player, not the stick: skill, strength and the hands' force couple dominate, and studies disagree on whether stiffness matters (possibly for wrist shots, not slap shots). Skilled shooters load the shaft through ice contact before the puck, keep the puck on the blade longer and bend the stick more. "Kick point" (where the shaft bends most, near or below the lower hand) is real mechanics, but the claimed release-speed effects of low vs mid kick are vendor claims.

### Cited Findings
- Slap-shot sequence: the blade hits the ice slightly before the puck, loading the shaft. The loaded shaft "acts as a spring and recoils," and the kick point "is near or below the lower hand" [conf] — [Anderson & Smith (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/); [Smith & Bigford (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/laboratory-measurements-of-ice-hockey-stick-performance.pdf/)
- Elite vs recreational slap shots: longer blade-puck contact for elite players (38 vs 27 ms), similar puck acceleration, and higher speed (120 vs 80 km/h) [peer-reviewed] — [Villaseñor et al. 2006](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jab/22/3/article-p202.xml)
- Peak shaft angular and linear deflection correlated strongly with puck velocity (r = 0.80). Puck velocity depended on skill and body strength, not stick type [peer-reviewed; r from a secondary source] — [Wu et al. 2003, Sports Engineering 6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02844158); [Lawrie 2014 review](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)
- Skilled vs unskilled slap shooters: peak vertical blade force 123 vs 73 N, stick bend 15° vs 11°, shot speed 24 vs 18 m/s. Skilled players showed a proximal-to-distal sequence of upper-limb peak angular velocities [peer-reviewed, secondary] — [Woo et al. 2004, as cited in Zane 2012](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d)
- At the end of ground contact, skilled slap shooters had 15 cm more forward displacement and higher velocity (27.5 ± 4.3 vs 15.1 ± 6.2 m/s); there were no skill differences during downswing and initial stick-ice contact [peer-reviewed, secondary] — [Lomond, Turcotte & Pearsall 2007, Sports Engineering, as cited in Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a); [paper record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227043638_Three-dimensional_analysis_of_blade_contact_in_an_ice_hockey_slap_shot_in_relation_to_player_skill)
- Stiffness vs speed, conflicting: (a) Pearsall et al. 1999 is described by one secondary source as finding the highest slap-shot puck speeds with the least stiff stick, but by another as showing "high variation between subjects and no significant trend" [peer-reviewed, secondary, conflicting summaries] — [The Sport Journal review](https://thesportjournal.org/article/biomechanics-of-ice-hockey-slap-shots-which-stick-is-best/) vs [Smith & Bigford (WSU)](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/laboratory-measurements-of-ice-hockey-stick-performance.pdf/). (b) Shaft stiffness influenced puck speed in wrist shots but not slap shots [peer-reviewed, abstract snippet only; authorship not confirmed, possibly related to Worobets, Fairbairn & Stefanyshyn 2006, Sports Engineering 9] — ["Effect of ice hockey stick stiffness on performance" (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316058601_Effect_of_ice_hockey_stick_stiffness_on_performance). (c) Stiffness (77/87/102 flex) did not affect hand forces or shot velocity [thesis] — [Zane 2012](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d)
- In field testing with instrumented sticks, puck speed differed 32% between players but only 14% between sticks [conf, Procedia Engineering] — [Kays & Smith 2014, "Field measurements of ice hockey stick performance and player motion"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273823372_Field_Measurements_of_Ice_Hockey_Stick_Performance_and_Player_Motion)
- The shaft's bending profile has been measured with strain gauges during slap shots, comparing player calibre and shaft stiffness, and a portable on-ice strain system exists [peer-reviewed, title and abstract only] — [Hannon et al., "Dynamic strain profile of the ice hockey stick"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257002349_Dynamic_strain_profile_of_the_ice_hockey_stick_Comparisons_of_player_calibre_and_stick_shaft_stiffness); [Portable strain measurement system for ice hockey sticks](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263470165_Portable_strain_measurement_system_for_ice_hockey_sticks)
- Measured hand-force timing in slap shots: high-calibre players apply large lower-hand force first and then upper-hand force (loading couple), then switch to an opposed clamp during recoil. Low-calibre players show "no evident lower-upper hands' time coordination." Fastest vs slowest trials: male slap 118 vs 65 km/h, male wrist 105 vs 45 km/h, female slap 100 vs 36 km/h, female wrist up to 79 km/h [thesis] — [Zane 2012](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d)
- Wrist-shot accuracy: kinematic variables at the trailing leg, trunk, lead arm and lead-forearm pronation explained 61.3-97.7% of accuracy variance. An elite shooter aiming low far-side showed 30° less pronation and 12° more lead-wrist flexion/extension range [thesis, secondary] — [Magee 2009, as cited in Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a); related: [Michaud-Paquette et al., "Predictors of scoring accuracy: ice hockey wrist shot mechanics", Sports Engineering 11(2)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/226767552_Predictors_of_scoring_accuracy_Ice_hockey_wrist_shot_mechanics)
- Kick-point claims: low kick puts the flex low on the shaft for faster loading and release (quick wrist and snap shots); mid kick suits full wrist shots and slap shots and is "popular among defensemen"; hybrid sits between. Bauer's Pulse claims two "energy zones" blending mid-kick loading with low-kick release [vendor/retail claims, not independently verified] — [HockeyMonkey, how to select kickpoint](https://www.hockeymonkey.com/learn/how-to-select-kickpoint); [Bauer Pulse product page](https://www.bauer.com/products/bauer-pulse-stick-intermediate)
- Experts named shaft "flex" as their second most important stick-choice factor after lightness [secondary] — [Hove et al. 2006, via Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)

### Inferences
- For "feel" of a shot, the cue sequence is: blade-ice contact (scrape or thump), shaft load building over tens of milliseconds as the hands' couple and body weight bend it, the puck's arrival (a short high-force event in the first ~10 ms of puck contact), and release when the shaft unloads within about 30-40 ms. In VR, a rising vibration "load" envelope while the virtual shaft bends, then a sharp release cue and a "crack" sound at puck departure, maps onto this sequence. Visual shaft bend is a strong cue because it is what the stick-choice literature calls "flex."
- Because stick stiffness barely changes shot speed in measurements, while the hands' couple and body sequence strongly do, a trainer for a recreational defenseman gains more from feedback on technique (couple timing, lower hand low, blade loading the ice before the puck) than from fine-tuning virtual flex values. Keep virtual flex plausible (senior flex of about 77-102 bends noticeably under about 100+ N of hand force) and show the bend.
- Recreational players' shorter puck contact (27 vs 38 ms) and missing hand coupling are the measurable skill gaps a defenseman's slap and wrist shot trainer could target.

### Gaps
- No study found that records what shooters report feeling (load, release, "kick") alongside measured strain. "Feel" during shots is undocumented beyond preference surveys.
- No independent test found of low vs mid kick-point release time. Kick-point benefits remain vendor claims.
- Hannon et al.'s strain numbers (timing of peak bend relative to puck contact) were not retrievable (403).

## 6. Cross-sport analogues where the science is deeper (racket, golf, bat, lacrosse, field hockey)

### Takeaway
Other implement sports show that "feel" is multisensory and strongly shaped by impact sound: in golf, sound and torsional vibration dominate rated feel; in tennis, players use sound to judge impact quality and location, and lingering post-impact vibration ("nervousness") reads as poor feel; in VR haptics, sound alone shifts perceived stiffness. Players aim, consciously or not, for the vibration node (minimal ring). This is the strongest scientific support for substituting with sound plus vibration.

### Cited Findings
- Golf: interviews showed the sound and vibration at impact contribute significantly to a club's "feel." Some feel traits thought to be tactile "are influenced more by the impact sound" [peer-reviewed] — [Roberts, Jones, Rothberg et al., "Evaluation of impact sound on the 'feel' of a golf shot", J Sound Vib 2005](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222000887_Evaluation_of_impact_sound_on_the_'feel'_of_a_golf_shot); [Roberts et al. 2006, "Influence of sound and vibration from sports impacts on players' perceptions of equipment quality", Proc IMechE L](https://doi.org/10.1243/14644207JMDA86)
- Golf: torsional vibration and impact sound had the greatest influence on the feel of a shot. In putting, sharpness and loudness of impact sound were key influences on feel [peer-reviewed, abstracts and summary] — [Roberts, sound and vibration on the feel of a golf shot](https://jonroberts.info/?page_id=464); [Barrass, Roberts & Jones 2006, impact sound in golf putting](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7170927_Assessment_of_the_impact_sound_in_golf_putting)
- Golf shaft flex perception: 20 low-handicap (0-10) golfers rated the "impact sensation" of drivers that differed only in shaft weight and flex, in pairwise comparisons. The authors argue that fitting should weigh impact sensation, not only clubhead speed [conf] — [Böhm, Krämer & Senner 2009, TUM](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/node?id=1319191). Within-golfer shaft strain patterns were far more consistent than between-golfer patterns (12 golfers, three shafts differing only in stiffness) [peer-reviewed] — [Sports Engineering 2019, s12283-019-0308-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12283-019-0308-3)
- Tennis: "nervousness" is defined as the lingering vibration after impact. Players identified impact sound as a contributor to perceived shot quality and impact location. Of the candidate sweet spots (node, centre of percussion, maximum rebound), research suggests players learn to aim for the vibration node, where post-impact vibration is minimal [peer-reviewed, 2025, abstract and snippets] — [Definition of feel-related impact parameters of tennis rackets, Sports Engineering 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12283-025-00502-x)
- In real forehands, players hit neither optimal area. Off-centre impacts raise racket vibration and wrist shock, and the authors link reduced vibration perception to lower racket-vibration amplitude [peer-reviewed] — [Tennis racket vibrations and shock transmission to the wrist during forehand drive, PLoS One 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4503399/)
- Tennis, unverified detail: a stationary-impact study of 20 players reported central impacts as more comfortable than impacts 100 mm toward the tip (seen only in a search summary, so treat as unverified) — [Ball impact location on a tennis racket and its influence on ball speed, arm shock and vibration](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230824781_Ball_impact_location_on_a_tennis_racket_and_its_influence_on_ball_speed_arm_shock_and_vibration)
- String dampers vs handle vibration and discomfort were studied directly. I could not retrieve the abstract, so its findings are not reported here — [PubMed 10413265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10413265/)
- Baseball bats: higher damping is perceived as less "sting" [secondary, via hockey goalie-stick work] — [Russell et al. (ASA)](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/162nd/Russell_3aED.html)
- Haptic VR: matching impact sounds to taps changed which virtual surfaces felt stiffer, and naive users were more affected than experienced ones [conf] — [DiFranco, Beauregard & Srinivasan 1997](https://typeset.io/papers/effect-of-auditory-cues-on-the-haptic-perception-of-4jztux1fiy)
- Field hockey and lacrosse: I found only coaching material on soft first touch and catching, with no measurement studies [coaching] — [The Hockey Site](https://thehockeysite.com/how-do-i-coach-a-soft-first-touch-in-field-hockey/); [Lacrosse Drive, catching the ball](https://lacrossedrive.com/?p=3716)

### Inferences
- Jon's observation that contact sounds "helped a lot" matches the golf, tennis and haptic-VR literature: much of what athletes call "feel" is auditory, and sound can override or reshape touch, especially for less experienced users. Investing in sound design (puck-blade thud with low spectral centroid for "heavy rubber"; a brief bright tick or ring for "light composite"; blade-ice scrape; a crack on shot release) is likely the highest-yield substitution.
- The "sweet spot" literature suggests a usable VR cue: hits near the blade's sweet zone should produce a short, clean pulse with little ring, and toe or heel hits a longer, buzzier ring and a different sound. This gives players impact-location feedback the way tennis players get it.
- Stiffness and flex are only weakly perceived even by golfers through feel alone. Flex is better conveyed visually (shaft bend) and through timing (release delay) than through vibration texture.

### Gaps
- No cross-sport study found that tests whether vibration-only feedback on a handheld controller (no implement) can carry sweet-spot or impact-quality information. That belongs to the VR and haptics side of the research.
- I could not open the full Roberts et al. papers or the 2025 tennis paper, so effect sizes are not reported.
- I found no lacrosse or field hockey measurement studies of stick vibration or feel.
