# Sound and Waveforms Sell the Heavy Puck

A controller-only trainer can make the hands-to-stick-to-puck chain feel like hockey by firing three cues at once from every 1 kHz contact event: a short, frequency-shaped vibration that confirms the touch, a low, fast-decaying "thock" that carries most of what athletes call feel, and a visible shaft bow plus an honest puck response that carry load and weight, while the hands-to-stick mapping stays strictly 1:1 so the blade skills he builds can carry onto real ice. The evidence ranks those cues in that order of payoff: in golf, impact sound predicted perceived feel better than vibration did; touch is the signal that releases the next motor command; and vibration cannot render weight, so anything force-like belongs to sound and sight. The prototype currently uses the weakest version of both feel channels, a haptic call that ignores frequency and lands on render-frame boundaries and a winsound player that plays one sound at a time, even though Jon's own Meta runtime (1.208.0) exposes PCM, amplitude-envelope and parametric haptics that his installed pyopenxr already wraps. The power-play session mixes two kinds of failure: execution errors that feel work can fix (28 of 138 soft passes not controlled, 33 releases toward no option, 8 of 17 shots wide) and read errors it cannot (in 4 of the 6 seam passes the drill's own lane model gave the pass at most a 5% chance at release, and zero goals from 17 shots matches the drill's own estimate of about one). The first changes are plumbing: one contact-event bus feeding a persistent mixing audio stream and a streamed PCM haptic channel, with the physics loop moved off Python's global interpreter lock (GIL) so it holds 1000 samples per second; then come a tuned contact vocabulary, Eleven-style blade-face calibration and feedback on each reception. Anything that moves the stick away from the hands (weight offsets, gain changes, follow lag, possession magnets) should stay out, because it costs precision and can leave after-effects on the ice.

## The power-play log splits into blade errors and read errors

Before deciding what feel should fix, it pays to sort the 138 reps by what went wrong ([session log](../prototype/data/drill-powerplay-20261107-155102/events.jsonl)). **28 incoming passes (20%) were never controlled, and every one of them arrived softly.** The winger's passes in this drill run 6.5 to 8 m/s, and the missed ones (median 7.65 m/s) were barely faster than the caught ones (median 7.3 m/s), so pace was not the problem. Another **33 releases left the blade more than 25 degrees away from every option the drill offers**, at a median of only 7.4 m/s; twelve of these went back to the half-wall winger, which may have been deliberate, but the rest look like pucks that squirted off the blade. Of the **17 shots, 8 went wide**, 5 were blocked and 4 were saved. These three categories are blade execution: reception, puck control and face angle.

The other headline numbers are mostly not about feel. **The drill's goalie model priced the 17 shots at about 1.0 expected goals in total**, so zero goals is ordinary bad luck at this sample size: a count with a mean of one comes up zero about 36% of the time. His shots left the blade at a median of 22 m/s (79 km/h), right on the measured recreational slap-shot speed of 80.3 ± 11.6 km/h ([Villaseñor et al. 2006](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jab/22/3/article-p202.xml)), so power is not the gap; direction is. The six seam passes were not slow either (median 17 m/s), but **in four of them the lane model scored the pass at 5% or less to complete at the instant of release**, and three were held 4.5 to 4.9 s after he gained control, against a session median of 2.1 s, which gave the penalty-kill box time to close. Overall the drill scored only 21 of 95 decisions as good reads. Those are reads and timing, and the debrief should separate them from execution so that feel work is judged on the right numbers.

Direction errors are unforgiving at the point. The net is about 19 m from the drill's left-point blade spot, so **each degree of blade-face or aim error moves the puck about 33 cm at the goal line**, and with a net half-width of 0.915 m a shot aimed at the centre misses the net entirely at about 2.75 degrees. The project's own geometry puts 1 degree of top-hand wrist error at 2.4 cm at the blade ([README](../README.md)). The mapping was also still moving during the session: he refit the stick mid-session, changing roll from −21 to −11 degrees and pitch from −33 to −50 degrees, and widened the catch forgiveness from 1.0 to 1.5 cm ([session meta](../prototype/data/drill-powerplay-20261107-155102/meta.json)). Add a physics loop running at about 920 of 1000 samples per second, and the feel agenda is clear: confirm every contact crisply for receptions, make the blade face precise and readable for shots and passes, and deliver the cues through a clean, fast timing pipeline.

## Real players feel a 10 ms thud, a bending shaft and the puck's answer

The measured physics of a puck on a stick is short and violent. In slap shots the puck stays on the blade for **27 ± 5 ms for recreational players and 38 ± 9 ms for elite players** ([Villaseñor et al. 2006](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jab/22/3/article-p202.xml)); the forces peak in the first ~10 ms as the puck is dragged up to stick speed, after which the bent shaft unloads into it ([Smith & Bigford, WSU](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/laboratory-measurements-of-ice-hockey-stick-performance.pdf/)). The puck is a lossy, nonlinear spring, 156 to 170 g of vulcanized rubber whose restitution is 0.45 to 0.55 warm but only **0.12 to 0.27 frozen**, and whose dynamic stiffness rose by more than 600% when frozen and fired at game speed ([Anderson & Smith, WSU](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)). The stick rings: its first bending modes sit at 47 to 60 Hz and its second at 116 to 161 Hz (same source), while higher bending and torsional modes around 213 to 261 Hz sit near the hand's most sensitive band of 200 to 400 Hz, and wood damps that ringing two to four times more than composite does ([Russell et al., ASA](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/162nd/Russell_3aED.html)). Yet the WSU lab found **no strong correlation between a stick's measured vibration and players' sense of feel** ([Anderson & Smith](https://ssl.wsu.edu/documents/2015/10/experimental-characterization-of-ice-hockey-sticks-and-pucks.pdf/)), and nobody has measured vibration at the hands during real stickhandling or receiving, or the friction and sound of a blade on ice. Players also wear padded gloves, which very likely filter out the higher frequencies; no glove transmission data exists, but it suggests that the "feel" a real player gets is already a low-bandwidth signal.

What the hands do matters more than what they sense. Coaching consensus casts the top hand as the control hand that rotates the blade to cup the puck and the bottom hand as a loose guide that slides along the shaft, and blames poor receptions "almost always" on a bottom hand gripping too tight ([Revak, Hockey IQ](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/puckhandling-technique-top-and-bottom-hands)). Grip-force sensors confirm the shooting half of this: skilled shooters load the lower hand first and then the upper hand, as an opposed force couple that bends the shaft against the ice, then switch to a clamp as the shaft springs back, while low-calibre players show no such coupling ([Zane 2012, McGill](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/aab2df80-5fd0-437c-a6d8-cd44c577354d)). In stickhandling the top hand moves least and the blade most. Varsity players amplified velocity from the butt to the blade by 2.08 m/s on the forehand against 1.26 m/s for recreational players, and struck the puck with the blade moving 0.61 m/s faster than the puck ("volley and catch"), where recreational players pushed it along at nearly the same speed ([Lawrie 2014, McGill](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)). Two consequences follow for VR: any noise in the top controller's rotation is magnified at the blade, and skilled handling is a train of distinct, brief contacts, each of which deserves its own cue.

Three further findings decide how to substitute for real touch. The first is that **vision dominates stick inertia**: when Lawrie added up to 25% to a stick's moment of inertia, every subject could feel it, but their stickhandling movements barely changed, so missing stick mass in VR costs less than feared, while stick length, which people perceive acutely, must be right ([Bongers et al., via Lawrie](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)). The second is that **touch is a trigger**: in grip-force research the next motor command is released once touch confirms contact (Johansson & Westling, summarized in [Lawrie 2014](https://mcgill.scholaris.ca/items/eeeb2675-8b05-4122-a533-2f476979bd4a)), which makes a crisp contact-onset cue the single most important haptic job. Without one, the player has a reason to keep his eyes on the puck, even though hockey players track an incoming puck partly in the near periphery, 5 to 15 degrees off their line of sight ([Polikanova et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11574569/)). The third is that **sound shapes feel more than vibration does**. For golfers, impact sound correlated with perceived feel more strongly than vibration did ([Roberts et al. 2006](https://dspace.lboro.ac.uk/dspace-jspui/bitstream/2134/10860/2/The%20influence%20of%20sound%20and%20vibration%20from%20sports%20impacts.pdf)); tennis players read lingering post-impact vibration as poor feel and learn to hit the vibration node, where it is smallest ([Sports Engineering 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12283-025-00502-x)); and when physical stiffness was identical, people ranked virtual surfaces by the sound of the tap, naive users most of all ([Liu & Ando 2016, reviewing DiFranco et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5130246/)). Jon's report that contact sounds "helped a lot" is exactly what this literature predicts.

| Real cue | What the real signal is | Best VR carrier | How well it survives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contact onset | Force peak in the first ~10 ms of a 25-40 ms shot contact | Vibration transient plus thud, from the same timestamp | Well; this is the cue to get right first |
| Rubber-on-composite character | Lossy puck (restitution 0.12-0.27 frozen); composite rings longer than wood at ~50-260 Hz | Sound pitch and decay; vibration spectrum via PCM | Well on Touch Plus; sound carries most of it |
| Hit location (sweet spot) | Lingering ring off the vibration node reads as poor feel | Length of the ring tail, in sound and vibration | Plausible; untested on controllers |
| Blade on ice | Unmeasured scrape and friction | Speed-gated scrape sound, faint vibration texture, shaft bow | Partly |
| Shaft load and release | Opposed hand couple; skilled sticks bend ~15° against ~11° | Visible bow, rising load cue, release crack | Visually; the force itself cannot be rendered |
| Puck weight on the blade | Momentum, cradling, blade kick-back | Puck exit speed with no rebound; a louder, lower thud | Weakly, through sound and motion |
| Stick weight and inertia | Felt, but did not change movements | Nothing; keep the stick following the hands with no lag | Not needed |
| Bottom hand sliding, grip pressure | Loose bottom hand sliding on the shaft | Glove drawn sliding on the shaft | Visual only |

His request for "a heavy rubber puck against a light flexible composite stick" breaks down cleanly. "Rubber" means a dead, fast-decaying contact, because decay rate is the cue listeners lean on most to judge material ([Klatzky, Pai & Krotkov 2000](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/lci/papers/docs2000/pai-KlaPai00.pdf)) and a slow decay reads as metal or glass. "Heavy" comes from loudness, low pitch and the puck's motion: louder and lower-pitched impacts make objects feel heavier ([MDPI 2024 review](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/14/14/6020)), a collision sound implying a lighter object made a held object feel lighter ([Yamada et al. 2018](https://www.rm2c.ise.ritsumei.ac.jp/papers/2018/vrst2018_yamada.pdf)), and when two objects collide, people judge whichever one rebounds or leaves faster to be the lighter one ([Gilden & Proffitt 1989](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/gilden/files/2016/04/UnderstandingCollisionDynamics.pdf)). A heavy puck must therefore never pop off the blade faster than the blade is moving, or rebound off a blade that is cradling it. "Light, flexible composite" is visual and kinematic: the stick follows the hands with zero lag, bows visibly under load and whips back quickly, and its ring is a faint, short tail after the thud rather than a buzz. The prototype's blade restitution of 0.15 to 0.20 ([puck.py](../prototype/puck.py)) already sits inside the frozen-puck range and should stay there.

## Touch Plus can play waveforms, but the prototype only asks it to buzz

Each Touch Plus has **one wideband voice-coil motor in the grip, driving the palm**. Meta rates it at frequencies "up to 500 Hz" and says it matches the Touch Pro "with slightly lower output intensity" ([Meta: haptics technology](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/haptics-technology/); [Meta: Touch Plus, PC native](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/native/pc/native-touch-plus-controllers)). Unlike the single-frequency actuator in the Quest 2 controllers, this one makes frequency a real design variable. The only measurement found puts its output at **2.62 G peak-to-peak at 120 Hz and full amplitude**, falling roughly in proportion to 1.07 G at amplitude 0.4 ([Sabnis et al., CHI '26](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/sabnis-et-al-bimanual-haptics-pre-print-chi-2026.pdf)). No frequency-response curve or lower limit is published, so how strongly it plays a real stick's 50 to 60 Hz bending band is unknown. Its strength is modest next to a real puck strike, so contrast and timing will matter more than raw amplitude.

On Jon's PC the Meta OpenXR runtime (reporting "Oculus" 1.208.0) lists **`XR_FB_haptic_pcm`, `XR_FB_haptic_amplitude_envelope` and `XR_EXT_haptic_parametric`**, and the installed pyopenxr 1.1.6501 already defines their structs and `get_device_sample_rate_fb` ([local check](../research_notes/VR%20hockey%20stick%20and%20puck%20feel/quest_haptics.md)). The call the prototype uses today exploits none of it. Meta documents the `frequency` field of `XrHapticVibration` as "Ignored" ([Meta: OpenXR haptic feedback](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/native/android/mobile-openxr-haptic/)), so the current `buzz()` controls only amplitude and duration at a carrier the runtime chooses ([record.py](../prototype/record.py)). The call is also sent from the render loop, which merges every haptic event in a frame into one pulse with the maximum amplitude and maximum duration ([sandbox.py](../prototype/sandbox.py)). A contact the physics timed to the millisecond is shifted by up to a frame (11 ms at 90 Hz), and two quick stickhandling taps in one frame become one.

PCM (raw waveform playback) is the right path from Python. It drives the voice coil directly with float samples between −1 and 1: the app asks the controller for its sample rate with `xrGetDeviceSampleRateFB` (0 means PCM is unsupported), submits buffers of up to 4000 samples with `append = true` so each queues behind what is already playing, and reads `samplesConsumed` to detect back-pressure ([Khronos: XrHapticPcmVibrationFB](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenXR/specs/1.1/man/html/XrHapticPcmVibrationFB.html)). One rule bites: **a non-PCM effect, or a PCM buffer sent with `append = false`, cancels whatever is playing**, so once streaming starts, every buzz in the app, menu confirmations included, must go through the same PCM mixer. Because the timing lives in the sample offsets rather than in when the call is made, the waveform keeps the 1 kHz contact timing even if chunks are submitted from the render loop, provided each chunk goes out with enough lead that the buffer never runs dry. The parametric extension is the fallback: it is multi-vendor, was added in OpenXR 1.1.60 in May 2026, is not yet ratified and supports transients natively ([Khronos: XR_EXT_haptic_parametric](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenXR/specs/1.1/man/html/XR_EXT_haptic_parametric.html)), but although his runtime lists it, nobody has tested it on Touch Plus over Link. Meta's Haptics SDK is a C library built around authored `.haptic` clips ([Meta: Haptics Native SDK](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/native/experimental/exp-haptics-native/)) and is not needed here, though Haptics Studio remains useful for auditioning effects.

Over Link the haptic path is fast enough. Sabnis et al. measured **21.5 ± 1.4 ms from a physical tap on a Quest 3 controller to the vibration it triggered**, over USB Link at 120 Hz; the figure covers tracking, app logic, Link transport and the actuator, and sits below the 25 to 60 ms tactile timing threshold the authors cite ([Sabnis et al.](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/sabnis-et-al-bimanual-haptics-pre-print-chi-2026.pdf)). Reports of weak haptics on PC cluster on SteamVR, which supports none of these extensions ([Khronos OpenXR Inventory](https://github.khronos.org/OpenXR-Inventory/runtime_extension_support.html); [BigBox VR support](https://support.bigboxvr.com/hc/en-us/articles/32380950760724-How-to-fix-weak-haptics-on-Steam-with-Quest-2-and-Quest-3-headsets-connected-via-Link)), so staying on the Meta runtime, as the prototype already does, avoids both problems.

The design research converges on short, event-shaped transients. With position feedback alone, virtual tapping feels like "soft foam"; adding short, high-frequency transients matched to real contact accelerations made the same surfaces feel like wood in blind tests ([Kuchenbecker et al. 2005](https://repository.upenn.edu/meam_papers/228)). The workhorse model is a decaying sine wave, A(v)·e^(−Bt)·sin(ωt), with its amplitude set by impact velocity ([Okamura et al. 2001](https://biorobotics.harvard.edu/pastprojects/asme.html)). Hardness follows vibration energy: in a 2026 study, taps carrying more vibration energy were judged harder regardless of waveform (odds ratio about 2.76), with example parameters of 85 Hz and a 5 ms decay for foam against 400 Hz and 20 ms for metal ([Zamani et al., eLife](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/111461)), although reviewers rated that evidence incomplete. Sliding texture follows speed rather than force, since removing speed responsiveness hurt realism while removing force responsiveness did not ([Culbertson et al.](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/40005)). And two hands feel one object when both vibrate: on stock Quest 3 controllers, sending each vibration to both hands raised the sense of holding one object from 0-12.5% to **87.5%**, with 33% of the vibration on the second hand felt as "loose, slipping", 66% as "stable" and 100% as "stiff", and a 50 ms delay between hands already reading as elastic ([Sabnis et al.](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/sabnis-et-al-bimanual-haptics-pre-print-chi-2026.pdf)).

The limits are just as clear. Adding vibration did not improve perceived weight change in a CHI 2024 study where visual pseudo-haptics did ([Stellmacher et al.](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/entities/publication/d530560f-94e0-4952-a602-40613242a2dd/details)), so vibration cannot carry the puck's heft. Prolonged vibration raises detection thresholds, and the adaptation stays within one frequency channel of the skin ([adaptation and masking](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4613-3039-4_10)), which argues against a constant buzz. Meta's own guidance asks for "whitespace", subtle feedback for frequent events and strong feedback only for rare ones ([Meta: haptics best practices](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/haptics-best-practices/)).

| Event | Waveform (starting values) | Amplitude | Hands | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Puck meets blade (reception, pass, shot) | Decaying sine body at 80-150 Hz with a 5-10 ms decay, plus a 250-400 Hz layer with a 2-5 ms decay whose share grows with impact speed; whole event 30-60 ms | From the normal relative speed, compressed (square root); floor ~0.25; 1.0 for the hardest hits | Bottom hand 100%, top 60-80%, zero delay | Okamura model; Zamani foam vs metal; Sabnis crosstalk |
| Composite ring after a hit | 116-161 Hz (second bending band, close to the 120 Hz where output was measured); decay ~15 ms on centre hits, 25-30 ms off the toe or heel | 10-20% of the thud on centre hits, 30-40% off-centre | Both | Stick modes; tennis "nervousness"; untested |
| Stickhandling tap | One or two cycles of a 120 Hz sine (8-17 ms) | 0.2-0.4 | Both | Sabnis grain; Meta "frequent = subtle" |
| Blade on ice | Short grains spaced by blade travel, gated on contact and a small speed threshold, fading out quickly on lift | 10-25% of the puck-hit peak, rising with press depth | Both | Culbertson speed; Rietzler pressure cues |
| Shot load and release | Low ramp while the blade is pinned and the shaft bows; sharp transient when the puck leaves | Ramp at most ~30%; release near full | Both, bottom stronger | Shot sequence from Smith & Bigford; inference |
| Distant events (posts, boards, goals) | None on the hands | none | none | Nothing physical reaches the stick; sound only |

These are starting values derived from the studies above, not tested settings, and should be tuned in blind A/B trials with Jon as the subject. One rule gives him reception feedback for free: because both amplitude and the share of the crisp layer scale with relative normal speed at contact, a catch where he gives with the puck produces a soft, dull pulse and a stiff stab produces a sharp, loud one, matching the coaching advice to let the stick "give a few inches" ([How To Hockey](https://howtohockey.com/never-miss-pass/)).

## Sound carries most of the feel, and winsound throttles it

Impact sound is the strongest single lever on perceived feel, so it deserves the first engineering effort. Higher-pitched sounds with shorter decays are judged stiffer, and these low-level acoustic features outweigh the material the listener assumes ([Liu & Ando 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5130246/)). For the puck that means a low-mid body with a fast decay, with loudness and brightness scaled by impact speed, so that a soft reception sounds quieter and duller and a one-timer louder and sharper. The current "clack" is already a damped rubber "thock", but its body sits at 380 to 1150 Hz with nothing lower ([sounds.py](../prototype/sounds.py)), and the heavy-puck evidence argues for adding lower content and avoiding bright, clicky tails that imply a light object. No published spectral analysis of a puck on a blade exists, so the best template is a real recording: a 292-effect arena library includes puck-on-boards, slap shots and a puck sliding on ice with a recorder taped to it ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/sound-library/ice-hockey/)), or Jon can record his own stick at a rink with a phone. Game practice adds variety cheaply through small random pitch shifts on each play ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-power-of-pitch-shifting)), with a common starting point of 5 to 10 variants, ±10% pitch and ±3 dB ([Generalist Programmer, low authority](https://generalistprogrammer.com/game-audio-development)). Continuous contacts, the blade on the ice and the puck cradled on the blade, need a looping scrape layer whose gain follows sliding speed and how far below the ice the hands are pressing, which also supplies the "floor" cue that the missing ice cannot.

winsound cannot deliver any of this. It cannot play from memory asynchronously (that combination raises a RuntimeError) and it has no mixer ([Python docs: winsound](https://docs.python.org/3/library/winsound.html)); in the prototype a new sound cuts off the last one, so each frame keeps only the most important event and drops the rest ([sounds.py](../prototype/sounds.py)). During stickhandling, when blade-on-ice and puck-on-blade contacts overlap, half the cues vanish. The standard fix is one persistent output stream with a mixing callback: python-sounddevice, running over PortAudio on Windows' WASAPI, lets the app request a latency or block size and read back the stream's actual latency ([python-sounddevice docs](https://app.readthedocs.org/projects/python-sounddevice/downloads/pdf/latest/)). Shared-mode WASAPI should be expected to bottom out around a 10 ms period, since several Windows 11 devices reported 480 frames as default, minimum and maximum and miniaudio returned 480-frame buffers even when 128 were requested ([miniaudio #1084](https://github.com/mackron/miniaudio/discussions/1084)). The estimated shared-mode minimum is about 20 ms ([Nirbheek 2018](https://blog.nirbheek.in/2018/03/low-latency-audio-on-windows-with.html?showComment=1525112690757)), and real measurements ran from 30 ms upward in OpenAL Soft and 45 ms in one miniaudio test ([OpenAL Soft #682](https://github.com/kcat/openal-soft/issues/682); [miniaudio #937](https://github.com/mackron/miniaudio/issues/937)). Exclusive mode can go lower, but it is device-specific and may not work on the Quest's virtual audio endpoint at all. Audio should go to the Link headset endpoint directly and never through SteamVR's audio redirection, where users report delays of 100 to 200 ms ([Steam forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/250820/discussions/0/3193621324043154078)).

Timing tolerances are lopsided and tight for impacts. People notice a sound that leads a tap by about 25 ms but tolerate one that lags by up to about 42 ms ([arXiv 2307.05451](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.05451)). For impact-type audio and vibration pairs, the acceptable window ran from about −10 ms (vibration first) to +40 ms (vibration after), narrowing as the vibration gets stronger ([JCAA](https://jcaa.caa-aca.ca/index.php/jcaa/article/download/1661/1408/1798)), and motor performance itself degrades above about 75 ms of latency ([Waltemate et al. 2016](https://cg.cs.tu-dortmund.de/publications/2016-latency.pdf)). Virtual button presses tolerate far more, 179 ms with audio first and 451 ms with haptics first ([IEEE Transactions on Haptics 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/toh.2023.3268842)), which is why generic UI rules of thumb do not apply to a puck strike. The practical rules are to fire sound and vibration from the same physics event with the same speed parameter, never to play a sound early, and never to hold back the haptic to wait for slower audio. Asgard's Wrath 2 shows how far this can go, since its team routed haptic PCM through the audio engine so one parameter drove both ([Meta dev blog](https://developers.meta.com/vr/blog/asgards-wrath2-and-haptics-studio/)); Jon's `synth.py` and `sounds.py` already synthesize audio, so one generator can emit both waveforms.

The 920-of-1000 physics rate is a symptom that will get worse. The render loop, the physics thread, a sounddevice callback and a haptic submitter all need the same interpreter lock, and an audio callback starved of it underruns audibly. The fix is to make the 1 kHz inner loop release the lock, either by vectorizing it with numpy or by compiling the substep with numba or Cython, and to stamp every event with `time.perf_counter()`; on Python 3.11 and later on Windows, `time.sleep` uses a high-resolution timer ([Python docs: time](https://docs.python.org/3/library/time.html)), and lowering `sys.setswitchinterval` from its 5 ms default lets the render and audio threads get the lock sooner ([Python docs: sys](https://docs.python.org/3/library/sys.html#sys.setswitchinterval)). Controller poses should be located at the frame's `predictedDisplayTime`, as late as possible before drawing ([OpenXR 1.1 spec](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenXR/specs/1.1/html/xrspec.html); [Meta: Unity OpenXR settings](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/unity-openxr-settings/)), with the physics interpolating between the newest poses, and testing should run with Asynchronous Spacewarp (ASW) disabled in the Oculus Debug Tool so that real missed frames show up ([Meta: Oculus Debug Tool](https://developers.meta.com/vr/documentation/native/pc/dg-debug-tool/?locale=en_US)). Link encode bitrate matters too: one hobbyist measured about 32 ms headset motion-to-photon at 500 Mbps against about 80 ms at 150 Mbps on a Quest 2 ([Greendayle](https://github.com/Greendayle/VR-Motion-to-photon-latency-)). The Rust port planned for Spike 1 removes the lock problem entirely ([README](../README.md)).

## Honest hands, cosmetic lies: which tricks survive the trip to real ice

Pseudo-haptic weight, which fakes heft by bending the visuals, comes in two families, and neither suits a precision stick. **Large, visible tracking offsets** sell weight as a metaphor: in Rietzler's studies, participants rated offsets of up to about 20 to 24 cm a "good" weight cue and accepted up to 35 to 42 cm, offsets raised presence and enjoyment, and 12 of 18 bowlers preferred them ([Rietzler et al. 2018](https://www.uni-ulm.de/fileadmin/website_uni_ulm/iui.inst.100/institut/mitarbeiterbereiche/rietzler/Breaking_the_Tracking.pdf)). But they move the displayed object away from the hand by design, and they make things feel heavy, the opposite of a light composite stick. **Subtle changes to the ratio between hand motion and displayed motion** (0.7 to 1.3) preserve hand ownership but shift perceived weight by only about ±5 g on a 185 g object, with vision weighted at just 0.18 against proprioception ([Samad et al. 2019](https://research.facebook.com/file/208988017925238/Pseudo-Haptic-Weight-Changing-the-Perceived-Weight-of-Virtual-Objects-By-Manipulating-Control-Display-Ratio.pdf)). The detection limits are narrow: a hand offset of about 4.5 degrees in either direction, or a gain between 0.88 and 1.07, goes unnoticed ([Zenner & Krüger 2019](https://umtl.cs.uni-saarland.de/paper_preprints/zenner-krueger-hand-redirection-thresholds-vr-19-pre-print.pdf)), yet even unnoticed drift costs performance, with task scores falling significantly on trials where the virtual hand drifted from the real one ([Burns 2007](https://techreports.cs.unc.edu/papers/07-002.pdf)). For a trainer, pseudo-haptic weight is seasoning at most, applied only at the blade end, only during puck load and below about 4.5 degrees.

Contact with an ice surface that is not there is where visual tricks earn their keep. Users detect a hand passing through a surface far more readily than a mismatch between where they see and feel their hand ([Burns 2007](https://techreports.cs.unc.edu/papers/07-002.pdf)), so seeing the blade sink into the ice is the most noticeable flaw. In Rietzler's wall-pushing studies, stopping the hand at the surface and letting an offset build raised immersion and the feeling of resistance; offset plus vibration was the strongest condition (effect size r = .84 for the sense of restriction), participants "did refuse the use of clipping", and the experts recommended an escape strategy for when offsets grow too large ([Rietzler et al. 2018b](https://www.uni-ulm.de/fileadmin/website_uni_ulm/iui.inst.100/institut/mitarbeiterbereiche/rietzler/ConveyingThePerception.pdf)). Stopping the visual has a cost, though: a purely constrained hand made grasping worse because it hides how hard the user is pressing, and the best performers added explicit cues for penetration depth ([Prachyabrued & Borst 2016](https://vrlab.cmix.louisiana.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Prachyabrued_Borst_GraspCues_TVCG.pdf)), with audio contact cues preferred over visual ones at a small cost in performance ([Canales & Jörg 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10314326)). Jon's design, in which the blade stops, the shaft bows and then the stick slides through the hands, is the evidence-backed shape. The slide belongs at the bottom hand, which really does slide on a shaft, rather than at the knob. The missing cue is pressure: a scrape whose level follows how far below the ice the hands are, plus a readable bow. When the blade lifts, the stick should rejoin the true grip without a visible pop, closing the gap at no more than about 40% faster than hand speed, which stays under the velocity thresholds Burns measured. The README's planned snap assist is the one assist that moves the blade itself ([README](../README.md)), so it should stay vertical only, a few centimetres at most, logged and fadeable.

Flex is a legitimate visual channel. Visually rendered deformation biases perceived stiffness ([Srinivasan et al., via PMC4129386](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4129386)), and while pressing on an object, display ratios from 0.7 to 3.5 went unnoticed ([Weiss et al. 2023](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/pubdb/publications/pub/weiss2023usingpseudostiffness/weiss2023usingpseudostiffness.pdf)). The prototype's flex spring, at a flex of 75 with a 0.15 kg blade, has its first mode near 21 Hz, and its damping ratio of 0.6 gives about a 9% overshoot ([puck.py](../prototype/puck.py)), which is the "quick whip, not wobbly" behaviour the evidence favours. The bow could be drawn larger than the physics bend as long as the blade the puck collides with stays at the physically plausible position. The current choice to draw the ice bow without storing energy, made after a stick held low fired a resting puck at 6 m/s, is the honest one.

For mapping two controllers to one stick, game engines offer three modes: first hand only, first hand pointed toward the second, or an average of both ([Unity XR Interaction Toolkit](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.xr.interaction.toolkit@2.5/api/UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Transformers.XRGeneralGrabTransformer.TwoHandedRotationMode.html)). Hockey fits the second: the top hand anchors the knob and sets the blade's roll, the shaft points toward the bottom hand, and the bottom hand is projected onto the shaft and free to slide. Averaging is wrong because it moves the knob whenever the bottom hand moves, which a real stick never does. The prototype already follows this shape, and two refinements matter. Tracking noise between the two controllers is multiplied by roughly the ratio of blade distance to hand spacing, so the two-point direction should be blended with the top controller's own orientation rather than low-pass filtered heavily, which would add lag. The one-handed mode needs the in-hand fit captured while both hands hold, plus a short blend when a hand lets go.

The transfer risks are real but avoidable. Adapted distortions leave after-effects: amplifying or reducing hand motion in VR shifted later pointing in the direction the gain predicted ([ACAPS 2025](https://acaps2025.sciencesconf.org/649092/document)), and adapting to a 45-degree visuomotor rotation in VR left implicit after-effects ([York CVR 2019](https://deniseh.lab.yorku.ca/files/2020/05/CVR2019_sm_0607.pdf?x64373)). The near-total absence of realistic haptics may also push VR users toward a different visual control mode, undermining transfer ([Harris et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6794235)). Against that, VR training can transfer when it targets perception and decisions: in baseball, adaptive VR training improved on-field batting more than extra real practice did, across 80 players over 6 weeks ([Gray 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02183/text)). Constant visual guidance breeds dependence where auditory pacing did not ([Ronsse et al. 2011](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/motor-learning-with-augmented-feedback-modality-dependent-behavio/)), so visual aids should fade while sound cues can stay.

| Technique | Verdict for a trainer | Evidence and condition |
|---|---|---|
| Shaped impact sounds | Safe; highest yield | Change perceived stiffness and weight without moving the stick |
| Frequency-shaped contact vibration | Safe | Confirms contact; keep it short and sparse |
| Floor constraint with slide-through escape | Safe; better than clipping | Penetration is the most detectable flaw; users refused clipping |
| Visible shaft bow, even exaggerated | Safe if cosmetic | The blade the puck hits stays at the true flex position |
| Pressure cues (scrape level by press depth; ghost blade; tint) | Safe as fading aids | Constrained visuals hide how hard you press |
| Blade-end lag under puck load | Only below ~4.5°, only during contact, with physics using the true blade | Zenner thresholds; Burns drift cost |
| Hand or knob gain (0.88-1.07 goes unnoticed) | Avoid in training | After-effects; drift costs precision |
| Weight offsets of 8-42 cm | Avoid | Reads as heavy; moves the blade |
| Physics-driven follow stick | Avoid | Lag and "floaty" swings |
| Possession magnet | Avoid; at most a little damping inside the blade's cup, within the same physics step | Latency complaints; masks skill |
| Restitution above measured values | Avoid | The puck reads light; contact stops feeling like skill |
| Asymmetric-vibration "pull" | Experiment only | Shown only on custom research devices with voice-coil actuators ([Tanabe et al.](https://aminer.org/pub/5ff680d3d4150a363cb52f4c/pulling-illusion-based-on-the-phase-difference-of-the-frequency-components-of)) |

## Shipped games stumbled on puck control, face angle and fast swings

The hockey market leader gave up on controller puck feel. Sense Arena supports controllers-only play in a 6 by 6 ft space, but its stickhandling module, DanglePro, uses mixed reality with a real stick and a real off-ice puck ([Sense Arena FAQ](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/faqs)). Its users say puck control with controllers is "way too hard" and that the puck "skittles under my stick" ([r/hockeyplayers, mirror](https://pol1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/hockeyplayers/comments/1hnq5nz/is_sense_arena_25_worth_the_money)), and a goalie forum summed it up as helping "get your hands to a puck but won't help you hold onto it" ([The Goal(ie) Net](https://www.thegoalnet.com/forums/topic/9007-sense-vr-training/)). Pick-up League Hockey draws complaints that its puck is "too sensitive and glitchy", that the possession mechanic adds "noticeable latency" and that a light tap sends the puck flying ([Meta store](https://www.meta.com/experiences/pick-up-league-hockey/4092691917425965/)), and the developers of Hockey+ VR said their magnet-based assist took "multiple iterations" to feel invisible while still rewarding skill ([Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/hockey-4ctumw)). Hockey VR is the only title that states a position on hands, "use both controllers for more realistic puck handling" ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1769010/Hockey_VR)). None of these titles documents a tuned, controllers-only stick model, which leaves that ground open.

Paddle sports show what works. Eleven Table Tennis is the reference: its developers rebuilt its physics engine from scratch and gave players exact per-axis paddle calibration, locking every axis but one while tuning it, and its wiki suggests a physical check of tossing a ball at a stationary paddle and adjusting until the ball comes straight back ([Eleven-VR wiki](https://eleven-vr.fandom.com/wiki/Customising_your_paddle)). A reviewer argued that a short vibration suffices there because a real ping-pong hit gives little feedback ([slar.se](https://slar.se/eleven-table-tennis-a-vr-masterpiece.html)), an argument that does not carry over to a one-timer, and players observed that at maximum paddle speed "it feels like just holding the paddle and catching the ball" ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/488310/discussions/0/350543951938105791)), a warning against implements that add speed. Racket Club's creative lead said they chose a level of abstraction on purpose, "no longer about pure simulation", and that nailing the feel of impact was the milestone that made the game viable ([Meta Quest blog](https://www.meta.com/blog/racket-club-vr-launch-tennis-pickleball/)).

Golf and batting point straight at Jon's wide shots. A Golf+ reviewer using the bare controller was "spraying shots everywhere, unable to control face angle consistently" ([Golfers Authority](https://golfersauthority.com/golfplus-vr-headset/)). Another golf developer conceded that the Quest controller "is very light" compared with a club and capped ball curve for accessibility ([UploadVR](https://www.uploadvr.com/ultimate-swing-golf-interview/)). In The Golf Club VR, a ground-contact vibration told players where the ground was but not the face angle ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/489600/discussions/0/333656722962820543/)), and VR cricket players found that even a small delay between hands and bat ruined timing on long, fast swings ([PlanetCricket](https://planetcricket.org/forums/threads/vr-cricket-game-backyard-cricket.111605)). The hockey version of all this is an Eleven-style per-axis grip-to-blade calibration, a return test for the blade and an implement that never lags.

The shared failure that software cannot fully fix is tracking at speed. A Meta forum thread open since 2024, with posts continuing into 2026, reports Quest 3 controllers losing tracking in medium-to-fast motion in Beat Saber, Eleven and tennis titles: hands jump ahead or lag and then catch up within a second, and one user said switching to Touch Pro controllers fixed it ([Meta forum 2024](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/discussions/OtherTroubleshooting/unresolved-as-of-may-30-2024---quest-3-controller-tracking-issues/1090544); [Meta forum 2026](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/discussions/OtherTroubleshooting/quest-3-controller-tracking-issues-in-fast-paced-games/1363852)). Jon's own Spike 0 run measured 100% tracking and about 1 mm of jitter ([README](../README.md)), but shots are where losses cluster. With a swept contact model, a pose that jumps on recovery can sweep the blade a long way in one tick and fire a huge impulse into the puck, so the sweep should be capped and contact softened on frames that OpenXR flags as not tracked.

On haptics, the lessons from shipped games are consistent. Asgard's Wrath 2 put haptics under its audio team, generated more than 700 effects from finished sound effects, found that full-frequency haptics "glued things together" like a subwoofer, and warned against overuse ([Meta dev blog](https://developers.meta.com/vr/blog/asgards-wrath2-and-haptics-studio/)). Home Sports argues that each action needs distinct feedback and names a hockey slapshot as one example ([Resolution Games](https://www.resolutiongames.com/blog/how-cutting-edge-technologies-are-taking-home-sports-to-the-next-level-1)).

## A stationary, tethered defenseman is the trainer's best case

Standing in place is an advantage here, not a compromise. Standing rather than sitting raised presence without raising sickness ([Clifton & Palmisano 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10055-019-00407-8)), and fixed-spot drills create no artificial motion at all. When he must move, discrete repositioning between reps carries teleport-class comfort, since teleport and arm-cycling caused less sickness than joystick steering ([Coomer et al. 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326760529_Evaluating_the_effects_of_four_VR_locomotion_methods_joystick_arm-cycling_point-tugging_and_teleporting)). Arm-swing methods are out because they "can interfere with simultaneous interaction" ([arXiv 2601.02167](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02167v2)), and here both arms are the stick. If a short scripted glide is ever needed, for backward gap control for example, Meta's guidance is a vignette at strength 0.4 to 0.6, applied only while moving and faded quickly on stopping ([Meta IWSDK: slide locomotion](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/iwsdk/concepts/locomotion/slide/)), with brief, infrequent acceleration ([Meta: locomotion best practices](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/locomotion-best-practices/)). The drills he chose, a rim retrieval behind his own net and the power-play point, are the situations where a defenseman skates least.

The tether and the cluttered room call for designing turns out of the drills. The app can store the yaw that points away from where the cable hangs at calibration and spawn every drill facing it. It can also track unwrapped head yaw, so that a full turn counts as 360 degrees rather than resetting to zero, and past about ±270 degrees cue a turn back between reps or rotate the next drill to bring him back to centre. Turning moments can be staged so the puck comes to him, for instance by spawning the rim retrieval already facing the boards rather than making him spin from the net. Physically, a ceiling pulley keeps the Link cable off the floor, and reversing a few turns untwists it ([CordTheory](https://cordtheory.com/quest-link-cable-management/)). The stationary boundary should be sized for his real hands rather than the virtual blade, with every puck he must reach kept inside comfortable arm reach so he never lunges into furniture; Sense Arena's own minimum for controllers-only play is 6 by 6 ft ([Sense Arena FAQ](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/faqs)).

How well off-ice practice transfers depends on how closely the movements match. Off-ice agility work transferred to on-ice agility, but less well than on-ice work did ([JSSM 2019](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-18-680.xml-Fulltext)), and a 2025 comparison found transfer "hinges on the consistency of an athlete's movement patterns" between environments ([Frontiers in Bioengineering 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2025.1692676/full)). That argues for training in his real stance, with knees bent and the hand spacing and roles of a left shot, and for entering his real stick's measured length, lie and curve rather than values derived from his height, since length is the stick property people perceive most sharply. The transfer a controllers-only trainer can realistically claim is perceptual and tactical: reads, timing, blade angles and reception timing, not the weight of a real stick in the hands.

## Build order: plumbing first, then the contact vocabulary

Steps 1 to 4 come first because tuning feel on a pipeline that drops events and shifts them by up to a frame is wasted effort. Fixing the physics budget (step 2) comes before adding audio and haptic threads (steps 3 and 4), because more threads in a GIL-starved process cause audible underruns. If time allows only one change, step 3 alone will move perceived feel the most.

| # | Change | Starting parameters | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One contact-event bus | Each 1 kHz contact emits {`perf_counter` time, kind (puck-blade, blade-ice, post, boards), normal speed, sliding speed, hit point from heel to toe on a 0-1 scale, press depth below the ice}. Audio and haptics both consume it; nothing is aggregated per frame | Meta's sync rules; Asgard's Wrath 2 drives sound and touch from one parameter; skilled handling is many distinct contacts (Lawrie) |
| 2 | Physics off the GIL | Substep loop in numba `@njit(nogil=True)` or vectorized numpy; Python 3.11 or later; `perf_counter` everywhere; `sys.setswitchinterval(0.001)`; physics interpolates poses the render thread locates at `predictedDisplayTime`. Done when it holds 995 or more samples/s with audio and haptic threads running and shows zero missed frames with ASW off | 920/s today; GIL contention; OpenXR prediction guidance |
| 3 | Mixing audio stream replaces winsound | sounddevice `OutputStream`, WASAPI shared mode, 48 kHz float32, blocksize 256 then try 128, Link headset endpoint chosen by name, all sounds preloaded as numpy arrays, cap of 16 voices, each event placed at its sample offset, `stream.latency` logged. Target 20-40 ms from contact to ear, and never early | winsound plays one sound at a time; thresholds of −25/+42 ms |
| 4 | PCM haptics | Enable `XR_FB_haptic_pcm` at instance creation; call `get_device_sample_rate_fb` each session; a per-hand mixer renders events into a ring buffer and submits 5-10 ms chunks with 10-20 ms of lead, `append=True`, watching `samplesConsumed`; route every buzz through it. Fall back to parametric, then to today's call if the sample rate is 0 | Frequency is ignored by the simple call; non-PCM calls cancel the stream; 21.5 ms Link path |
| 5 | Contact vocabulary | The haptic table above; sound with a low-mid body, fast decay, and gain and brightness rising with normal speed; a scrape loop following sliding speed and press depth; 5-10 variants at ±10% pitch and ±3 dB; master and scrape gain dials. Check each change in blind A/B trials | Sound outweighs vibration for feel; Zamani; Sabnis; Meta |
| 6 | Blade-face calibration and cues | Enter his real stick's length, lie and curve; Eleven-style per-axis calibration (roll, then pitch, then yaw, each with the other axes locked), saved for each grip mode; a return test where a slow puck sent square at a stationary blade comes back within about 1° (about 33 cm at 19 m); blend in the top controller's own orientation instead of heavy low-pass filtering; a fading face-normal line on the ice as a training aid; log face angle at release against the puck's heading | 8 of 17 wide; mid-session refits; Golf+ face angle; Eleven toss test |
| 7 | Honest reception with feedback | Keep blade restitution at 0.15-0.20 and add no magnet; let the blade's retreat speed at contact decide the outcome; log blade height, face angle and retreat speed at every missed reception to find which one dominates | 20% missed; coaching on cushioning; collision heuristics for mass (Gilden & Proffitt); complaints about Pick-up League's possession |
| 8 | Tracking guards | Read OpenXR's per-frame tracked/valid flags for each controller; on untracked frames make contact soft or skip it; cap blade sweep at 3 cm per 1 ms tick (30 m/s, above the 15-25 m/s blade speed of a shot); keep stickhandling in front of and below the headset | Quest 3 tracking loss at speed; hazard of swept contact against jumping poses |
| 9 | A debrief that separates reads from hands | For each rep, report whether the lane was open at release, whether the puck went where the blade face pointed, and how long he held it; measure head-down time during possession before and after steps 3 to 5 | 4 of 6 seam lanes closed at release; the Johansson contact hypothesis |
| 10 | Room and tether | Drills face away from the cable; unwrapped yaw with a nudge at ±270°; ceiling pulley; stationary boundary sized for the hands | Meta comfort guidance; cable-management guides |
| 11 | Later | Record real puck-on-blade and blade-on-ice audio, plus a phone accelerometer taped to his real stick, as templates; measure the Touch Plus response from 50 to 400 Hz with a phone accelerometer; audition effects in Haptics Studio; Touch Pro controllers as an option; shot energy stored only from the hands' opposing couple | Evidence gaps; Zane's force couple |

Every step has a cheap acceptance test. A phone filming at high frame rate can capture a controller tap, the haptic buzz and the speaker click together, which verifies steps 3 and 4 against the −10 to +40 ms window. Session logs can then compare wide shots, unintended releases and missed receptions before and after steps 5 to 7, and a feel change should only count as a win if those three numbers move.

## What to leave out

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| Weight offsets or gain on the hands or knob | Rietzler's offsets read as heavy, the opposite of a light stick; drift costs performance (Burns); adapted gains leave after-effects in later pointing |
| A physics-driven follow stick, or smoothing that lags | Blade & Sorcery players call lagging weapons "floaty" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/629730/discussions/0/1743355067135550800)); cricket timing breaks; performance degrades above 75 ms |
| Averaging both hands into the stick pose | It moves the knob whenever the bottom hand moves |
| Possession magnets or "has the puck" states | Users notice possession latency; magnets mask the skill being trained |
| Restitution or speed gain above measured values | The puck reads light; contact stops feeling like skill (Eleven at maximum paddle speed) |
| Pulses re-sent every frame, or a constant scrape buzz | Masking and numbness; Meta asks for whitespace |
| Sounds triggered before contact by prediction | A sound that leads is noticed at about 25 ms |
| Bright, clicky puck sounds | They imply a light object |
| Using vibration to convey weight | It did not improve perceived weight change (Stellmacher) |
| The SteamVR runtime or its audio path | No PCM or parametric haptics; 100-200 ms audio delays reported |
| Dropping the stick when a collision overloads it | Schell Games found this caused accidental drops ([Schell Games](https://legacymicrosites.schellgames.com/blog/engineering-a-super-spy-in-i-expect-you-to-die)) |
| Letting the blade clip through the ice | Users refused clipping (Rietzler 2018b) |
| Arm-swing skating, or thumbstick sliding as the default | Conflicts with holding the stick; continuous steering is the most sickening option |
| Visual guides that are always on | Guidance dependence; fade them instead |

## Conclusion

The prototype's feel ceiling is set by plumbing, not physics. A swept, substepped, 1 kHz contact model is already more than any shipped hockey title documents, yet its events reach Jon's ears through a one-voice player and his palms through a frequency-blind call tied to the render frame; fixing that delivery should change perceived feel more than any physics parameter, and a phone camera can verify it. The power-play log also redraws what the trainer can honestly teach. It cannot transfer the weight of a real stick, and no amount of vibration will change that, but it can transfer blade-face precision, reception timing and reads, and those three, not shot power, stand between him and goals. One cheap bet links feel to decisions: if crisp contact confirmation that does not depend on his eyes lets him trust possession without looking down, his head-down time during possession should fall and his seam reads should improve, and feel work would then pay off in the decision layer too.
