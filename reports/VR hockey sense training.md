# Train the reads Sense Arena leaves out

Jon's trainer should build hockey sense as a defenseman-first library of game situations. In each one, the information that decides the right play sits behind him or inside an attacker's movement, he answers with his physics stick and body instead of a button, and every rep is scored openly against a computed best option and replayed from a view he could not have had live. That recipe follows the strongest evidence available. Perceptual-cognitive training transfers to the field at about half its lab size (**Hedges' g = 0.65 versus 1.51** across 22 team-sport studies). Occlusion training with replay feedback reaches **d = 0.85 on field tests**. The strongest VR-to-real study, Gray's baseball batting trial, worked because practice was adaptive and variable: plain VR batting practice did not transfer. Generic drills such as 3D multiple-object tracking improve only themselves, and the developer's own group failed to replicate its flagship soccer result with three times the sample. No peer-reviewed study shows Sense Arena or any other hockey VR product improving on-ice play, and the market leader leaves clear openings: **14 defense drills against 45 for forwards and 54 for goalies**, a Hockey IQ Score with no published formula, manual easy/medium/hard difficulty, and user complaints that controller-driven pucks "skittle" under the stick. The build order that best fits the evidence and Jon's game starts with a shoulder-check retrieval and first-pass module, then an occlusion "read the carrier" module, then a gap-and-angle 1-on-1 tuned to his one-handed left-side angle, then 2-on-1 lane defense, with net-front coverage and 3-on-2 reads after that. Realistic expectations are modest on-ice gains after six or more weeks of 10-25 minute sessions two to four times a week, verified on game video rather than by in-app scores.

## Hockey sense is a learned library of reads, steered by when and where you look

Sports science has no agreed definition of hockey sense. Researchers treat it as a bundle of perceptual-cognitive skills: exploring the ice (scanning, which in hockey means shoulder checks), picking up advance cues from an opponent's body, stick and skating, recognising familiar patterns, and selecting and timing a response, with executive functions such as inhibition and working memory as a supporting layer. Even the newest hockey work validates "game sense" against human judges. A McGill group had five scouts rate 40 elite players aged about 15-16 roughly weekly for a year, then tested which objective measures (anticipation and eye-movement tasks, a VR hockey test, a cognitive battery) predicted those ratings, and the authors say long-term follow-up is still needed ([McGill Newsroom](https://www.mcgill.ca/newsroom/node/317174)). No gold-standard objective measure of hockey sense exists, so every number a trainer reports is a proxy, worth remembering whenever a product sells one composite "IQ" score.

Recognition is the engine. When 23 expert and 14 non-expert players' decisions were analysed across 118 decision points from game film, experts labelled situations "familiar" about **twice as often**, and familiar decisions were judged better only when the decision came from recognising the event ([Mulligan, McCracken & Hodges 2012](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/node/160)). A cognitive task analysis likewise found elite hockey players decide in a recognition-primed way instead of weighing options one by one ([Mulligan, SFU thesis](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/9369/etd4364.pdf)). In youth soccer, anticipation and the use of situational probabilities separated elite from sub-elite players from age 9, while basic visual-function tests did not separate them at any age ([Ward & Williams 2003](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/2149)). The design consequence: a trainer builds hockey sense by growing the player's library of recognisable situations, not by sharpening raw vision.

What experts do with their eyes matters more than how fast their eyes move. In a live defensive-zone task with Team Canada players, elite defenders fixated tactical locations faster on successful plays and finished with one long fixation isolating a single target, a **"quick then quiet"** cascade, yet their maximal gaze-shift speed did not differ from teammates known for tactical errors ([Martell & Vickers 2004](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4008469?lng=en); [Vickers & Martell, AAHPERD 2004](https://aahperd.confex.com/aahperd/2004/finalprogram/paper_5579.htm)). Across 482 one-on-one checking trials, 18 elite university players held a final "quiet eye" fixation of about **1.1 s (1117-1125 ms) before contact**; fixations on the near ice appeared mainly when a player was struggling to control the puck, and successful checks followed a longer quiet eye on the puck and stick or the attacker's body ([Vickers et al. 2017](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4081/3/Effect%20of%20the%20Look-Up%20Line%20on%20the%20Gaze%2C%20Head%20Orientation%20and.pdf)). Goalies show the same pattern: quiet eye started earlier and lasted longer on saves (952 ms) than on goals (826 ms) ([Panchuk & Vickers 2006](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/3062/)).

Scanning is the other hockey-specific finding with real data. Across 2,545 actions by 88 Swedish professionals, scanning before a pass reception was associated with a more successful next action (**β = 0.12, 95% CI 0.07-0.17**), and neither position nor gender moderated it, so it applies to a defenseman receiving an outlet as much as to a forward ([Berg et al. 2025](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4092070?lng=en)). Eye-tracked USA Hockey development players showed how brief hockey scans are: **58.1% contained a single fixation** between looks back at the puck, mean fixation length was 134.5 ms, and scans just before reception were shorter and simpler than scans made off the puck ([Bespomoshchnov et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2026.2744018)). A Western University thesis found players' heads dropped toward the ice as traffic increased ([Western thesis](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8528)). Soccer supplies the only usable rates, which are calibration rather than norms: Premier League players averaged 0.44 scans per second before receiving, scanning fell when an opponent was within a metre and plateaued at about 4 m, and the effect on passing was "small, but positive" (μβ = 0.13) ([Jordet et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7573254)).

Speed of reading separates levels too, though the hockey numbers are thin. In a temporal-occlusion decision task, 20 elite players were correct **77% of the time against 51%** for novices and responded in 812 ms against 1074 ms, but this is an unreviewed preprint and gives an order of magnitude, not a norm ([Ma et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9304532/v1)). Executive functions differ in speed, not accuracy: among 132 Chinese youth players, experts were faster on inhibition, working-memory and switching tasks but no more accurate ([Wang, Yang & Shi 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1670150)). The table maps these components onto what a Quest 3 trainer can measure; because the Quest 3 has no eye tracking, fixation-level measures must be approximated from head orientation.

| Component | Best hockey evidence | What the trainer can measure |
|---|---|---|
| Exploration (scanning) | Scanning before reception predicts success, β = 0.12 (Berg 2025) | Head-yaw excursions away from the puck before a touch, and whether they covered the decisive area |
| Cue pick-up and anticipation | Experts 77% vs 51% correct, about 250 ms faster (Ma 2026, preprint) | Accuracy and latency at occlusion points; honest versus faked cues |
| Pattern recognition | Experts call situations familiar twice as often (Mulligan 2012) | Accuracy on new variants of trained patterns |
| Decision selection and timing | Coaching only; no defenseman studies | Value of the chosen option against the best; latency from cue onset |
| Gaze at the moment of action | Quiet eye about 1.1 s before contact (Vickers 2017) | Head stability and orientation on the carrier in the final second |

Defenseman research is sparse. Beyond the Vickers lab's checking studies, the research found no peer-reviewed work on gap-control decisions, odd-man rush reads, pass-lane anticipation by defensemen, or adult recreational players of any position. Modules for rush reading and gap timing therefore rest on general anticipation research and coaching literature, while engagement and breakouts have direct hockey data. For Jon the picture is encouraging: an adult recreational defenseman most likely lacks the library of familiar situations and the automatic scanning habit of elite players, and those are the most trainable parts because they are knowledge built from exposure. Among elite samples, adults transferred perceptual-cognitive training as well as adolescents (**g = 0.63 versus 0.58**) ([Zhu et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/)), though no study has tested recreational hockey players. The habits worth measuring and unlearning are puck-watching and a head that drops as traffic builds.

## Transfer to the ice is real but roughly halves, and design decides whether it appears

### Representative, adaptive, feedback-rich practice carries the transfer

The best-quantified evidence comes from video and simulation training. A PROSPERO-registered meta-analysis of 22 team-sport studies found large gains on the trained task (response accuracy g = 1.51) that shrank to **g = 0.65 on court** (13 studies, n = 247), with response time following the same pattern (g = -0.91 trained versus -0.44 transferred) ([Zhu et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/)). A meta-analysis of temporal-occlusion anticipation training (12 studies, N = 248) found d = 1.21 overall and **d = 0.85 on field-based transfer tests**; the training showed a non-occluded replay after each prediction, and skilled athletes improved too ([Müller et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11467115)). The working rule for a designer is to expect on-ice effects of roughly half what the in-app test shows.

The moderators all point the same way, although none reached statistical significance. Transfer was larger with 3D or VR stimuli (**g = 0.96**) than with life-size video (0.46) or computer video (0.19), larger with sport-specific action responses (0.87) than verbal or keyboard responses (0.41), and larger for programs longer than four weeks (1.25 versus 0.38). The authors recommend first-person VR, sport-specific responses, more than four weeks, two to four sessions a week and 10-25 minutes per session ([Zhu et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505547/)). Expert-novice differences are also clearer when participants perform real sport actions in situ rather than pressing buttons at video ([Travassos et al. 2013](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/7398/)).

Two VR studies show that the training method, not the headset, is the active ingredient. In Gray's trial with 80 high-school baseball players, adaptive VR batting (staircased pitch speeds, growing location variability, interleaved pitch types) improved real hitting against a no-extra-training control (real hits d = 1.2, swings at pitches outside the zone d = 1.7); five years later the adaptive group's on-base percentage still differed from control (d = 1.8), while **plain VR batting practice did not transfer to real hits** ([Gray 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5733365)). The caveats are real: a single author, data collected in 2008-2010, low-fidelity VR and some internal inconsistencies. In hockey, 34 Swiss professionals practised picking the largest open area of the net in VR for one 15-minute session; the group shown the puck's-eye view of the net as feedback rose from **0.78 to 0.90** accuracy, while identical practice without feedback did not change (0.76 to 0.78) ([Bloechle et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11993527)). There was no retention test and no on-ice test, but the lesson is sharp: VR earns its keep by showing information real practice cannot. Novices trained in VR table tennis also improved coach-rated real play (d = 1.08) ([Michalski et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6736297/)).

The counter-evidence deserves weight. Among tennis novices, perception-action training was no better than perception-only training ([Williams, Smeeton & Allen 2004](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413200490518002)), so coupling has not always been shown to add value. A review of VR and 360° decision training found transfer results mixed, including immersive-test gains with no change in real-match decisions ([Jia et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)). A 2026 racket-sport VR meta-analysis found g = 0.78 but a prediction interval of -0.01 to 1.57, meaning a new program could show no benefit at all ([PLOS ONE 2026](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0345541)). The Zhu meta-analysis itself reported publication bias, only 2 of 14 RCTs with adequately described randomisation, and only 7 studies with retention tests.

### Generic cognitive drills improve only themselves

NeuroTracker is the cautionary tale. Its flagship positive result, better passing decisions in soccer after 3D multiple-object tracking, rested on seven trained players with control groups collapsed after the fact ([Vater, Gray & Holcombe 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500884/)). When the same research group ran a randomised trial with 62 academy players, 3D-MOT scores rose but there was **no transfer to an attention test, small-sided games or early-career success** ([Romeas et al. 2025](https://pure.etsmtl.ca/en/publications/no-transfer-of-3d-multiple-object-tracking-training-on-game-perfo/)). An independent review found only three studies that tested far transfer to real tasks, two of them null, and noted the developer co-authored almost every published NeuroTracker study ([Vater et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500884/)); a review of commercial cognitive trainers found only 1 of 43 studies tested a sporting task ([Harris, Wilson & Vine 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5958310/)). In hockey, NeuroTracker baselines did not relate to draft rank, points per game or on-ice goal differential in 35 elite juniors ([VU Amsterdam](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/about-the-predictive-value-of-a-3d-multiple-object-tracking-devic/)), and among 48 varsity athletes they related to nothing except fewer penalty minutes ([Acquin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1608463)).

The hockey VR trials repeat the pattern. Twenty-five elite youth players trained on Sense Arena's multiple-object tracking, "Find the Lane" and divided-attention drills twice a week for nine weeks. The abstract reports a group-by-time effect on a stick-button Flanker test close to the trained format, but the full text shows non-significant group effects (p = .247 and .181), p-values that disagree with the abstract, and **no on-ice measure** ([Heilmann & Schubert 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12611849/)). In a crossover with 74 elite youth players, a 20-minute VR warm-up produced "a small decline" on a dual-task tapping test where a dual-task warm-up helped ([Brinkbäumer et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2026.1766605)), which undercuts the pre-game "brain warm-up" use that vendors promote.

### No hockey VR product has shown on-ice gains

For ice hockey specifically, the research found no peer-reviewed controlled study showing that VR training, Sense Arena or otherwise, improves on-ice or game performance. Sense Arena's FAQ cites "studies and testimonials" without naming one ([Sense Arena FAQ](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/faqs)), and a 2019 11-week trial with 34 UW-Stevens Point players never published outcomes ([UWSP news](https://www.uwsp.edu/ucm/uNews/Pages/WPR-VirtualReality19.aspx)). Its site cites a study in which 15 minutes of VR feedback improved professionals' identification of scoring chances ([Sense Arena](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/off-ice-drills/cognitive-drills/how-progressive-training-works-article)); the likely source is the Fribourg study, which used a custom Pimax-and-Unity setup and whose university announcement names no commercial product ([Bloechle et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11993527); [University of Fribourg](https://www.unifr.ch/news/en/32915/)). The honest status of hockey VR is plausible but unproven.

VR also has physical limits that should shape what it trains. Weaker binocular depth cues and missing haptics may push users away from the online visual control that real interceptive actions use, so VR-learned skills could be "unrepresentative of the real-world" ([Harris et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6794235)), and moving from VR to real golf putting temporarily disrupted both gaze control and accuracy ([Harris et al. 2020](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/160944)). High motion-to-photon latency degrades performance and provokes cybersickness ([Stauffert et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/frvir.2020.582204)). The consequence for Jon's project: the hockey-sense content should lean on what to look at and what to decide (reads, scanning, lane choice, anticipation), and the physics stick should serve as a coarse, representative response (where the blade goes, where the pass goes) rather than as the thing graded for fine timing.

## Sense Arena leaves the defenseman, the metrics and the stick open

NHL Sense Arena began in 2018 as a goalie trainer and is the only NHL-licensed VR hockey trainer. It sells for **$29 a month billed annually ($348) or $59 month to month** on Quest 2, 3, 3S and Pro ([Sense Arena players page](https://hockey.sensearena.com/players)), claims 450,000+ users in 40 countries ([NHLPA](https://www.nhlpa.com/news/sense-arena-and-nhlpa-partner-to-bring-nhl-stars-to-virtual-reality/)), and says more than half its users are U16 and U18 ([NHL.com](https://nhl.com/news/nhl-sense-arena-virtual-reality-tool-on-display-at-fanatics-fest-nyc)). Its template is short drills inside leveled programs. The Goalie Advancement Program runs three focus areas (angles, traffic, reading a release) across eight levels of three training days, each day six to eight drills of about 8-10 minutes, at a user-selected easy, medium or hard setting; a Player Advancement Series covers scanning, anticipation, retrievals, reading opponents and decisions with limited time and space ([Sense Arena progressive training](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/off-ice-drills/cognitive-drills/how-progressive-training-works-article)). Its five publicly named hockey-IQ drills are Shoulder Check, Entry to O Zone, D Zone Faceoff, Multitask Colors (pass to the teammate whose overhead color is called) and 3v3 Small Ice ([Sense Arena top 5 drills](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/hockey-iq-hub/nhl-sense-arenas-top-5-drills-for-building-elite-hockey-iq)). Around them sit an NHL 3-on-3 season mode, XP and leaderboards, and DanglePro, a mixed-reality stickhandling module that uses a real stick and off-ice puck and runs only on Quest 3/3S ([NHLPA](https://www.nhlpa.com/news/sense-arena-and-nhlpa-partner-to-bring-nhl-stars-to-virtual-reality/)).

The drill library is lopsided: **Forwards 45, Defense 14, Goalies 54, Cognitive 18** ([Sense Arena progressive training](https://hockey.sensearena.com/resources/off-ice-drills/cognitive-drills/how-progressive-training-works-article)). The headline Hockey IQ Score has four components (decisions, scanning without the puck, reading with the puck, reaction speed) with no published scale or formula, and the outcome claims after 20 sessions (+9% faster decisions and +11% reaction time for skaters, **+10.6% save percentage** for goalies) come with no sample, method or baseline ([players page](https://hockey.sensearena.com/players); [goalies page](https://hockey.sensearena.com/goalies)). Daily Faceoff could not verify an earlier "98% of users reported higher hockey IQ" claim ([Daily Faceoff 2024](https://www.dailyfaceoff.com/news/mindblowing-virtual-reality-proving-to-be-an-effective-hockey-training-aid-for-pros)).

Users and coaches split predictably. USA Hockey's Roger Grillo, whose organisation is a Sense Arena partner, praised VR as "a safe, positive, environment to fail in" but described adoption as individual rather than team-wide ([Daily Faceoff 2024](https://www.dailyfaceoff.com/news/mindblowing-virtual-reality-proving-to-be-an-effective-hockey-training-aid-for-pros)). An independent goalie reviewer found the video shooters "surprisingly realistic" but concluded the benefits do not justify the cost for most families, pointing to no skating, no physical feedback and few visible users despite NHL and PWHL sponsorships ([University of Rochester Libraries](https://www.library.rochester.edu/about/news/sense-arenas-vr-hockey-training-works-why-isnt-it-widespread)). A recreational player on Reddit found controller puck control "way too hard... sometimes it skittles under my stick," and a goalie replied it is "only worth it for goalies" ([r/hockeyplayers mirror](https://pol1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/hockeyplayers/comments/1hnq5nz/is_sense_arena_25_worth_the_money)). The store rating sits at 3.9 from about 1.3K ratings ([QuestStoreDB](https://queststoredb.com/app/nhl-sense-arena-26-vr-hockey-3454538864674405/)).

The rest of the market does not compete on hockey sense in VR. Hobby physics games such as Hockey VR ($4.99 on Steam) and VR Hockey Reborn (free, 4.0 from 434 ratings) offer stick and puck physics, shooting and multiplayer but no decision curriculum ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1769010/Hockey_VR); [QuestStoreDB](https://queststoredb.com/game/vr-hockey-reborn/5307901462582204)). Screen tools fill the cognitive niche: Hockey IntelliGym, whose one small controlled study (22 U16/U18 players over 20 weeks) measured fewer head impacts rather than better decisions ([University of Delaware study](https://core.ac.uk/download/372985826.pdf)), and video "pause and decide" games that give about five seconds to choose ([Project Hockey IQ](https://members.thecoachessite.com/article/cale-makar-hockey-iq-game-2.0-%7C-project-hockey-iq)). The best structural template comes from baseball. WIN Reality trains pitch recognition and swing decisions, explicitly not mechanics, and publishes a concrete benchmark: calling the pitch type correctly **70%+ of the time before the pitch's halfway point** ([WIN Reality](https://winreality.com/blog/what-is-pitch-recognition-a-hitters-guide/)). It even admits a regular-practice group improved about as much on a video recognition test ([WIN Reality](https://winreality.com/?p=185404)). That candour is itself a competitive position. ## Coaching literature turns defenseman reads into scoreable rules

### Gap control means matching up-ice speed and arriving at one stick length

Coaches define gap as the vertical and lateral space between the defenseman and the attacker. Bertagna calls it the foundation of the position and warns that the defender must judge the attacker's "VMG," the speed actually made up the ice, because a weaving forward looks faster than he is; the lateral rule is to keep the defender's outside shoulder in line with the forward's inside shoulder and widen as the forward widens ([Bertagna, *The Hockey Coaching Bible*](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/gap-control)). The only concrete number found is the **3-2-1 rule: about three stick lengths at the far blue line, two at the red line, and one stick length or contact at the defender's own blue line**, from a 2010 coaching blog ([HockeyShare](https://www.hockeyshare.com/blog/gap-control-basics)). Gap should depend on handedness: against a strong-side forward carrying the puck on the boards side, leave a wider gap because he must expose the puck to cut middle; against an off-wing forward carrying it inside, play tighter ([Trimble 2023](https://andrewtrimble.substack.com/p/some-gap-control-basics)).

Coaches disagree on aggression, and a scorer has to take a side. Hockey Canada says "Don't attack the rush; receive it" and "stay up, don't step up," and tells defenders to "look through the puck carrier" to find the next attacker and their own support ([Hockey Canada, *Developing Skilled Defencemen*](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)). Santagata attacks the habit of taking three steps back and handing forwards three to four stick lengths, urging defenders to "CLOSE SPACE" with their eyes on the forward's chest, which moves less than feet or stick ([Santagata](https://lbxhockey.substack.com/p/how-to-play-a-1-v-1-defensemen)). Analytics justify making the blue line the centre of the program: controlled entries yield about **0.66 unblocked shot attempts against 0.29** for dump-ins ([Hockey-Graphs](https://hockey-graphs.com/2017/08/10/measuring-the-importance-of-individual-player-zone-entry-creation/)), and entry denial is "the most repeatable stat of zone entry defense" ([All Three Zones](https://allthreezones.substack.com/p/the-defenseman-compass)). The handedness rule applies directly to Jon (inference): playing left defense, the attacker coming down his side is usually the opposing right wing, so a right-shot winger there carries the puck on the boards side and calls for a wider gap with a poke chance on the cut, while a left-shot winger carries it inside and calls for a tighter gap. The scorer can reconcile the coaching split by rewarding speed-matched closing that ends at one stick length by the blue line and penalising both the early lunge and the three-steps-back retreat.

### One-handed angling works when the reach stays hidden and the strike comes late

Angling forces the carrier where the defender wants him, normally outside and into the boards, with the stick taking the middle lane; Hockey Canada's cues are "stick on puck, body on body; shoulders square, move feet, no lunging" ([Hockey Canada](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)). One-handed defending is an accepted NHL style, especially among smaller defensemen. Josh Gorges said two hands limit mobility and that holding a long stick one-handed in tight lets him "hide it... then you have that extra element of surprise"; Duncan Keith entered one-handed, "stick on puck," and switched to two hands once the puck was loose; and the Sharks taught two hands in battles because "you weren't as strong" one-handed ([The Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/sports/hockey/should-defencemen-keep-two-hands-on-the-stick/article4182331)). Jared Spurgeon, listed at 5'9", keeps his stick hidden on the inside so attackers cannot judge his reach, Lidstrom held it tight by the hip and struck "like a cobra," and Makar shows a small fake poke and strikes the moment the attacker looks down at the puck ([Revak](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/poke-check)). The mistakes to watch are swinging the stick "like a sword" and overreaching until the upper body loses balance ([Santagata](https://lbxhockey.substack.com/p/how-to-play-a-1-v-1-defensemen)).

Jon's style therefore has role models (Gorges, Spurgeon) and three sourced risks: showing the reach early, lunging, and weakness in battles. A fourth risk is an unsourced inference worth testing in VR: a left shot holding the stick in his bottom (left) hand has long reach to his forehand side but short reach across his body, so an attacker who cuts inside to his right, or who attacks down the right side when he plays right defense, tests his weak side.

### On a 2-on-1, the stick owns the pass lane

The traditional rule is that the goalie takes the shooter and the defender takes the pass. The defender stays between the attackers, keeps the stick in the passing lane, angles closer to the carrier as he gets deeper, does not drift to the weak-side attacker, does not commit early (especially against criss-crosses), and never turns his back on a delay ([HockeyShare, "2 on 1 Dissected"](https://www.hockeyshare.com/blog/2-on-1-dissected-part-2)). Hockey Canada lists both "play non puck carrier - take away pass" and "play puck carrier - with pressure" ([Skills of Gold checklist](https://www.hockeyeasternontario.ca/media/ncbaxqjk/hc_defensive_tactics_checklist.pdf)), and goalie coaches note that teams split on it and must agree a system with their goalie ([Wilson & van Vliet](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/defend-against-common-plays-off-the-opposition-rush)). For 2-on-2 and 3-on-2 reads, readable primary sources offer only headings: build from 1-on-1 up to 3-on-3, "step up on delays into the zone," and decide whether to hold or switch on crossing forwards ([Hockey Canada](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)). For a one-handed left shot, the stick-in-lane posture is natural when the receiver is to his left and awkward when the blade has to cross his body to the right, so both sides need reps.

### A shoulder check only counts if it changes the answer

Hockey Canada's retrieval cues are compact: "shoulder check, angle, and deception. Explode to the puck to give yourself time," "know what you are going to do before you touch the puck," and "don't give your problem to someone else, eat it or get it out," with a breakout vocabulary of quick up, wheel, overs, reverse, rim, set and support ([Hockey Canada](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)). USA Hockey's Ken Martel calls scanning the new name for shoulder checks, "more about when and where they look," and its staff warn that traditional shoulder-check drills fail when the player "doesn't really have to observe any conflict on the play." Up to nine other skaters can sit as far as 180 degrees outside a player's view, players touch the puck **less than about 2% of a game**, and scanning also reduces unseen hits ([USA Hockey ADM, "Scan Like Messi"](https://admkids.com/news_article/show/1312331)). Ian Cole supplies an if-then cue a simulator can encode, "If their 'F3' goes and stands in front of our winger, we're going to rim it right to somebody," and warns that opponents read repeated patterns, so the right answer must vary ([Chicago Sun-Times 2026](https://chicago.suntimes.com/blackhawks/2026/10/22/blackhawks-breakouts-nhl-science-ian-cole-1000-games)). No source gave a target number of shoulder checks.

### In the defensive zone, stay D-side, swivel, and stop instead of curling

Hockey Canada's zone cues cover the essentials: D-side position, stick on the ice, "body protect path to net, stick in passing lane," box out and face the puck at the net front, "let goalie see puck, no double screens," and "head on swivel" ([Hockey Canada](https://cdn.hockeycanada.ca/hockey-canada/Hockey-Programs/Players/Downloads/2018/2018-19-developing-hockey-defencemen-e.pdf)). Revak names **puck-watching and curling away from the check** instead of stopping and starting as the two most common defensive errors ([Revak](https://hockeysarsenal.substack.com/p/two-most-common-defensive-errors)). No data-backed list of recreational defensive mistakes exists; the beer-league advice found came from blogs and podcasts, so the recreational-error targets in the modules below come from the general coaching sources.

## Believable opponents need honest kinematics, and fair scores need a value model

### Elite kinematics are measured; recreational ones must be tuned

NHL tracking pins elite speeds: the median defenseman's top speed sits just under 22 mph (about 9.8 m/s), forwards burst at about twice the defensemen's rate, and the record is 24.6 mph (11 m/s) ([Puck Over The Glass](https://puckovertheglass.substack.com/p/nhl-edge-skating-speed-part-1)). Elite players reached 7.07 m/s at 6.1 m with average acceleration of **4.37 m/s²** ([Greer & Dillman](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/1412/1170)), and recreational players accelerate more slowly with a different muscle strategy ([Buckeridge et al.](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201709007467)). A published rink-control model supplies agent parameters: reaction time 0.189 s, defenders reacting to a pass 0.1 s later, a velocity response constant γ = 1.3 s⁻¹, pick-up reach of 6.5 ft, and soft, medium and hard passes of 45, 65 and 85 ft/s (13.7, 19.8 and 25.9 m/s) ([arXiv 2205.13678](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.13678)). Slap shots measured 29.1 m/s for elite players and 26.5 m/s for recreational players ([Woo et al.](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/1368)). No study has measured adult recreational skating, so opponents for Jon's level should start at roughly 70-85% of elite speeds with lower acceleration and be tuned by playtesting (inference). Skaters at constant top speed will look wrong, because real skating mixes glide, short bursts and pivots.

### Kinematic truth matters more than photorealism

Experts read movement, not surfaces. Handball goalkeepers facing VR throws rendered at levels from textured figures down to point-light displays showed no significant difference in response time or stop success, though they changed motor strategy, and the smallest-ball condition had the lowest stop rate ([Vignais et al. 2009](https://jssm.org/08-4-501.p_d_f)). World-class badminton players kept their information pick-up when strokes were reduced to point-light displays ([Abernethy, Zawi & Jackson 2008](https://repository.hku.hk/handle/10722/115071)). Deception is where kinematics decide the outcome: against VR rugby side-steps, experts read the honest centre-of-mass motion and waited longer before moving, while novices were fooled by upper-trunk yaw and foot placement ([Brault et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3372470/)). Harris and colleagues argue that psychological fidelity decides transfer and propose a construct-validity test: the simulator should separate experts from novices ([Harris et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00605/pdf)). For the animation system, this means the honest cue channels (centre-of-mass path, skate edges, blade angle at release) must be able to diverge from the dishonest ones (head look, shoulder fake, stick fake), or Jon can never learn which to trust, and the puck should stay visually salient.

### Scripted plays plus a reactive rule layer beat learned models for now

Learned "ghosting" models exist for soccer, where imitation learning on a season of tracking data produced league-average virtual defenders ([Disney Research](https://studios.disneyresearch.com/2017/03/03/data-driven-ghosting-using-deep-imitation-learning/)), but no public hockey equivalent exists, NHL frame-level tracking is not public, and deep trajectory models struggle to beat simple linear extrapolation ([Victor et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.00173)). The practical architecture is a hybrid (inference): plays authored as timed waypoint and event scripts, a per-agent rule layer that reacts by time-to-intercept (gap up, pressure the carrier, collapse to the slot) within the kinematic limits above, and learned policies only later. Scenarios belong in data, not baked video, so every position, speed, handedness and deception flag can vary. 360° video, the route STRIVR took for football, cannot be edited after capture and pins the viewer in place ([Jia et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)), which a skating sport cannot accept.

### Variability, adaptive difficulty and replay feedback drive learning

Random practice beat blocked practice on seven-day retention and on field decision time in tennis anticipation training, and "smart-random" sequences structured like real rallies beat pure randomness ([Broadbent thesis](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4590/); [Broadbent et al.](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/2060/)). For adaptive difficulty, Math Garden's Elo-style system updates learner ability and item difficulty after every response and serves items at about **75% expected success**, blending in response time ([Klinkenberg et al. 2011](https://dare.uva.nl/id/67ab8977-5e36-4f9f-962d-be294f2b8c09)). The standard form is P(correct) = 1 / (1 + e^-(θ − β)), with the player's θ rising and the scenario's β falling by K × (score − P) after each trial; the Math Garden equations themselves were not verified, so this is the textbook version. The difficulty knobs for hockey scenarios are time to decision, the number and closeness of options, the deception rate, visual load, and how far behind the player the decisive information sits. Feedback frequency made no difference to novices' learning in a video handball anticipation study, with gains retained over seven months ([Schorer et al. 2010](https://www.ijsp-online.com/abstract/view/41/107)), so effort belongs in feedback content. STRIVR has athletes re-watch the rep and turn their heads to find the receiver they missed ([ABC7](https://abc7news.com/post/virtual-reality-technology-helping-football-players-master-the-game/1182990/)), and reviewers recommend showing high-level athletes alternative solutions ([Jia et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)).

### Score the choice against its expected value, not the outcome

The most defensible score combines choice quality against a model value of each option, response time from the moment the decisive cue appears, information-directed scanning, and consistency across variants. A pass-evaluation model built on Olympic tracking data values each option as completion probability times location value, finds the best option over all angles and speeds (including not passing), and reports the chosen pass as a "relative outcome" against the best ([arXiv 2205.13678](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.13678)). Because Jon's engine knows every agent's exact state and limits, it can compute those probabilities from simulated time-to-intercept instead of estimating them from broadcast data. Options within about 10% of the best value should count as correct (inference), with coach ratings overriding the model where it is weak, such as board battles and puck protection, and entry decisions can lean on the 0.66-versus-0.29 shots-per-entry gap. Scoring the outcome alone is a mistake, because a good choice can fail on execution; the outcome should be shown separately.

Scanning scores need humility. Head turns in one soccer study linked to faster and more forward passes but not to pass success ([McGuckian et al.](https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/handle/11250/2595844?show=full)), and the scanning effects in both hockey and soccer are small (β ≈ 0.12-0.13), so scanning should guide coaching rather than decide pass or fail. Without eye tracking, the proxy is head-yaw excursions of roughly 30-45° away from the puck in the 5-10 s before reception (inference), with credit for whether the scan covered the sector holding the best option. Validity problems are common in this field: higher-level players were faster but not more accurate in one VR test, and perceptual-cognitive test scores have failed to predict on-field performance ([Jia et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284098/)). Assessment scenarios must also be kept out of training, a flaw several VR studies committed.

## Build six modules in this order, on one shared engine

All six modules share one foundation. Scenarios are stored as data: initial positions and velocities, scripted intents, reactive rules, decision points, candidate options with computed values, deception flags and variability ranges. Skaters are driven by mocap-style animation with separable honest and faked cues, a time-to-intercept value model scores every option, each scenario family carries an Elo rating, and every rep can be replayed from Jon's own head pose, from above with the best option drawn in, and from the attacker's eyes. Locomotion should stay minimal: a standing, stick-in-hand mode with short glides or decision-point freezes keeps perception and action coupled while limiting cybersickness, which one low-quality study found more common standing than seated ([Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/126013021.pdf)). Following the meta-analytic dose, sessions should run 10-25 minutes, two to four times a week, for six weeks or more, in 8-10 minute blocks; each module starts with a short blocked introduction and then joins a smart-random rotation.

| Priority | Module | Core read | Why this slot |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scan, retrieve, first pass | Read forecheck pressure and support behind you before the touch | Strongest hockey scanning evidence, independent of position; minimal locomotion; fuses the physics stick with the decision |
| 2 | Read the carrier | Predict pass, shot or cut from honest cues under occlusion | Best field-transfer evidence (occlusion d = 0.85); builds the attacker cue rig that modules 3 and 4 need |
| 3 | Gap and angle 1-on-1 | Match up-ice speed, arrive at one stick length, angle and poke on the cue | Jon's signature skill; the blue line is where a defenseman's repeatable value shows |
| 4 | 2-on-1 lane hold | Own the pass lane without committing early | Classic rush read with clear coaching rules; tests the backhand side |
| 5 | Net-front swivel | Stay D-side, check man and puck, keep the goalie's sightline clear | Targets puck-watching and curling, the most-cited errors |
| 6 | 2-on-2 and 3-on-2 reads | Count the numbers, hold or switch on crossers, step up on delays | Valuable, but coaching sources are thin and it needs a reliable AI partner |
| Always | Benchmark and transfer check | Held-out scenarios plus game video | The only way to know whether any of it reaches the ice |

### Module 1: scan, retrieve, first pass

| Spec | Design |
|---|---|
| Scenario | A puck is dumped or rimmed into Jon's corner or behind his net at varied speeds and angles. Forecheckers (F1, later F2) and his support (D partner, strong-side winger on the wall, center, weak-side winger) spawn in randomized layouts mostly behind him. The layout decides which of quick up, wheel, D-to-D, reverse, weak-side rim, wall bump or "eat it" is best, with if-then cues such as "F3 stands in front of our winger, so rim it" |
| Player action | Glides or steps to the puck, checks over one or both shoulders, then executes with the physics stick: pass, rim or carry |
| Measured | Scans (head-yaw excursions away from the puck, including brief ones, since hockey scans are often a single fixation of about 135 ms), their timing before the touch, and whether they covered the sector holding the decisive cue; latency from touch to release; chosen option's value against the best; pass execution; time spent head-down; whether the forechecker arrives before the release |
| Progression | Elo per scenario family at about 75% success: less free time before F1 arrives, F2 added, decisive information moved further behind, forechecker shoulder fakes, options closer in value, then chained into neutral-zone regroups |

This module goes first because it has the strongest hockey-specific evidence (scanning before reception predicts success regardless of position, per [Berg et al. 2025](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4092070?lng=en)), it needs almost no locomotion, and it fuses Jon's existing physics stick with the decision in one rep, which neither Sense Arena's controller puck nor its separate DanglePro module does. Sense Arena already ships a "Shoulder Check" drill; the difference here is USA Hockey's rule that the check must matter ([USA Hockey ADM](https://admkids.com/news_article/show/1312331)), so the information behind Jon changes every rep and decides the answer. For a 5'8" defenseman who will lose many strength battles, "explode to the puck" plus a scan that tells him whether to eat it or get it out also reduces hits taken.

### Module 2: read the carrier

| Spec | Design |
|---|---|
| Scenario | One or two attackers come through the neutral zone at Jon on his blue line. At a variable moment before the critical action (shot or pass, inside cut or wide drive, which side the pass goes) the scene blanks or freezes. Attackers show honest cues (centre-of-mass path, edges, blade angle) and, on a schedule, faked head, shoulder and stick cues |
| Player action | Within a deadline, puts the stick blade into the predicted lane and turns his shoulders toward the predicted path |
| Measured | Accuracy and latency from cue onset at each occlusion point; accuracy on deceptive versus honest trials; the earliest occlusion point at which he reads reliably above chance |
| Feedback | A non-occluded replay after every rep, plus the attacker's-eye view of the lane Jon's stick left open |
| Progression | Earlier occlusion, higher deception rate, randomized handedness, speeds rising toward the elite range, more attackers |

Occlusion training with replay feedback has the best field-transfer evidence of any perceptual paradigm ([Müller et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11467115)), and this module is cheap because it needs only the attacker rig and cue channels that modules 3 and 4 need anyway. The attacker's-eye replay is a defenseman's version of the Fribourg puck's-eye feedback (inference): information that real practice cannot give.

### Module 3: gap and angle 1-on-1

| Spec | Design |
|---|---|
| Scenario | As in the Michigan Tech drill, Jon first makes a breakout pass with the physics stick; the receiving forward turns and attacks down Jon's wall from the far blue line. Speed, VMG (weave versus straight), handedness (strong side versus off-wing) and route (wide drive, inside cut, delay) are randomized, and a "whistle" can switch lanes to force a re-gap |
| Player action | Controls backward speed and pivot timing (input mapping to be playtested for comfort), keeps his outside shoulder on the attacker's inside shoulder, steers him to the boards, and pokes one-handed in the cue window or switches to two hands when the puck is loose or pinned |
| Measured | Gap at the far blue line, red line and own blue line against 3-2-1 bands calibrated to Jon's own stick and one-handed reach; speed match against the attacker's up-ice component; lateral alignment error; how long the stick is visibly extended before the strike; poke timing relative to the cue window (attacker's eyes down, puck off the hip); a lunge flag when the head follows the stick past balance; outcome (entry denied, dumped, offside or steered outside the dots versus a controlled entry) and shot location |
| Progression | Body-only reps first, adding the stick later as in the Horseshoe gap progression; forehand wall first, then the mirrored wall and inside cuts to his backhand; strong-side then off-wing attackers; faster and weaving attackers; delays that demand a step-up; two-hand prompts on loose pucks |

This is Jon's signature module and the place where entry denial, a defenseman's most repeatable contribution, is won ([All Three Zones](https://allthreezones.substack.com/p/the-defenseman-compass)). The drill pattern comes from the Michigan Tech 1-on-1 ([Weiss Tech Hockey](https://weisstechhockey.com/michigan-tech-1-on-1-drill/)) and the stick-later progression from the Horseshoe drill ([IceHockeySystems](https://icehockeysystems.com/node/5711771)). It ranks third only because it is the heaviest build: believable skating animation, a gap input that does not make Jon sick, and a physics stick that can be held in one hand when he releases the top-hand grip. It also turns the one-handed debate into data, because hidden-reach time, lunges and inside-cut losses will show whether his style needs a two-hand switch.

### Module 4: 2-on-1 lane hold

| Spec | Design |
|---|---|
| Scenario | Two attackers and an AI goalie, entering from both sides so the pass lane is sometimes on Jon's forehand and sometimes across his body; variants add criss-crosses, delays, a backdoor runner and look-off fakes; a toggle selects the system, "D takes the pass" or "D pressures the carrier" |
| Player action | Holds the middle, keeps the blade in the lane, closes on the carrier as he passes the hash marks, and holds off any slide |
| Measured | Share of time the blade covers the pass line; position between the attackers; commit time relative to the shot or pass; shot origin (outside the dots versus the slot); passes completed; lateral movement demanded of the goalie |
| Progression | Criss-crosses and delays first, then deception, then a trailer that turns it into a 3-on-2 and feeds module 6 |

The scoring rules come straight from the 2-on-1 coaching points ([HockeyShare](https://www.hockeyshare.com/blog/2-on-1-dissected-part-2)), and the system toggle reflects Hockey Canada's two listed options, so Jon can rehearse whichever one his own goalie expects.

### Module 5: net-front swivel

| Spec | Design |
|---|---|
| Scenario | Cycle play in the corner and behind the net with a net-front attacker, point shots and backdoor threats |
| Player action | Holds D-side position, keeps the stick on the ice in the slot lane, boxes out and fronts the puck without screening his goalie |
| Measured | D-side position error; stick-in-lane share; swivel frequency between puck and man and the longest puck-only stare; a puck-to-goalie line-of-sight check for double screens; curling paths flagged against stop-and-start routes |
| Progression | Faster cycles, more attackers, switches behind the net, weak-side duties |

### Module 6: 2-on-2 and 3-on-2 reads

This module adds an AI partner and a backchecker and asks Jon to count the numbers, hold or switch on crossing forwards, and step up on delays. It ranks last because readable coaching sources give only headings for these assignments, and a partner that behaves inconsistently would teach the wrong reads; it should wait until modules 3 and 4 have validated the attacker and goalie behaviour.

### The benchmark and transfer check runs from day one

Every two weeks or so, a short block of held-out scenarios, never used in training, should be served at about 50% expected success to track progress without test learning. Before trusting any module, a construct-validity check is worth running: a few more experienced players should outscore less experienced ones on it, or the module needs fixing ([Harris et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00605/pdf)). The real proof is on the ice. A helmet or rink camera in Jon's league games lets him count shoulder checks before retrievals, entries denied versus allowed on his side, and retrieval outcomes before and after a training block (inference); that is the kind of untrained, game-based outcome the research literature mostly lacks.

### What to avoid

Skip generic cognitive drills (multiple-object tracking, color-calling, Flanker-style tasks) as core content: they improve themselves and little else, and a VR warm-up even produced a small dual-task decline. Avoid plain repetition, since the no-feedback group in the Fribourg study and the plain-VR batting group in Gray's study did not improve on the outcomes that mattered; every module needs adaptive variability and feedback that reveals the right answer. Do not answer decisions with buttons or menus when the stick and body can respond. Do not script shoulder checks that cannot change the answer, block a module into long runs of identical reps, or let one default (always rim, always take the pass) be correct most of the time. Do not score outcomes alone, collapse everything into one opaque IQ number, or publish percentage claims without methods, which is the trust gap Sense Arena already has. Do not treat scan counts as pass or fail, given their small link to success. Do not spend on photorealism before kinematics, run opponents at constant speed, or shrink the puck. Do not rely on heavy artificial locomotion, or on fine interceptive timing such as one-timers and stickhandling feel as the measure of hockey sense, because VR's depth and haptic limits hit those hardest. Never assess on scenarios used in training.

## Conclusion

The evidence changes what "beyond Sense Arena" should mean. The market leader competes on licensing, content volume and a single score, while the research says transfer comes from how practice is built: representative situations, answers given with the stick and body, information the player has to go and find, difficulty that adapts, and feedback that reveals what he could not see. VR's most defensible advantage is that last item. A puck's-eye or attacker's-eye replay shows a player something no amount of real ice time can, and the Fribourg study is the cleanest demonstration that this, not repetition, drives the gain. Jon's sandbox already holds the hardest asset to fake, a physics stick that executes the decision in the same rep, and the defenseman is the least-served position in both the research and the products. A trainer built on these principles would therefore stand out on evidence and content rather than on graphics.

Honesty about evidence can also be a product feature. Nobody has shown that any hockey VR trainer improves on-ice play, and scanning's link to success is real but small, so the right promise is narrower and testable: better reads in the headset, about half of that on the ice, measured on held-out scenarios and on game video. For one recreational defenseman the check costs little, a camera plus counts of shoulder checks and entries denied over six weeks. It would also make his one-handed angling style an experiment with data rather than a habit.
